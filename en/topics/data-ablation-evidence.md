## Quantitative impact of data strategy ablations (distinguishing: filtering strictness ablation / caption density-style ablation / data mixture ablation, and corresponding evaluation metrics)

`data_ablation` · Detail level: detailed

### [Allegro](../models/Allegro.md)

Quantitative data strategy ablations are essentially absent, which is the main shortcoming in this paper's level of disclosure:
· Filtering strictness ablation: none. The paper provides 4 tiers of threshold configuration, but does not run a controlled experiment on "how relaxing/tightening the threshold affects the final metrics," nor does it report the VBench change from removing any one filter level.
· Caption density/style ablation: none. There is no comparison of short caption vs. long dense caption, or Tag2Text caption vs. Aria caption, on training effectiveness.
· Data mixture ablation: none.
· The related controls that were done are all qualitative visualizations: T5 vs. mT5 text encoder comparison (Fig. 7–8, qualitative examples), stage-by-stage visualization of the effect of multi-stage progressive training (Fig. 9, no isolated metrics).
· End-to-end evaluation: quantitative VBench evaluation (946 prompts / 4,730 videos) and a user study (46 prompts, 6 dimensions, 5,448 ratings, 2 annotators per pair), concluding that it surpasses all contemporaneous open-source models and most commercial models, second only to Hailuo (MiniMax) and Kling.
Hence, the effectiveness of its data design is mainly endorsed indirectly through "final model ranking," lacking quantitative evidence attributable to individual data decisions.

### [Apollo](../models/Apollo.md) ⚠️

The paper's ablation experiments focus on **architecture and training strategy**, with **no ablation targeting data strategy at all** — this is a key limitation when assessing the effectiveness of Apollo's data methodology:
[Existing ablation 1 — architecture (Table 2)] "Comparison of different methods. The Dual Tower uses standard cross-attention, while the Single Tower utilizes our proposed Omni-Full Attention." Compares dual-tower cross-attention with single-tower Omni-Full Attention, supporting the single-tower scheme.
[Existing ablation 2 — multi-task masking (Table 3)] "Ablation of multi-task masking," three progressive tiers:
  - T2V only: Video ID 0.71, other metrics not applicable
  - T2V + T2AV: Video ID 0.76, Audio MOS 88.181, CLAP 0.188, WER 0.044, DeSync 0.895, Sync-conf 5.024, IB 0.201
  - All tasks (Ours): Video ID 0.80, Audio MOS 93.106, CLAP 0.232, WER 0.028, DeSync 0.650, Sync-conf 6.787, IB 0.316
  Conclusion: the more complete the task set, the more consistently all metrics improve — video identity consistency +0.09, audio MOS +4.9, CLAP +0.044, WER relative drop 36%, DeSync relative drop 27%, lip-sync confidence +1.76, cross-modal alignment +0.115. The paper emphasizes that "for T2AV joint generation, our multi-task model significantly outperforms a counterpart trained solely on T2AV," i.e., multi-task joint training provides a positive gain for joint generation itself, refuting the intuition that "multi-tasking dilutes single-task ability."
[Existing ablation 3 — training stages (Figure 5)] "Ablations of different training stages. Metrics include video, audio, TTS, and audio-video consistency," showing progressive improvement across the three-stage curriculum. Because the difference between Stage II/III is essentially a difference in data mixture and data quality, this ablation **indirectly** constitutes evidence for the data strategy — it demonstrates that the data path of "metric-driven rebalancing + manual curation and refinement" is effective. However, since the figure does not label the mixture numbers and does not isolate variables (progressing through stages also stacks on more training steps), the gain cannot be attributed to the data itself.
[Completely missing] Filtering strictness ablation (e.g., a comparison of 27% retention rate vs. 50% vs. 80%, which could have been Apollo's most valuable contribution), caption density/style ablation (dense vs. short caption), data mixture ablation (different ratios of the four audio scene categories), data scale ablation (81M vs. smaller subsets) — none of these exist. The paper presents the "27% retention rate" as a factual statement rather than a verifiable design choice. [uncertain]

### [CineDance / CineDance-1M](../models/CineDance.md) ⚠️

The paper's ablations focus on "method selection within the annotation pipeline" rather than the traditional sense of "the impact of data strategy on generation quality" — a distinction that needs to be made explicit:
[Pipeline ablations that were done]
  · Tab. 4 (narrative parsing strategy ablation): compares "having the LLM directly output timestamps" vs. "bottom-up shot-index grouping," and cross-compares different sizes of the Qwen backbone. The best combination, Qwen3.5-27B + bottom-up strategy, achieves F1 = 88.4%, with only 3.1% of sequences shorter than the 20-second soft threshold (a markedly lower fragmentation rate than the control groups).
  · Tab. 5 (speaker diarization ablation): on a 100-clip benchmark, Qwen3-Omni-30B + sliding-window prompting reaches 83.1%, versus only 56.4% for whole-clip input.
  · Tab. 7 (ASR-to-character binding ablation): the windowed scheme raises Qwen3-Omni from 67.2% to 95.4%; compared with the Gemini series at 82.8%–87.4%, DiariZen at 63.1%, and Pyannote-3.1 at 62.7%.
  · Artifact audit comparison: CineDance-1M's non-compliance rate of 2.8% vs. Koala-36M's 37.4% (a 13.4× improvement), which can be viewed as qualitative evidence for "filtering strictness."
[Data ablations not done] The paper does not report the quantitative impact of "removing a filter level / changing the shot-grouping threshold / adjusting caption density or style / changing data mixture" on downstream generation quality (CineBench dimension scores). That is: filtering strictness ablation, caption density/style ablation, and data mixture ablation — all three classic data ablation types — are absent.
[Judgment] This work's empirical focus is on "annotation quality itself being measurable and optimizable," rather than "how data strategy changes generative model performance" — the latter being a clear evidence gap. [uncertain]

### [CogVideoX](../models/CogVideoX.md) ⚠️

The paper's ablation experiments focus primarily on model architecture and training techniques, with very few quantitative ablations on the data side — a clear shortfall relative to Movie Gen:
[Architecture/training ablations (not data)] 3D RoPE vs. sinusoidal absolute position encoding (RoPE loss converges noticeably faster); Expert AdaLN vs. MMDiT vs. no Expert AdaLN (by FVD, CLIP4Clip Score, and loss, Expert AdaLN is better); 3D full attention vs. 2D+1D separated attention; Explicit Uniform Sampling (the loss curve is markedly more stable and converges faster); RoPE extrapolation vs. interpolation (extrapolation preserves local detail, interpolation produces blurry large images).
[Qualitative conclusions directly related to data]
· Caption density ablation: the paper claims that "our innovative video captioning model significantly improves generation quality and semantic alignment," and in Appendix H shows a comparison of Panda-70M short captions with CogVLM2-Caption long captions to illustrate the density difference, but does not give an A/B net-win-rate or a metric delta [quantitative result uncertain].
· High-quality fine-tuning ablation: explicitly reports a two-sided result — removing subtitle watermarks yields a small visual quality improvement, but semantic ability degrades slightly. This is a qualitative but highly valuable conclusion regarding the "filtering strictness" trade-off.
· Mixed-duration training / Frame Pack: the motivation argued is that training with a fixed number of frames causes the model to split into two generation modes by token count with poor generalization, but no comparison numbers are given [quantitative result uncertain].
· Data mixture ablation: not done [uncertain].

### [Cosmos-Predict2.5](../models/Cosmos-Predict25.md)

The paper does not perform a strict "controlled-variable data ablation" (there is no controlled experiment of retraining under the same configuration with a swapped dataset), but it provides three types of quantifiable data-strategy evidence:
[① Filtering strictness: cross-generation comparison rather than same-generation ablation] Relative to Cosmos-Predict1, Cosmos-Predict2.5 compresses the clip survival rate from 30% down to 4%, while the raw footage pool expands from 20M hours to 35M hours. The overall benefit shows up on PAI-Bench: the Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B pretrained model scores T2W Overall 0.751, I2W Overall 0.799, rising to 0.768 / 0.810 after post-training; the 14B pretrained model scores 0.757 / 0.806, reaching 0.768 / 0.810 after post-training. But because the architecture (flow matching replacing EDM) and text encoder (Cosmos-Reason1 replacing T5) changed at the same time, the gain cannot be attributed to data filtering alone — this is the main limitation of this evidence.
[② Data mixture strategy: human win rate for domain SFT] Human preference win rate (SFT Win / Base Win / Tie) of five-domain SFT relative to the pretraining baseline: robot manipulation 72.6% / 8.3% / 19.0%; object permanence 50.9% / 27.7% / 21.4%; driving 47.9% / 28.8% / 23.3%; high motion 44.0% / 34.7% / 21.3%; complex scenes 42.6% / 35.4% / 22.0%. The discernible pattern is: the narrower the domain and the greater its distributional gap from the general pretraining distribution (robot manipulation), the higher the marginal benefit of domain data.
[③ Verification of model merging as a substitute for mixture ratio] Comparing Model Soup, TIES, DARE-TIES against the base model on a win-rate radar chart across six dimensions (five specialized domains + General), the conclusion is that the merging methods (except DARE-Linear) perform comparably, and the merged model achieves per-domain advantages while retaining general-domain performance ("gets the best of all the worlds"). This indirectly validates the viability of the route of "not adjusting the mixture ratio, but instead using parameter merging."
[④ Rigorous controlled comparison for synthetic data augmentation (downstream task)] The robot policy-learning experiment is the only strictly controlled-variable comparison in the entire paper: same task, the same 10 test scenarios with 3 trials each — the base policy (only 100 real demonstration segments) scores 1/30, traditional image-augmentation baseline scores 5/30, and Cosmos-Transfer2.5 generative augmentation scores 24/30. This is the strongest quantitative evidence at the "data augmentation strategy" level.
[⑤ RL gains on the data/reward side] VideoAlign reward before and after RL: 2B pretrained model T2W total score 1.08→1.69, I2W 0.23→0.42; merged model T2W 1.23→1.74, I2W 0.24→0.45; human-vote RL win rate 40.0% (pretrained+RL) and 46.7% (merged+RL), both better than pre-RL.
[Not done] Ablation of caption density/length (short vs. medium vs. long), ablation of the 5-second window segmentation granularity, ablation of each filter's individual on/off switch, ablation of the 26-category taxonomy mixture — these are clear gaps in this report's data ablations.

### [Data-Juicer 2.0](../models/Data-Juicer.md) ⚠️

The entire methodology of Data-Juicer Sandbox is organized around data ablation — it upgrades data ablation from "a validation experiment in the paper's final section" to "a search mechanism running throughout the entire data-recipe design process." This is its most distinctive contribution to this survey.
[Ablation type 1: filtering strictness ablation (DJ's main battleground)] In the Probe stage, for each candidate operator, the dataset is divided by statistic into three equal-sized pools — P_i,low / P_i,middle / P_i,high — plus a randomly sampled control pool; a reference model is trained on each pool with the same budget and evaluated with VBench (16 metrics). This is equivalent to running a complete ablation of "which segment of the distribution is most beneficial to retain" for each filtering dimension, and it achieves strict variable control (equal pool sizes, identical training budgets). The candidate operators on the video side are video_aesthetics_filter, video_nsfw_filter, and video_frames_text_similarity_filter; ultimately NSFW filtering and frame-text similarity filtering win out and enter the final recipe, while the aesthetics score filter is not selected.
[Ablation type 2: data mixture/combination ablation] In the Refine stage, the winning operators are cross-combined into 2^n−1 data pools (a pyramid structure), each verified in turn for combination effect — i.e., an exhaustive ablation of multi-operator mixtures.
[Ablation type 3: the three-way ablation of data quantity vs. quality vs. compute — the most valuable quantitative conclusion] The paper compares three scale-up routes and gives a clear ranking: repeating the highest-quality data 6× yields performance higher than using 8× the compute with suboptimal data. This is a joint ablation spanning data quality, data repetition count, and compute budget, and points directly to the conclusion that "in the text-to-video setting, improving data quality has a better cost-performance ratio than adding compute."
[Quantification of the final effect]
  · T2V-Turbo, trained on the DJ-refined data pool, tops VBench: Board Average 82.53%, Uniform Average 81.26%, surpassing the second-place Gen-3's 82.32% and the third-place VEnhancer's 81.97%.
  · Improvement over the T2V-Turbo baseline: Board Average +1.53%, Uniform Average +2.59%.
  · The HuggingFace dataset card additionally records that the 147k data pool brings a "1.09% improvement relative to T2V-Turbo."
  · The paper emphasizes this result was achieved "at a compute cost at least 22× lower" — the most direct evidence for "data optimization substituting for compute stacking."
  · A small-scale probe experiment on EasyAnimate likewise obtains a considerable improvement via the data recipe (the specific figures are not given in the accessible content).
[Cross-model transferability verification] The optimal recipe found at small scale (EasyAnimate) remains effective when directly transferred to the architecturally different T2V-Turbo (based on VideoCrafter-2.0), indicating that the benefit of the data recipe is transferable across architectures — this is the precondition for the Sandbox methodology to hold, and one of its most important experimental findings.
[Total experiments] The paper reports completing 100+ sets of experiments, covering three task types: image-text pretraining (CLIP), image-to-text (LLaVA-type), and text-to-video (DiT-type).
[Uncertain] The caption density/style dimension ablation is not disclosed (because the case study did not re-caption the data); the individual probe rankings and score details of the three candidate operators are not disclosed; whether other video operators such as the motion score participated in probing is not disclosed.

### [Foley-Omni](../models/Foley-Omni.md) ⚠️

The paper's ablation experiments (Table 6) focus on [training curriculum] and [model architecture], with no pure data-strategy ablation performed.
[Ablations done — quantitative results]
  Metric definitions: FD_VGG↓ (Fréchet Distance on VGGSound, audio fidelity), WER_GRID↓ (speech intelligibility on GRID), IB_V2ST↑ (audio-video semantic consistency on V2ST-Bench), WER_V2ST↓ (speech intelligibility on V2ST-Bench).
  · Full model: FD_VGG 1.57 / WER_GRID 15.3 / IB_V2ST 0.26 / WER_V2ST 7.59
  · Single-stage training (removing the three-stage curriculum): 1.73 / 27.4 / 0.24 / 29.29 — WER_V2ST worsens 3.9×, WER_GRID worsens 79%, all four metrics degrade across the board. This belongs to the training curriculum/data scheduling ablation and can be classified as a broad-sense data mixture ablation: it proves that "feeding data in stages" has a decisive advantage over "mixing everything at once," with speech tasks suffering the most damage.
  · Removing the Z_sync synchronization side-channel: 2.21 / 18.9 / 0.22 / 12.40 — IB drops from 0.26 to 0.22 (the most direct degradation in audio-video consistency), FD_VGG rises from 1.57 to 2.21 (audio fidelity worsens by 41%), WER_V2ST rises from 7.59 to 12.40. This proves the Synchformer temporal-feature pathway makes a substantive contribution across all three dimensions.
[Ablations not done — clear gaps] [uncertain]
  · Filtering strictness ablation: none of the six thresholds (480p / 1Mbps / motion[0.1,3.2] / AudioBox 0.6 / IB 0.3 / Sync 0.2) has ever undergone a threshold sensitivity analysis; there is no performance comparison curve for "loose filtering vs. strict filtering."
  · Caption density/style ablation: there is no comparison of the effect difference between three-field structured annotation vs. a single free-form description (e.g., LTX-2-style full soundscape description) — this would have been the most direct experiment for validating the value of its core contribution, the "three-field schema," and its absence is a pity.
  · Acoustic post-verification ablation: no comparison of "with/without Bandit energy gating" was done, so the actual benefit of correcting visual hallucination cannot be quantified, nor can the reasonableness of the −35 dB threshold be verified.
  · Data mixture ablation: no comparison of different task-group hour mixtures (e.g., raising the music proportion) was made.
  Overall, this paper's ablations of model architecture and training curriculum are thorough, but almost no ablation verification was done for one of its self-claimed core contributions — the "data cleaning pipeline" — whose effectiveness rests mainly on methodological narrative rather than experimental evidence.

### [Goku](../models/Goku.md) ⚠️

The paper's ablation experiments mainly revolve around **model and training strategy**, and **direct ablation of the data processing strategy is essentially absent** — this is its main shortcoming on the data dimension:
[Existing data-related ablations]
  (1) **Joint image-text training vs. pure video training (a data-mixture-type ablation)** — the paper's Figure 5(b) shows: a model that adds image data for joint image-video training generates results significantly better than a model trained only on video data. This is the only clear "data mixture ablation" in the entire paper, supporting Goku's core claim of joint modeling (high-quality images compensate for video image-quality shortfalls, stabilizing semantic learning).
  (2) **Model scale ablation** — comparing Goku-T2V 2B and 8B, finding that increasing model scale significantly mitigates distorted object structures. This is on the model side, not the data side.
  (3) **The effect of prompt rewriting (indirect evidence of caption style)** — after using GPT-4o to expand GenEval's short prompts into semantically preserving detailed descriptions, the GenEval score reaches 0.76. This indirectly proves that the model's performance depends heavily on long prompts consistent with the dense caption style used during training — empirical evidence that "caption density/style affects inference-time performance" — but the paper does not organize it as a formal caption ablation.
[Clearly missing] There is no ablation of filtering strictness (e.g., the effect of aesthetic threshold 4.3 vs. higher/lower on VBench), no controlled experiment on caption density and structure (short vs. long caption), and no ablation of the distribution-balancing stage (balanced vs. unbalanced effect on per-category metrics) — the absence of the third item is especially regrettable, because distribution balancing is Goku's most distinctive design, yet its benefit is never quantified. [partially uncertain]

### [Hailuo / MiniMax Video](../models/Hailuo.md) ⚠️

[Uncertain] (completely absent). MiniMax has not published any paper or technical report, so no data ablation experiment of any form exists — no filtering strictness ablation, no caption density/style ablation, no data mixture ablation, and no corresponding evaluation metric curves.
The only statement that comes close to "the impact of data" is the Hailuo 02 blog post juxtaposing "3× the parameter count + 4× the training data + improved quality and diversity" with the generational capability leap — but this is a product-iteration description with multiple variables changing simultaneously, from which the independent contribution of the data factor cannot be isolated; it does not constitute ablation evidence.

### [HunyuanVideo-Foley](../models/HunyuanVideo-Foley.md) ⚠️

[Conclusion: no data-side ablation at all] This is the most obvious empirical shortcoming of this work. The paper's ablation experiments (Tables 5-7) are entirely about model architecture and training strategy, none of them touching data:
[Ablations actually done (three groups, all architecture/training strategy)]
1) Architecture component ablation (on MovieGen-Audio-Bench):
 - Joint attention only: PQ 6.32 / IB 0.31 / DeSync 1.05 / CLAP 0.32;
 - Removing interleaved RoPE: PQ 6.36 / IB 0.31 / DeSync 0.79 / CLAP 0.30;
 - Removing unimodal DiT blocks: PQ 6.23 / IB 0.31 / DeSync 0.79 / CLAP 0.30;
 - Full architecture: PQ 6.38+ / IB 0.32+ / DeSync 0.78+ / CLAP 0.30+.
 The most notable finding is the contribution of interleaved RoPE to temporal synchronization — with joint attention only, DeSync is as high as 1.05; after adding interleaved RoPE it drops to 0.78~0.79, an improvement of about 25%, proving that positional-encoding-level audio-video temporal alignment is the main source of synchronization ability. The unimodal DiT blocks mainly contribute to audio quality (PQ drops from 6.38 to 6.23 when removed).
2) REPA encoder selection: ATST-Frame (PQ 6.28) > EAT (PQ 6.24) > EAT+ATST combination (PQ 6.00). The combination is actually the worst, indicating the supervisory targets of multiple self-supervised representations conflict with each other.
3) REPA application layer position: layer 8 (PQ 6.34 / DeSync 0.74) > layer 16 (6.32 / 0.78) > layer 12 (6.28 / 0.81). Shallow-layer guidance is better than deep-layer guidance.
4) DAC-VAE reconstruction quality comparison (table, not a strict ablation): the 128-dimensional DAC-VAE achieves PESQ 4.45 / SI-SDR 14.76 on AudioSet, significantly better than discrete DAC (4.17 / 11.08) and the Stable Audio VAE (2.33 / 3.32); STOI 0.99 on LibriTTS. This proves the advantage of continuous latents over RVQ discrete tokens in reconstruction fidelity.
[Completely missing list of data ablations] Each of the following is a design the paper claims to have made but never validated the value of:
- Is the 100,000-hour scale necessary? No scaling curve, no controlled training at different data volumes;
- What is the impact of the looseness/tightness of the 80% silence threshold? No control;
- Is the 32 kHz effective sample-rate floor necessary? This is the item most in need of ablation — it directly bears on the ability to generate high-frequency detail, and the paper explicitly cites it as one source of its audio quality advantage, yet there is no experimental support whatsoever;
- What is the contribution of AudioBox aesthetic filtering? No control;
- What is the contribution of ImageBind + AV-align alignment filtering? This is the most expensive step in the data pipeline, and its actual improvement to the IB and DeSync metrics is entirely unknown;
- What is the effect of category-distribution management (balanced representation)? A core mechanism the paper claims, zero verification;
- What is the gain from the high-quality tag strategy? Appendix A.1 qualitatively claims it "enhances high-frequency detail retention," but there is no A/B comparison data whatsoever. This is the cheapest and easiest ablation to run (simply run the same model with/without the tag at inference), yet it still was not done.
[Assessment] The paper lists the "scalable data pipeline" as the first of its three major contributions, yet its entire ablation budget was spent on architecture — this leaves every design choice on the data side (including its most distinctive bandwidth threshold and quality tags) stuck in a state of "reasonable but unverified." Readers cannot judge how much of the 100,000 hours of data was necessary, or which of the seven filtering levels actually did the work. [uncertain]

### [HunyuanVideo](../models/HunyuanVideo.md) ⚠️

Neither technical report provides a controlled ablation experiment for data strategy — there is no filtering strictness ablation (e.g., the effect of relaxing/tightening the aesthetic threshold on the metrics), no caption density/style ablation (e.g., comparing short caption vs. dense caption), and no data mixture ablation (e.g., comparing T2V:I2V:T2I = 1:6:3 against other ratios). This is the entry's biggest methodological shortcoming: the pipeline description is exhaustive, but the necessity of each step lacks quantitative evidentiary support.
Both generations provide end-to-end human comparative evaluations rather than ablations: the original version had 60 professional evaluators compare against five closed-source models including Gen-3 and Luma 1.6 on 1,533 prompts, coming out ahead overall on text alignment, motion quality, and visual quality; version 1.5 had 100+ professional evaluators compare against Wan2.2, Kling 2.1, Seedance Pro, and Veo3 on 300 text prompts and 300 images, using Rating and GSB methods. These results cannot be attributed to specific data strategies. [uncertain]

### [InstructAV2AV](../models/InstructAV2AV.md) ⚠️

The paper's ablation experiments focus entirely on model architecture and training strategy, with no data strategy ablation performed at all — this is the clearest gap in this work's experimental design, especially considering it lists "scalable data synthesis pipeline + InsAVE-80K" as two of its three main contributions.

[Ablations done (in table form, on the InsAVE-80K evaluation set)]
  Full-model baseline: FVD 180.38 / TV-A 25.23 / TC 95.65 / FAD 2.75 / TA-A 34.96 / AV-A 27.72
  · W/o Source Concatenation (removing source concatenation): FVD 467.20 (worsens by 159%, the worst of the three ablations) / TV-A 25.08 / FAD 4.20 (worsens by 53%). The failure manifests as severe background degradation — as expected, since SC is the core mechanism for anchoring source context and preserving non-target regions.
  · W/o SIGA (removing Source-Instruction Gated Attention): FVD 187.28 (only a slight 3.8% worsening) / TV-A 24.90 / FAD 3.26 (worsens by 18.5%). The failure manifests as audio hallucinations and stuttering. Evidently, SIGA's role is mainly reflected on the audio side and in instruction-following quality, with little effect on visual fidelity.
  · W/o Two-Stage Training Strategy (removing the two-stage curriculum): FVD 291.55 (worsens by 61.6%) / TV-A 24.87 / FAD 5.18 (worsens by 88.4%, the worst audio degradation among the three). The failure manifests as visual distortion and inconsistency.
  A common feature across all three ablations is that TV-A (text-video alignment) changes very little (25.23 → 25.08/24.90/24.87), indicating that the basic ability to follow instructions mainly comes from the backbone and the data itself, while architectural changes mainly affect fidelity and consistency.

[Data-side ablations entirely missing] [uncertain]
  · Filtering strictness ablation: none of the CoTracker3 motion threshold, LAION aesthetic threshold, Audiobox threshold, or the MLLM five-dimension bar has undergone sensitivity analysis; there is no performance comparison of "loose vs. strict filtering."
  · Data scale ablation: no scaling experiment at 25%/50%/100% of data volume was done, so the question "has 79K already saturated, would further scaling of synthesis volume still yield gains" cannot be answered — this is the question most in need of an answer for a work centered on "scalable synthesis."
  · Automatic verification-step ablation: no comparison of training effect "with/without the Qwen3-Omni five-dimension verification" was done, so the actual value of this verification step cannot be quantified, nor can it be verified whether the strict conjunction condition "must pass all five simultaneously" is too strict (potentially rejecting a large number of usable samples).
  · Data-engine component ablation: no comparison of the downstream model impact of data synthesized "with/without frame-wise cross-attention audio conditioning" was done, so the benefit of enforcing synchronization at the synthesis stage cannot be quantified.
  · Bidirectional instruction ablation: whether the reverse samples brought by instruction_reverse actually help mitigate synthetic distribution bias was not verified (the main text does not even mention this design).
  · Five-subset mixture ablation: no comparison of the effect of different task mixtures was done, nor is per-task breakdown performance reported.
  · Caption/instruction style ablation: no comparison of different instruction-generation strategies (e.g., templated vs. Qwen3-Omni free generation) was done.
Overall assessment: this work's ablation of the model architecture is thorough and its conclusions are clear, but almost no experimental verification was done for its data contribution — the effectiveness of the pipeline can only be indirectly supported by the overall result of "the final model performs well," with no way to decompose the independent contribution of each data design.

### [2026 Miscellaneous Audio-Video Joint Generation](../models/JAVG_2026_misc.md) ⚠️

This batch of works' ablations generally cluster around architectural modules, with genuine data ablation extremely rare — a shortcoming common to all seven:
[The six with no data ablation]
- ALIVE: "The paper contains no ablation studies isolating the impact of individual pipeline stages" — not a single stage of the six-stage data pipeline is individually ablated and verified. Given that its pipeline is the most complex in this batch (especially the costly five-step SubjectID correction sub-pipeline), the absence of a "with/without SubjectID correction" comparison is the most regrettable omission. Likewise, the benefits of the 13-dimensional quality classifier, the six-tier clarity filter, and the nine-domain mixture adjustment are all unquantified.
- NAVA: "No ablation studies on data composition are presented" — the ablations focus on architecture (Align-then-Fuse layer count) and guidance mechanisms, with no data-side ablation. The benefits of the 3:1 → 1:2 modality-mixture schedule and of the 160K SFT subset are both without controlled experiments.
- OmniCustom: no data ablation [uncertain]; the individual contributions of its three filtering rules (SyncNet dual thresholds, aesthetics <0.3, duration <10s) are not verified.
- StreamChar: ablations are in Tables 1–2, all architecture/system-side — the most valuable one is the sink chunk (persistent visual anchor) ablation: removing it worsens drift from 0.0067 to 0.0304, a degradation of about 4.5×, quantitatively proving the decisive role of the visual anchor for long-range consistency. There is also a comparison of single-stage vs. two-stage distillation. No data-side ablation.
- Baton: Table 2 ablates the VA-Planner components, Table 3 ablates RS-RoPE and backbone variants — all architecture-side. But one design choice highly relevant to the data/annotation paradigm is argued: continuous feature regression vs. discrete token prediction, with the paper claiming "regressing continuous features preserves richer semantic structure"; if Table 2 contains this comparison it would constitute empirical evidence for "the form of the semantic intermediary representation" [specific figures not extracted, uncertain].
- CCL: Table 2 reports ablation results, with metrics WER / Sync-C / Sync-D / DeSync / IB across five items, ablating the four modules TARP, LCT, DCR, UCG — architecture-side. But Table 1's horizontal comparison (CCL 4M vs. Ovi 30.7M vs. MOVA 50M), while not a strict ablation, is the most compelling "data efficiency" evidence in this batch (see quality_over_quantity_evidence). The ablation training configuration is 6,000 steps @batch 128, while the final configuration is 12,000 steps @batch 320.
[The only one that systematically studies the effect of "scorer selection": ITS-JAVG]
Although not a training-data ablation, its systematic ablation of "verifier combinations" is methodologically equivalent to an ablation of "data-filtering scorer combinations," making it the item closest to a data ablation in this batch:
(1) Single verifier vs. multiple verifiers: proves that "single-verifier guidance effectively improves its intended evaluation metrics, yet fails to achieve a balanced improvement across all metrics" — a single scorer only improves the metric it itself measures, while other metrics may actually degrade (an asymmetric trade-off).
(2) Empirical evidence of verifier hacking: the search algorithm will "exploit blind spots" of the scorer, i.e., degrade along dimensions the scorer cannot see.
(3) Identification of the optimal verifier combination: one of the paper's contributions is finding a verifier combination that yields balanced improvement.
(4) The benefit of ARW: compared with fixed-weight reward aggregation, adaptive weighting (normalized by the variance of each reward) yields a more balanced improvement.
The direct implication of these conclusions for training-data pipelines: filtering with a single aesthetic score or a single SyncNet threshold will systematically bias the retained data toward that scorer's preferences and cause degradation in its blind spots (e.g., audio quality, semantic plausibility); multi-operator collaboration (e.g., NAVA's triple alignment operators — SyncNet + SyncFormer + ImageBind) is in principle more robust.
[Overall judgment] This batch of JAVG works from the first half of 2026 is nearly blank on data ablation: not one performed a "filtering strictness ablation," a "caption density/style ablation," or a "data mixture ablation." The effectiveness of the data strategy is mainly endorsed by engineering intuition and final metrics, lacking traceable causal evidence. This stands in sharp contrast with the engineering complexity on the data side over the same period (ALIVE's six-stage pipeline, NAVA's multi-operator filtering) — pipelines keep getting more complex, but no one verifies whether each step is worth it.

[Subjective Arena ablation (Figure 7/8)] MOVA-720p ELO 1113.8, significantly higher than LTX-2 (1074.1), Ovi (925.4), and WAN2.1+MMAudio (886.9); win rates over 70% against both Ovi and cascaded systems, and 51.5% against LTX-2. ### [Ovi](../models/Ovi.md) ⚠️

The paper has only one ablation experiment, and it falls under "caption/conditioning-encoding structure" rather than "filtering strictness" or "data mixture" — but it happens to be key evidence for the AV joint-caption design.
[Ablation 1 (the only formal ablation, Section 5.5 + Table 3): separate vs. merged encoding of audio conditioning]
- Variant A "Ovi with CLAP": uses a CLAP text encoder to encode the non-speech audio description, and T5 to encode the speech transcript, attempting to decouple T2A and TTS to avoid interference between the two tasks.
- Variant B "Ovi" (the final scheme): merges the speech transcript and the audio description into a single piece of text, encoded with a single T5 embedding.
- Results: FD_PANNs 20.78 → 18.03 (↓2.75), FD_VGG 7.13 → 5.02 (↓2.11), IS 8.34 → 11.20 (↑2.86), CLAP 0.190 → 0.224 (↑0.034), WER 0.033 → 0.035 (essentially unchanged, linguistic correctness not harmed).
- Conclusion: separate encoding, though each stream can be handled on its own for sound effects or speech, fails to integrate the two into a unified, coherent audio track; merged encoding significantly improves audio fidelity and text-audio alignment while preserving linguistic correctness, and lets the audio and video towers share the same T5 representation, simplifying cross-modal modeling and enhancing multimodal coherence.
[Ablation 2 (architecture side, Figure 2, qualitative): scaled RoPE] Without scaling, the diagonal of the cross-modal RoPE affinity matrix is misaligned, hindering synchronization; scaling by 31/157≈0.197 sharply aligns the diagonal. Only a qualitative visualization is given, no quantitative metric.
[The "experimentally determined" conclusion on filtering strictness (no table)] The paper states it was "experimentally determined that even a small quantity of out-of-sync data can impede lip-sync abilities," i.e., experiments with different proportions of out-of-sync data were run and a strict threshold was chosen based on them, but the experimental setup and quantitative results are not given [uncertain]. This is the data ablation most worth asking about in the whole paper, yet it remains undisclosed.
[The cost to video quality (not a formal ablation, but an important observation)] Compared with the base model Wan2.2, Ovi's video quality is slightly degraded, which the paper attributes to "the joint training's audio-video dataset being much narrower than Wan2.2's pretraining corpus (a narrower audio-video dataset)" — a quantitatively perceptible cost from narrowing data coverage, also indirectly confirming that strict synchronization filtering reduces data diversity.

### [Script-a-Video](../models/Script-a-Video.md)

This work's ablation design concentrates on two dimensions, "representation format" and "architectural module" — it is a caption structure/style ablation, not a filtering strictness or data mixture ablation:
[Dimension 1: caption representation-format ablation (this work's most central evidence)]
The design is extremely clean: LTX-2-AV (a monolithic prompt) vs. LTX-2-AV-MTSS (an MTSS structured script) — the two share identical architecture and identical training paradigm, with the only variable being the organizational structure of the conditioning text. The paper explicitly states "any performance gap between them is directly and entirely attributable to the MTSS paradigm." Multi-shot scene results:
- Human-rated identity consistency (Cons.): 1.22 → 1.77 (+45%)
- Human-rated audio-video alignment (A-V): 1.18 → 1.85 (+56%)
- Human-rated multi-shot controllability (MSC): 1.00 → 1.71 (+71%)
- WER: 0.84 → 0.13 (an 85% reduction)
- Shot Boundary Deviation: 3.79 → 3.27 frames
- Audio Quality (UTMOS): 4.18 → 4.60
This set of results is strong evidence that "caption structure itself is a performance lever," and the cost of the change is zero (no architecture change, no added data).
[Dimension 2: cross-model format ablation on the caption side] Across five different captioning models, comparing "native monolithic output" vs. "MTSS structured output" (zero-shot prompting), all show positive gains:
- Gemini-2.5-Pro overall error rate 0.3959 → 0.2511 (Miss 0.1902→0.1285, Incorrect 0.0848→0.0644, Hallucination 0.1209→0.0582, with hallucination showing the largest drop, 52%); UGC-VideoCap composite 93.97 → 95.51;
- Qwen3.5-Omni-Flash overall error rate 0.5217 → 0.3655; UGC-VideoCap 76.07 → 83.28 (with Audio Avg rising sharply from 65.92 to 82.84);
- Qwen3-Omni overall error rate 0.5853 → 0.5156; UGC-VideoCap 62.80 → 71.54 (Audio Avg 52.81 → 68.79).
Notably: the audio-dimension gain is consistently larger than the visual-dimension gain, indicating that the explicit decomposition of audio events in the Event stream provides the greatest improvement in capturing auditory information. Meanwhile, Qwen3-Omni's Hallucination under zero-shot MTSS actually rises slightly from 0.1104 to 0.1355, the only negative entry in the whole table, indicating that forcing a weaker model to fill structured fields can induce fabrication.

### [Audio-Video Generation Evaluation Benchmarks Collection](../models/av_benchmarks.md) ⚠️

None of the five, being evaluation benchmarks, performs training-data strategy ablation [not applicable], but they provide a great deal of diagnostic conclusions useful for guiding data strategy:
[PhyAVBench's model-capability profile] A comprehensive evaluation of 17 SOTA models: even the strongest, Sora 2, achieves a CPRS of only 0.4512 (out of a maximum of 1.0, where 0.5 is random-orthogonal level), indicating all current models are basically in a state of "almost no physical sensitivity" for audio physical correctness; among V2A routes, MMAudio is best but reaches only 0.4003. SceneScribe-1M (a data mixture ablation, uniformly using a "baseline vs. baseline+this dataset" comparison, covering six downstream tasks): ① monocular depth estimation (MoGe, average improvement across 8 benchmarks on the Rel metric limited, on the order of ~6.14); ② 3D reconstruction (VGGT, pose AUC15 rises from 83.4 to 83.8); ③ 4D reconstruction (MonST3R, camera pose ATE drops from 0.108 to 0.099); ④ 2D point tracking (CoTracker3, δ_avg^vis rises from 76.6 to 77.4); ⑤ 3D point tracking (SpatialTrackerV2, AJ rises from 23.25 to 23.5); ⑥ text/pose-to-video generation (AC3D, FVD drops from 38.20 to 35.15, CLIP rises from 28.62 to 29.98) — the gain magnitude for the generation task is significantly larger than for the perception tasks, indicating geometric annotation has the highest marginal value for camera-controllable video generation. SpatialVID (a filtering strictness and distribution ablation): the core evidence is a distribution comparison against Panda-70M — SpatialVID-HQ is more tightly distributed across aesthetics, brightness, and motion, with 80% of clips having curved/turning trajectories, whereas Panda-70M is dominated by static content and 80%+ cannot be reconstructed by MegaSaM, quantitatively showing that for geometric purposes, "motion filtering strictness" is the primary determinant of data usability. WildWorld (an annotation-type ablation, baseline Wan2.2-TI2V-5B): compares five conditioning-injection methods — CamCtrl (camera trajectory), SkelCtrl (skeleton video), StateCtrl (discrete+continuous state, Transformer entity modeling), StateCtrl-AR (autoregressive state prediction); SkelCtrl achieves nearly a 100% relative improvement over the baseline on Action Following and State Alignment, but sacrifices aesthetic quality, constituting a typical "structured conditioning vs. image quality" trade-off; StateCtrl-AR demonstrates autoregressive potential but suffers from error accumulation. Action100M (a caption density and annotation-type ablation): compares the supervisory effectiveness of four field types — brief action, detailed action, brief caption, detailed caption — individually, and contrasts them against direct pseudo-labeling with PLM-3B alone, verifying that a hierarchical tree + LLM aggregation approach outperforms single-model pseudo-labeling; it also performs a k-means semantic resampling (k=10³/10⁴/10⁵) ablation for long-tail mitigation.

### [Video Generation Post-Training Data Strategy](../models/post_training_data.md) ⚠️

[The anchor paper's quantitative results (only two numbers, no per-component ablation)] On its internal video generation model, the RLHF stage yields a 31% improvement in the overall GSB metric, with the gain "most pronounced in the visual quality and motion quality dimensions, both showing enormous improvement," while the improvement in text alignment is relatively modest — the authors attribute this to the limited accuracy of the current text-alignment reward model, which constrains the room for optimizing semantic correctness. Stacking a prompt enhancer on top of this brings a further 20% overall GSB improvement, likewise driven by visual and motion quality, while keeping text alignment from regressing. Additionally, a visualization of the RLHF effect on Wan-2.1 is provided (Figure 2).
[Methodological flaws (must be noted)] The paper does not give: the baseline model's name, the definition of the GSB denominator (is the 31% the Good rate, or (G−B)/N?), the annotated sample size, the number of annotators, confidence intervals, per-dimension scores, comparisons against other post-training schemes, the standalone gain from SFT, or the quality-speed trade-off curve before/after AD distillation. There is no data-side ablation of any kind (no filtering strictness ablation, no caption density ablation, no data mixture ablation), and no results are reported on any public benchmark such as VBench. Hence its "31%/20%" figures are not comparable across works. [uncertain]
[Horizontal comparable ablation evidence]
· Cosmos-Predict 2.5 provides the most solid per-domain SFT ablation on this topic (human win rate, SFT vs. pretrained baseline): robot manipulation 72.6% vs. 8.3%, object permanence 50.9% vs. 27.7%, high motion 44.0% vs. 34.7%, complex scenes 42.6% vs. 35.4%, driving 47.9% vs. 28.8% — directly quantifying the benefit of "curating SFT data by domain," and the benefit is not proportional to the amount of data within the domain (the 730K robot-manipulation domain yields the largest benefit, while the 10.4M object-permanence domain yields a moderate benefit), indicating that the gap between the domain and the base model's ability is more decisive for SFT gain than data quantity;
· Cosmos's rCM distillation ablation: the 4-step generation's PAI-Bench score is almost on par with the teacher (T2W 0.764 vs. 0.768, I2W 0.816 vs. 0.810, with the distilled version's I2W even slightly higher);
· AVSCap's key claim: RL gains > scaling SFT data volume — direct evidence for "post-training investment takes priority over annotation-volume expansion";
· MOVA's prompt-rewriter ablation: human Arena ELO rises from 982.9 to 1025.3, independently quantifying the value of PE (consistent in direction with the anchor paper's qualitative conclusion that PE brings a 20% GSB gain);
· StreamChar's ablation: single-stage distillation performs worse than two-stage (mutually corroborating with the anchor paper's design of splitting AD into three sub-stages);
· video-SALMONN 2's MrDPO: reduces the 7B model's caption error rate by 28% relative to baseline;
· LongCat-Video's HPSv3-percentile design (taking the top 30% highest-scoring frames) and the Motion RM's design of taking grayscale video as input are both targeted ablation products aimed at reward-signal contamination, but the paper gives no comparison numbers for them [uncertain].

### [Merged Survey of Mainstream Video Pretraining Datasets: Panda-70M, InternVid, Koala…](../models/pretraining_datasets.md)

**Four of the seven performed controlled data-comparison experiments under the same architecture and compute — this is the most valuable quantitative evidence in this entire survey**:
[Koala-36M — the most complete ablation design] Training a single Sora-style 3D full-attention DiT (T5 text encoder + 3D causal VAE) from scratch, at 2 seconds and 256×256, on A100-80G, batch 32, lr 1e-4, all trained through 140M samples, evaluated with VBench (with prompt expansion applied to VBench captions to bridge the domain gap). The VBench total scores of the six comparison groups: Panda-70M **0.6493** → Koala-w/o TSA (48M unfiltered) **0.7140** → Koala-37M-manual (manual multi-threshold) **0.7073** → Koala-36M(VTSS) **0.7156** → Koala-w/o TSA+conditioning injection **0.7433** → **Koala-36M+conditioning injection 0.7460**. Three separable conclusions: (a) **segmentation + annotation improvements account for the largest share** (Panda→Koala-w/o TSA total score +6.5 points, mainly reflected in temporal metrics: subject consistency 0.8584→0.9335, background consistency 0.9435→0.9668, temporal flickering 0.9576→0.9857); (b) **VTSS outperforms manual multi-threshold filtering** (0.7156 > 0.7073, and the VTSS set is even smaller — 36M vs. 37M); (c) **using the quality score as a conditioning input is the single largest lever** (+0.0304), especially rescuing the dynamic-degree metric (0.5750→0.9194). Final Koala-36M+conditioning vs. Panda-70M: **total score +9.67 points, semantic score +28.2 points (0.5915 vs. 0.3093)**, while **using only half the data volume of Panda**. Additional resolution/duration experiments' FVD: 256-2s **549.79 vs. 570.87**, 256-4s **354.79 vs. 451.09**, 512-2s **392.26 vs. 579.57**.
[MiraData — the cleanest caption density/style ablation among the seven] Under the same model, changing only the prompt granularity: short caption → dense → structured, degree of dynamics 9.45→17.39→**19.53**, Tracking Strength 27.03→52.53→**68.85**, temporal consistency DTC 24.39→46.13→**60.83**, overall alignment 7.73→14.88→**15.36**. **The conclusion is extremely clear and counterintuitive: longer, more structured captions do not improve image quality** (aesthetic score 4.84→5.14→4.99, imaging quality 63.64→63.43→64.07, essentially flat), **but substantially improve dynamics, temporal consistency, and text alignment (overall alignment nearly doubles from short to structured)**. This is the cleanest evidence in the entire industry for "the benefit of caption structuring." There is also a data-source ablation: MiraDiT trained on MiraData vs. WebVid-10M, degree of dynamics 7.12→**15.46**, Tracking 22.36→**49.47** (both roughly double), but the authors honestly flag a weakness — OpenSora is still better on 3D consistency (MAE 75.45 vs. 85.27) and subject alignment (18.12 vs. 14.67).
[LVD-2M — filtering strictness ablation] Same architecture, a 1.75B MagicVideo-style T2V, pretrained at 32 frames then extended to 65 frames, with the two datasets under fully identical configuration (batch 64, 4-step gradient accumulation, 30k iterations, roughly one pass through 2M). Of VBench's **16 metrics, LVD-2M wins 10**, with key items: degree of dynamics 20.00%→**28.06%**, object class 76.95%→**86.93%**, human action 61.40%→**76.20%**; it loses on background consistency, temporal flickering, aesthetics, imaging quality, and multiple objects — i.e., the classic trade-off between **"more dynamic" and "more stable/beautiful"**. The authors proactively point out that this comparison is unfavorable to themselves: **VBench prompts average only 7.6 words, close to WebVid's 14.1 words and far from their own 88.7 words**. A generation-side user study (200 responses): for diffusion I2V, degree of dynamics preference is **60%/34%/6%** (fine-tuned/no preference/pre-fine-tuning); for LM-style T2V, degree of dynamics 52%/24%/24%, text matching 58%/10%/32%.
[OpenVid-1M — a combined data + architecture ablation] STDiT at 256×256: WebVid-10M (VQAA 13.40/VQAT 13.34/Blip_bleu 23.45/Clip_temp 99.62), Panda-50M (17.08/9.60/24.06/99.60), **OpenVid-1M (17.78/12.98/24.93/99.75)**; at 1024×1024, WebVid+4× super-resolution 69.26/65.74, Panda+4× super-resolution 63.25/53.21, **OpenVidHD-0.4M 73.46/68.58** — **showing native high-definition data is significantly better than low-definition + super-resolution**. Architecture+data ablation: STDiT+Ours-0.4M (11.11/12.46) → MVDiT+Ours-0.4M (22.39/14.15) → **MVDiT+OpenVid-1M full set (24.87/14.57)**.
[UltraVideo — resolution ablation] UltraWan-1K (LoRA) exceeds the official Wan-T2V-1.3B-480p on several VBench metrics (subject consistency 97.27 vs. 96.11, object class 82.29 vs. 66.66); the key comparison is that **Wan-T2V-1.3B, pushed directly to 1K, collapses badly** (aesthetics 54.82, object class 33.33, scene 0.00), while after fine-tuning with UltraVideo it recovers to normal — **proving that high-resolution capability must come from native high-resolution data, and cannot be obtained by upscaling at inference time**. Note that its VBench evaluation uses only **about 96 prompts (one-tenth of VBench)**, a rather small sample; the authors also point out that VBench is not well-suited to evaluating high resolution (degree of dynamics and background consistency can contradict human perception, and at true 4K, motion smoothness and degree of dynamics directly OOM).
[The two without data ablation] **Panda-70M** has only end-to-end comparisons of "trained with it vs. trained with something else" (AnimateDiff trained on Panda-2M, UCF101 FVD **421.9 vs. 499.3** for the official 2.5M version, and **the generated results have no watermark**, compared against WebVid-derived models; UMT on Panda-5M, zero-shot retrieval MSR-VTT R@1 **37.2 vs. 30.2**), with no internal strategy ablation. **InternVid** likewise has only end-to-end comparisons: WebVid-10M alone vs. +InternVid-18M-AES, UCF101 FVD **705.25→616.51**, IS 13.97→**21.04**, FID 98.25→**60.25**, MSR-VTT CLIPSIM 0.2657→**0.2951**; and being **watermark-free** is the primary reason it has been widely adopted by the open-source community. But it **has no coarse-scale vs. fine-scale caption ablation** — a clear omission for a paper whose core selling point is being "multi-scale."
## Evidence for quality vs. quantity (cases where small, high-quality data outperforms large, messy data)

`quality_over_quantity_evidence` · Detail level: brief

### [Allegro](../models/Allegro.md) ⚠️

Structural evidence exists but there is no isolated experiment: the design philosophy of the entire pipeline is to "trade an extremely low retention rate for quality" — video count drops from 500M to about 2M at the fine-tuning stage (a 0.4% retention rate), and the final 10K-step fine-tuning stage uses only this 2M curated set, i.e., the final quality calibration is completed with less than four-tenths of a percent of the data; Appendix A Fig. 11 shows the aesthetic distribution shifting rightward across stages, corroborating that the curated set's quality does indeed increase progressively. The paper's abstract and introduction also explicitly list "striking a balance between data volume and quality" as key to achieving commercial-grade results.
However, the paper does not provide a "small and refined vs. large and messy" controlled experiment (e.g., using wide-threshold data fine-tuned for the same number of steps as a baseline), so this evidence is indirect, design-level evidence rather than an empirical conclusion. [Partially uncertain]

### [Apollo](../models/Apollo.md) ⚠️

The paper provides no direct quantitative evidence that "small and refined beats large and messy," but its overall design implies a strong quality-first tendency, which can serve as indirect positional evidence:
[Positional evidence 1] An aggressive end-to-end retention rate of only 27% — willing to discard nearly three-quarters of candidate data in exchange for "high-quality, strictly aligned audio–video–caption triplets," a clear trade-off of quantity for quality.
[Positional evidence 2] Stage III is dedicated to "quality-refinement post-training," using a small, manually curated high-quality set for fine-tuning at the end — a typical pyramid structure of "learn capability at large scale, refine quality at small scale."
[Positional evidence 3] Even at the 81M scale, the paper still calls on commercial flagship models like Gemini 2.5-Pro for annotation, a signal that annotation quality is prioritized over cost.
[Key gap] All three points above are design choices rather than experimental evidence — the paper does not run a controlled experiment of "27% curated data vs. 100% of all data under equal compute," so it cannot prove that the 27% cutoff is optimal, nor can it quantify the benefit brought by filtering. By comparison, MOVA at least provides comparative figures for its 360p→720p curriculum. On this dimension, Apollo offers **posture** rather than **evidence**. [Uncertain]

### [CineDance / CineDance-1M](../models/CineDance.md) ⚠️

The paper provides no direct controlled experiment showing that "small and refined beats large and messy" — there is no comparison of training the same model on the CineDance-1M subset vs. a larger, messier dataset.
[Indirect evidence]
  · Artifact-audit comparison: CineDance-1M's non-compliance rate is 2.8% vs. Koala-36M's 37.4%, a 13.4x improvement. Koala-36M is far larger in scale than CineDance-1M, and the paper uses this to argue that "although this dataset is small, it is far cleaner," but it does not translate this cleanliness difference into a comparison of downstream generation metrics.
  · Cross-dataset comparison table (Tab. 6): argues for comprehensive leadership on the quality dimension via the combined advantages of "1080p + native audio track + shot-level dual-modality dense annotation + 92.8s/24.2 shots" — this is an attribute comparison, not an effect comparison.
[The paper's overall stance] actually leans more toward "having both quality and scale" rather than "trading quantity for quality" — deliberately retaining a scale of one million sequences, while using a design of "no hard pruning, storing all metadata" to hand the quality-quantity trade-off decision to downstream users, which itself is a kind of avoidance of the "one-size-fits-all pursuit of a high-quality subset" route.
[Conclusion] This dimension lacks direct experimental support. [Uncertain]

### [CogVideoX](../models/CogVideoX.md) ⚠️

Evidence exists but is less thorough than contemporaneous work, and the paper's own stance leans toward "scale and quality both matter":
· Evidence supporting quality-first: (1) using 6 Video-LLaMA classifiers plus dual optical-flow/aesthetic thresholds for strict upfront filtering, preferring to discard an entire clip rather than perform fine-grained splitting and repair, ultimately retaining only 35M single-shot clips; (2) the final stage uses only a 20% high-quality subset for 10k-step fine-tuning (less than 1/40 of stage 1's 400k steps), which effectively removes subtitles and watermarks from generated results and improves visual quality; (3) caption quality is listed by the paper as one of the key factors for improving generation quality and semantic alignment, i.e., "the same video with better text annotation yields a better model."
· Counter-evidence (honestly recorded by the paper): while high-quality fine-tuning improves visual quality, it causes "slight degradation of the model's semantic capability," indicating that over-tightening data quality sacrifices semantic coverage and concept diversity — a direct observation of the cost of the "small and refined" route, which is relatively rare among similar papers.
· The paper's overall stance leans toward scaling: the conclusion explicitly states "CogVideoX is scalable, and as the size of model parameters, data volume, and training volume increase, the performance will get better in the future," and does not advocate for a small-data route.
· No controlled experiment is given showing "small and refined data beats large and messy data" [Uncertain].

### [Cosmos-Predict2.5](../models/Cosmos-Predict25.md) ⚠️

There is fairly strong strategic evidence but no rigorous controlled experiment:
(1) The most direct statement is the cross-generation strategy shift — actively compressing the clip survival rate from 30% to 4% (retaining about 1/7.5 of the previous strategy's amount), while expanding the raw material pool by 1.75x, essentially "using a wider funnel opening to get a smaller, cleaner outlet." The paper self-assesses that this yields "a dataset that is larger, cleaner, and semantically richer" as well as "enhanced pre-training efficiency and improved downstream generalization."
(2) Indirect evidence on parameter efficiency: Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B surpasses Wan2.2-27B-A14B (0.806) and Wan2.1-14B (0.797) on PAI-Bench I2W with a score of 0.810; in human evaluation, the 2B model — 60.0% smaller than Wan2.2-5B and 85.7% smaller than Wan2.1-14B in size — still slightly outperforms Wan2.2-5B (30.0% vs. 26.2%) and is comparable to Wan2.1-14B (33.0% vs. 34.8%); the 14B post-trained model achieves a 48.6% vs. 31.8% human win rate against Wan2.1-14B, and 38.1% vs. 35.9% against the double-parameter Wan2.2-27B-A14B. The team attributes this to stricter data and a better text encoder, but does not perform a single-factor decomposition of the data's contribution.
(3) In the cooldown stage, using only 388K curated 4K videos is enough to improve detail precision and motion smoothness — a typical small-scale, high-quality data finishing touch.
(4) In robot policy experiments, 100 real demonstrations + 500 generative-augmentation samples achieved 24/30, while 100 real demonstrations + unlimited traditional pixel augmentation achieved only 5/30 — showing that the semantic diversity/quality of augmented samples matters far more than quantity.
Still missing is a direct comparison of "strict vs. loose filtering under the same data volume." [Uncertain: the single-factor gain of the 4% retention rate relative to 30% cannot be quantified]

### [Data-Juicer 2.0](../models/Data-Juicer.md)

The Data-Juicer Sandbox's T2V case is, in this survey, the most complete and most reproducible example of the "quality over quantity" evidence chain, because its data, recipe, model, and evaluation are all open source.
[Evidence 1: An extreme 12.09% retention rate still achieves SOTA] From about 1.217 million candidates, only 147,176 (12.09%) were retained, and the model trained on this 1/8 of the data topped VBench. If quantity were paramount, using the full 1.217 million should have been better — the experimental conclusion is the opposite.
[Evidence 2: Data repetition beats compute scaling — the sharpest point] The paper explicitly concludes that repeating the highest-quality data 6 times yields performance higher than using 8x the compute with sub-optimal data. This is methodologically devastating, because it pushes the "quality vs. quantity" comparison forward to "quality vs. compute" — even giving sub-optimal data 8x the compute cannot catch up with simple repetition of high-quality data. This directly challenges the common wisdom that "data diversity necessarily beats data repetition," showing that in a task as sensitive to video-text alignment as text-to-video generation, the negative impact of one low-quality sample outweighs the diminishing marginal returns of one repeated sample.
[Evidence 3: Winning with a 22x compute gap] The model that topped VBench used "at least 22x lower compute cost" compared to competitors such as Gen-3 and VEnhancer. This is a manifestation of "small and refined data beating large and messy data" in a cross-team horizontal comparison.
[Evidence 4: A minimalist recipe beats a complex funnel] The final effective recipe contains only two operators (NSFW filtering + CLIP frame-text similarity filtering), far simpler than the ten-plus-stage funnels of most model teams, and it has been rigorously validated through ablation. This suggests that "number of filters" and "data quality" are not positively correlated — strict gatekeeping on a small number of genuinely relevant dimensions beats loose screening across many weakly relevant dimensions.
[Evidence 5: Cross-architecture transferability] The recipe searched out at small scale (EasyAnimate) remains effective when transferred to T2V-Turbo, showing that "what counts as high-quality data" is somewhat model-agnostic and not an overfitting artifact of one specific model.
[Methodological significance] DJ provides not just conclusions but also a reusable mechanism for reaching those conclusions (Probe-Analyze-Refine). For video generation teams, the most practical takeaway is: rather than designing a long funnel by experience, first use a small model to probe each candidate filtering dimension in tertiles, and keep only the few dimensions that actually bring evaluation gains.

### [Foley-Omni](../models/Foley-Omni.md) ⚠️

There is no direct controlled experiment showing "small and refined beats large and messy," but there are several designs that indirectly embody this philosophy.
[Indirect evidence 1: Efficient small-data fine-tuning in Stage 3] The final V2ST capability is learned from only 216 hours of data (4.4% of the total) within 2 epochs, achieving WER 7.59 (better than Ground Truth's 8.03) and DeSync 0.16 (close to GT's 0.14). This shows that after building a foundation in earlier large-scale stages, the target task only needs a very small but highly matched amount of data to align — a manifestation of "small and refined," but lacking a control group trained with "larger-scale, lower-quality V2ST data."
[Indirect evidence 2: The pipeline's strict-filtering orientation] The design of six filtering thresholds plus field-level energy gating itself embodies a quality-first orientation — in particular, acoustic post-verification would rather leave a field empty (reducing supervision signal) than keep a possibly incorrect annotation, a clear case of "better none than bad."
[Indirect evidence 3: Rigorous manual review of the evaluation set] The 300 evaluation samples underwent three-dimensional manual gatekeeping — a scale far smaller than common benchmarks but with controllable quality.
[Counter-evidence] The data composition also reflects a pursuit of "quantity": VisualTTS invested 1,980 hours (40%) to support speech capability, showing that on this key capability the authors chose a large-scale-data route. One could say this paper follows a hybrid strategy: "foundational capability relies on quantity, task alignment relies on quality."
[Uncertain] The paper does not conduct any data-scale scaling experiment (e.g., comparing performance trained on 50% of the data), so the quality-quantity trade-off cannot be quantitatively established.

### [Goku](../models/Goku.md) ⚠️

The paper provides no direct controlled experiment showing "small and refined data beats large and messy data," but the design of the entire pipeline and several pieces of indirect evidence embody this orientation:
(1) **Division of labor between public pretraining and in-house fine-tuning** — 100 million LAION images (large and messy) are used for pretraining as a base, and 60 million in-house high-quality images (small and refined) are used for fine-tuning to set the final tone, explicitly acknowledging that public data alone is insufficient to support final quality.
(2) **The higher the resolution, the stricter the threshold, the less the data** — the 1080p tier retains only 7M (about 19.4% of the 480p tier's 36M); high-resolution training is effectively conducted on a highly curated small subset, a typical case of "using small, refined data in the later stage to set the quality ceiling."
(3) **Deduplication arbitrated by aesthetic score** — when two duplicate clips exist, the one with the higher aesthetic score is kept, a sample-level quality-first principle.
(4) **Extremely strict OCR threshold (text area allowed is only 1% at the 720p tier)** — willing to sacrifice a large amount of data volume to ensure image cleanliness.
(5) **Joint-training ablation** (Figure 5b) — adding high-quality image data improves video generation quality, which can be viewed as side evidence that "the value of a high-quality sample exceeds that of an equal number of homogeneous video samples."
All of the above is design-orientation evidence, not controlled experimental evidence. [Partially uncertain]

### [Hailuo / MiniMax Video](../models/Hailuo.md) ⚠️

[Uncertain] (no direct evidence, only directional statements). Hailuo 02's official wording — "training data expanded 4x, with improved quality and diversity" — emphasizes both quantity and quality simultaneously, which is a narrative of "bigger and better" rather than "small and refined beating large and messy," and therefore cannot serve as evidence for quality prioritized over quantity. MiniMax provides no comparative case of a small high-quality dataset beating a large low-quality dataset.
A weak piece of circumstantial evidence is the positioning of the NCR architecture: MiniMax places the narrative weight of generational improvement on architectural efficiency (2.5x training/inference efficiency) and compute cost-effectiveness rather than a data-scale race, indirectly reflecting that its technical route favors efficiency over stacking volume — but this is an argument about compute efficiency, not data quality.

### [HunyuanVideo-Foley](../models/HunyuanVideo-Foley.md) ⚠️

This work overall is a case of "dual investment in both quantity and quality," but it provides no evidence separating the contribution of the two, so no supporting conclusion for "small and refined beats large and messy" can be extracted from it:
[Design evidence pointing toward "quality-first" (all qualitative)]
- An effective sample rate >32 kHz is a fairly strict high-frequency fidelity threshold; the elimination rate among internet video audio tracks under this threshold should be broad, and the team chose to keep the high threshold rather than relax it to increase data volume;
- A dual gate of AudioBox aesthetic score + SNR;
- Dual-dimension alignment filtering with ImageBind + AV-align — this is the most costly stage yet it was kept;
- The high-quality tag plus mandatory tagging at inference time is essentially a further tilt toward the high-quality distribution at inference time.
[Evidence pointing toward "quantity-first"] The 100,000-hour scale is the paper abstract's number-one selling point, and it explicitly characterizes its pipeline as "scalable" rather than "curated/refined" — the wording itself places scale ahead of precision. As a reference point, the public dataset VGGSound is only about 550 hours, and this work's scale is about 180x that.
[A piece of counter-evidence about "not discarding low-quality data"] The existence of the high-quality tag mechanism itself shows that the team chose to keep data of varying quality and distinguish it with tags, rather than eliminating low-to-medium-quality data outright. This points to the same conclusion as UniVerse-1's LQLS strategy — under a scale-pursuing premise, "annotating the quality dimension and selecting at inference time" is more cost-effective than "one-size-fits-all filtering." This effectively dissolves the "quality vs. quantity" binary opposition: instead of making a trade-off, quality is turned into a conditionable variable.
[Key missing piece] There is no controlled experiment of "small-scale strict screening vs. large-scale coarse screening," no data-scale scaling curve, and no ablation on filtering strictness. Therefore this work can serve neither as evidence for "quality over quantity" nor as a counter-example — it can only show that the combination of "large scale + strict audio filtering" achieves SOTA, but the individual contributions of the two factors cannot be separated. [Uncertain]
[A cross-sectional observation] This work's lead margin on audio quality metrics (MOS-Q 4.14 vs. MMAudio 3.58, PQ 6.59 vs. 6.17) is noticeably larger than its lead margin on temporal synchronization metrics (DeSync 0.74 vs. 0.80). Audio quality is the dimension most directly affected by data bandwidth and aesthetic filtering, while synchronization depends more on architectural design (interleaved RoPE, Synchformer). This pattern of divergence is consistent with a division of labor where "the data side pushes on audio quality and the architecture side pushes on synchronization," and can be viewed as indirect circumstantial evidence for the effectiveness of the data strategy — but this is an inference, not a conclusion stated by the paper. [Uncertain]

### [HunyuanVideo](../models/HunyuanVideo.md) ⚠️

There is no direct controlled experimental evidence, but the funnel design of both generations is itself a strong expression of the "quality over quantity" stance, and version 1.5 provides quantifiable indirect evidence:
· 1.5's data volume monotonically shrinks from 800 million to 1 million (an end-to-end retention rate of 0.125%), yet with only 8.3B parameters (about 64% of the original 13B version) it matches larger, more expensive models such as Kling 2.1, Seedance Pro, and Veo3 in human evaluation. The team attributes this to "meticulous data curation" as one of several key factors — this is case-level evidence that "a smaller model + refined data can rival a larger model," but it lacks a controlled comparison of "the same model with different data," so strictly speaking it does not constitute experimental proof.
· The original version explicitly states that each filtering stage eliminates 50%–80% of the data, and reserves the most expensive manual annotation for only the final 1 million samples, reflecting a resource-allocation philosophy of "the later the stage, the more refined, the more concentrated the investment."
· A noteworthy counter-signal: relative to the original, 1.5 expands the raw pool to more than 10 million hours (a significant expansion), showing that the team's practice is "first expand the candidate pool, then apply stricter filtering" — i.e., the quality improvement comes from a higher elimination rate rather than a smaller initial pool; quantity and quality are not a trade-off relationship here. [Uncertain]

### [InstructAV2AV](../models/InstructAV2AV.md) ⚠️

[Uncertain] The paper conducts no data-scale/quality trade-off experiment at all; there is no direct evidence.

[Design that indirectly embodies a "quality over quantity" orientation]
  · Strict conjunctive acceptance: automatic verification requires a sample to pass all five dimensions simultaneously (instruction fidelity / content preservation / perceptual quality / AV sync / safety) — failing any one leads to elimination. This is a clear "better none than bad" orientation, far more conservative than a weighted-sum soft-screening approach.
  · Stringent material eligibility: speech samples must have "temporal alignment between speech and the visible on-screen speaker," and non-speech samples must have "a single, clearly dominant sound source" — both are highly exclusive hard conditions that would eliminate a large amount of material that could otherwise be marginally usable.
  · Triple manual gatekeeping of the evaluation set: 1K samples reviewed by 20 people in 10 reviewer pairs, independently across dimensions.
  · Restraint in the data scale itself: 79K pairs (about 110 hours) is a small dataset for fine-tuning a 5B-class model, far smaller than the million-scale common in similar work. That the model can surpass baselines at this scale indirectly indicates the value of high-quality controlled pairing — but there is no control group, so the contribution cannot be disentangled from "architectural improvement (SC/SIGA)" as opposed to "data quality."

[Existing counter-tension] On the other hand, the paper positions "scalable" as the core selling point of the pipeline, implying that part of its value proposition rests on "being able to produce more data" — which is in some tension with the "small and refined" narrative. The paper does not clarify whether 79K is a deliberately controlled scale ceiling (quality-first) or the compute ceiling at the time (wanting to produce more).

[The experiment that most should have been done but wasn't] Using the same pipeline to produce datasets of different sizes under different verification strictness levels (e.g., "passing only 3 items" yields 200K pairs vs. "passing all five" yields 79K pairs) and comparing training effects — this experiment would both demonstrate the value of strict acceptance and answer the question of "where the scale-quality optimum lies for synthetic data," which is of the most general significance for the synthetic-data paradigm.

### [2026 Miscellaneous Joint Audio-Video Generation](../models/JAVG_2026_misc.md) ⚠️

[CCL — the most direct, most quantified "small and refined beats large and messy" evidence in this batch]
Table 1 explicitly lists the three parties' training data volumes side by side: CCL 4M vs. Ovi 30.7M vs. MOVA 50M — CCL uses about 1/8 of Ovi's data volume and about 1/12 of MOVA's, and with only 12,000 steps of joint training (batch 320), achieves SOTA performance. The paper states it "achieves state-of-the-art performance with substantially reduced computational resources compared to recent academic approaches, while requiring minimal training data by leveraging pre-trained diffusion models."
It should be objectively pointed out that CCL's efficiency does not come purely from "high data quality," but from three factors compounding:
(1) Strong base-model transfer: the video stream is initialized from Wan2.1-14B pretrained weights — most visual priors are inherited for free, so its 4M data only needs to learn the cross-modal connection rather than learning video generation from scratch.
(2) A narrow but deep domain: the data sources (OpenHumanVid + interviews + short dramas + films) are highly concentrated on "narrative content with people speaking," trading breadth for density within that domain.
(3) Multi-task reuse: five tasks are sampled with mixed probabilities (joint 0.6, A2V 0.15, V2A 0.15, T2V/I2V 0.1, T2A 0.1), so the same batch of data is repeatedly reused in five different ways, multiplying effective data efficiency.
Therefore a more accurate statement is: "strong base model + narrow domain + multi-task reuse" can compress data requirements by an order of magnitude — this is an extremely valuable route for resource-constrained teams to reference.
[NAVA — evidence on the parameter-efficiency side + a one-in-a-thousand SFT purification]
(1) Parameter efficiency: with 6.3B parameters it sets new SOTA on Sync-C / Sync-D / video quality / audio WER on Verse-Bench, while "using 2x to 5x fewer parameters than open-source baselines."
(2) An extreme data-purification ratio: 15M → 160K SFT subset (a retention rate of about 1.07%), and the captions for this subset are regenerated by the more expensive Gemini-3-Pro. This practice of "curating one-in-a-thousand plus upgrading annotation" is itself a strong quality-first statement — but the paper does not conduct a controlled experiment "with/without the 160K SFT stage" [Uncertain], so the benefit cannot be quantified.
(3) Strict filtering: 100M videos → 15M (about a 15% retention rate), showing the team is willing to discard 85% of the data.
[OmniCustom — strict screening in exchange for customization quality]
From SpeakerVid-5M's 5.2M clips / 8,000 hours, screened down to 1M clips / 2,500 hours (a clip retention rate of 19%, duration retention rate of 31%), retaining only clips with good synchronization (SyncNet |offset|≤3 and conf>1.5), aesthetic score ≥0.3, and duration ≥10 seconds. Preparing high-purity data for the high-precision task of identity + timbre customization is a typical quality-first orientation. But there is no control comparing "loose vs. strict filtering." [Uncertain]
[ALIVE — counter-evidence: scale remains the primary lever]
ALIVE takes the opposite route: 700k hours of speech, 11M joint samples, 384M audio samples — winning through sheer absolute scale, while using an extremely complex six-stage pipeline to ensure quality. Its "11M → 4.3M balanced" reduction through balancing (retaining 39%) and its "5M SFT with high-aesthetic:realistic = 3:1" mixture ratio show that it performs fine-grained distributional control on top of a scale foundation — this is "scale + quality both pursued simultaneously," not an either/or choice.
[StreamChar — evidence of generalization: short in training, long in inference]
All training data is ≤20 seconds, yet it can generate a continuous 5-minute stream — this is another form of "achieving a lot with a little": not relying on long-video data but achieving long-horizon generalization through architectural mechanisms (a progress-aware pointer + persistent visual anchor). The sink-chunk ablation (drift 0.0067 vs. 0.0304) proves this route is viable. The implication for the data side is that certain capabilities (long-horizon consistency) may be more economically solved with architecture than with data (collecting long videos).
[ITS-JAVG — the most radical "zero training data" stance]
Completely training-free, obtaining quality improvements via multi-verifier search at inference time — shifting "quality" from both the data side and the training side entirely to the inference side. This is the most thorough challenge in this batch to the assumption that "more data = a better model." But the cost is a 5–10x amplification of inference cost, and the paper itself reveals the risk that verifiers can be gamed.
[Baton] With 1.5M clips, the smallest scale among the joint-generation works in this batch, its performance gain is attributed to VA-Planner's semantic planning rather than data — also a route of "compensating for data with method," but there is no data-volume control experiment. [Uncertain]

### [Joint Audio-Video Generation Baseline Collection](../models/JavisDiT_baselines.md) ⚠️

This collection provides two different kinds of "quality/efficiency-first" evidence, which need to be considered separately:
[Type 1: An explicit statement of the quality-quantity relationship (JavisDiT++, the most valuable)]
- The conclusion in Appendix D.2 is precisely worded: "ensuring good data quality is the foundation for increasing sample quantity to improve training effectiveness" — this is not the common "small and refined beats large and messy," but a more nuanced hierarchical relationship: quality is a threshold condition, and quantity is a lever above that threshold. This is spiritually consistent with Ovi's statement that "even a small quantity of out-of-sync data can impede lip-sync abilities," but JavisDiT++ elevates it to a scalable principle.
- Behavioral corroboration: actively cutting TAVGBench from 1.1 million down to 355,000 (a retention rate of 32.3%), willing to use only three-tenths of the data to filter out samples containing speech, low aesthetics, low motion, and heavy on-screen text.
- However, note that the same team made the exact opposite decision on the audio side ("no filtering at all" to maximize category coverage), showing that "quality-first" is not an unconditional principle but depends on whether that modality's bottleneck is quality or coverage — audio data is itself relatively clean and coverage is the bottleneck, whereas video data varies in quality and quality is the primary bottleneck. This idea of "differentiating filtering strategy by modality" is one of the most fine-grained data insights in this collection.
[Type 2: A training-efficiency claim (UniAVGen)] With 1.3 million training samples compared to Ovi's 30.1 million, it achieves an overall advantage across audio-video synchronization, timbre consistency, and emotional consistency. But this is a cross-model comparison rather than a controlled experiment: UniAVGen uses internally curated real-person data + an asymmetric cross-modal interaction architecture + a three-stage curriculum, which differs from Ovi in data distribution, architecture, and target domain (Ovi covers general scenarios, UniAVGen focuses on real people); therefore this 23x data-efficiency gap is more attributable to "narrow-domain focus + architectural efficiency" than to pure data quality. As a case of "small vertical-domain data can beat large general-domain data," it has reference value, but it cannot be directly generalized.
[Type 3: Awareness of stratified data-source quality (Harmony)] The paper explicitly labels the annotation-quality attributes of three audio data sources — AudioCaps (about 128 hours, manually annotated), Clotho (about 31 hours, manually annotated), WavCaps (about 7,600 hours, automatically annotated) — and additionally applies consistency-score screening to speech data. This configuration of using both "a small, manually annotated refined set" and "a large, coarse, automatically annotated set" is itself a practice that weighs quality and quantity together, but no controlled experiment on the two's respective contributions is given. [Uncertain]
[MM-Diffusion's alternative form of quality-first] Using an extremely small dataset of less than 8 hours in total (2.7 hours + 5.2 hours), it achieved an 80% pass rate on a human Turing test as early as 2022 — relying on extreme focus in dataset selection (9 categories of strongly sound-image-coupled natural scenes + a single dance domain). This is an early success case of the "narrow-domain, high-fidelity, small data" route.
[Cautionary counter-example] The capability boundaries of all the works in this collection (≤10 seconds, single shot, fixed resolution, narrow domain) also show that small, refined data can validate a method's effectiveness but cannot support general capability — quality-first has its applicable boundaries.

### [Kling 3.0 / Kling Video 3.0 Omni](../models/Kling_30_Omni.md) ⚠️

[Uncertain, only indirect same-team evidence] Kling 3.0 Omni's official materials disclose no direct case of "small and refined beating large and messy," but the design of its quality-tuning stage (fine-tuning on a small curated set that is "task-balanced + extremely high video quality") itself embodies a quality-first engineering conviction. Koala-36M's public conclusion is closer to direct evidence: 36 million rigorously cleaned clips outperform the larger-scale (70 million) but more poorly split and captioned Panda-70M; meanwhile, the core motivation of its VTSS is actually the opposite — avoiding mistakenly deleting high-quality data with hard thresholds in pursuit of "refinement," i.e., emphasizing "precise screening" rather than "indiscriminate strictness."

### [LTX-2](../models/LTX-2.md) ⚠️

There is implicit evidence but no supporting controlled experiment. LTX-2's entire data strategy is to "screen out a subset with significantly higher audio information content from the larger LTX-Video dataset" — training an open-source SOTA joint audio-video model using a true subset of the parent set, with no degradation in visual quality (still surpassing Sora 2 Pro and Wan 2.2-14B on the leaderboard). This itself is a case of "targeted screening beats piling on full volume." Similarly, LTX-Video's fine-tuning stage uses only the subset with the highest aesthetic scores, embodying the same orientation.
But neither paper conducts a "full volume vs. subset" controlled experiment, neither gives the subset's proportion, nor quantifies the benefit of "quality over quantity." Therefore this can only be counted as a methodological tendency rather than experimental evidence. [Uncertain]

### [LongCat-Video](../models/LongCat-Video.md) ⚠️

[Uncertain]. The report provides no direct quantitative evidence for "small and refined beating large and messy" (no controlled experiment). Only strategic-level indirect manifestations can be observed: the SFT stage uses a "carefully curated, high-quality dataset" to complete style and instruction-following alignment in only 7.5k iterations (less than 1.1% of pretraining's 678k iterations); and the explicit design of "sampling by the inverse of caption embedding density," which explicitly favors rare samples and actively forgoes redundant high-density samples, implies the team's judgment that "distribution balance takes priority over sample count." But none of this is supported by ablation data.

### [MOVA](../models/MOVA.md) ⚠️

The paper does not conduct a "small and refined vs. large and messy" controlled experiment, but the design of its entire training curriculum is itself an engineering realization of this philosophy, and it carries quantitative traces:
- The data scale shrinks substantially stage by stage while performance keeps improving: 61,500 hours (Phase 1) → 37,600 hours (Phase 2, about 61%) → 11,000 hours (Phase 3, about 18%), while LSE-C/LSE-D improve monotonically across the three phases (Figure 9), with Phase 3's LSE-D declining further and then plateauing.
- The overall cleaning retention rate is only 26.39% (Table 1), of which the single rule "keep only speech segments" alone cuts about 26 percentage points — an aggressive trade-off actively giving up more than 40% of data diversity for the sake of the target capability (lip sync).
- Among Phase 2's three filters, the lip-audio correspondence subset has only ~2.5M clips and the DOVER technical-score subset only ~2.4M clips, substantially narrower than the OCR subtitle-free subset's ~9.5M; the paper explicitly calls the resulting final dataset of 16.8M clips "balancing scale and quality."
- Time allocation also reflects the emphasis on high-quality data: Phase 3 uses only 11,000 hours of data yet trains for 20 days (the longest of the three phases), far exceeding Phase 2's 7 days / 37,600 hours.
Note that all of this is indirect evidence from design intent and training curves, without a control-group experiment showing "large and messy data performs worse under equal compute." [Uncertain]

### [Merged entry: ① Mochi 1](../models/Mochi_MAGI_Motif.md) ⚠️

This is precisely the highlight of this merged-entry investigation; the three works together provide an evidence chain of increasing strength:
① Mochi 1: no evidence (no data disclosed). [Uncertain]
② MAGI-1: provides structural evidence — starting from tens of petabytes of raw material, it progressively narrows through 11 categories of Filter Actors + strict deduplication via dual CLIP/DINOv2 models + MLLM semantic re-screening, and explicitly states that "as resolution increases, data volume gradually decreases and the filtering strategy becomes stricter to ensure higher quality"; it cites LLM literature to argue that "even a small amount of duplicate data can significantly harm performance" as the motivation for strict deduplication. But it gives neither retention-rate figures nor a "small and refined vs. large and messy" controlled experiment, so this is indirect evidence at the level of design philosophy. [Partially uncertain]
③ Motif-Video 2B: the strongest evidence in this group, and one of the rarer kinds in the entire survey — it makes "quality beats quantity" the central thesis of the paper directly and provides comparable results. The paper opens by posing the question: "Training strong video generation models usually requires massive data, huge parameter counts, and enormous compute. This work asks: under a much smaller budget — fewer than 10 million clips, fewer than 100,000 H200 GPU hours — can strong text-to-video quality still be achieved?" and points out that the current strongest open-source models (Wan2.1, HunyuanVideo, Seedance) are "trained on hundreds of millions of curated clips, with parameter counts of 5B–14B," and that "while this resource concentration has produced impressive results, it has also narrowed participation: in effect, very few teams can actually train competitive video generation models." The conclusion is: with 2B parameters, fewer than 10 million clips, and training within 100,000 H200 hours, VBench reaches 83.76%, "surpassing Wan2.1-14B, with 7x fewer parameters and an order of magnitude less training data."
The core statement methodologically supporting this conclusion is: "Rather than maximizing raw scale, we prioritize curation quality to support resource-efficient training."
Caution to maintain: Motif's win comes simultaneously from architecture (Shared Cross-Attention, a three-segment backbone functional decomposition) and training techniques (TREAD token routing, REPA/V-JEPA early representation alignment, two rounds of SFT, and starting 720p training from an SFT checkpoint); the paper does not isolate the contribution of "data curation quality" from these factors via ablation; and its advantage is concentrated in the semantic dimension, while the quality dimension (subject/background consistency, temporal flicker) still lags behind the Wan series — the paper itself reads this as "a specific trade-off rather than an overall win," and explicitly states that "long-horizon temporal stability and appearance consistency remain the primary targets for further scaling up and improving the data." Therefore this is strong but non-isolated evidence.

### [Movie Gen](../models/Movie_Gen.md)

The evidence is very strong, and it is the core methodological claim of the entire paper. ① Pretraining side: under the strictest threshold, the data acceptance rate is below 1% (0.94% at the end of the funnel), i.e., only about one percent of videos from the raw pool are selected for high-resolution training — trading an extremely low retention rate for quality. ② Post-training side: video SFT uses only one manually curated small-scale set (training with only 512 H100s, compared to up to 6144 H100s for pretraining), yet brings an overall quality gain of +34.65 net win rate — one of the largest single quality gains in the entire paper. ③ Audio side: pretraining uses O(1,000)K hours (on the order of one million hours) of data, while fine-tuning uses only O(1)K hours of film-grade audio-video data + O(10)K hours of high-quality audio-only data (together less than 1.1% of pretraining), yet achieves net win rates of +24.9 to +43.0 across all subjective dimensions — the contrast of "small and refined overwhelming large and messy" is extremely vivid. ④ The paper's conclusion summarizes this recipe directly: "We focus on curating high-quality data at scale for pre-training, and curating relatively smaller but higher-quality data for fine-tuning. This general recipe is effective for improving the generation quality of images, videos, and audio." ⑤ At the same time, the paper also emphasizes the necessity of scale — "scaling data, training compute, and model size simultaneously brings significant gains" — so its stance is "first lay a foundation with large-scale, strictly filtered data + then set the final tone with an extremely small-scale, manually curated dataset," rather than a purely small-data route.

### [NVIDIA NeMo Curator](../models/NeMo_Curator.md) ⚠️

There is no direct "small and refined beats large and messy" controlled experiment. But the design philosophy of the entire pipeline is itself an engineering expression of this claim, with clear quantitative anchors: starting from 20 million hours of raw video, it passes through seven gates — shot splitting (discarding <2 seconds), motion filtering (removing static and uncontrolled shaky footage), image-quality filtering (DOVER removes the bottom 15%), aesthetic filtering (threshold 3.5, with a strict default min-aggregation strategy), text-overlay filtering, type-taxonomy filtering (wholesale removal of animation/games/abstract patterns), and semantic deduplication (removing about 30%) — ultimately only about 10^7-scale clips enter the fine-tuning stage.
The Cosmos paper's statement of the curation objective is itself a declaration of quality-first: the goal is to "locate segments in the video that have rich dynamics and high visual quality, conducive to learning the physical laws encoded in visual content." In addition, the strategy of "upsampling human action/object manipulation, downsampling scenery" is also a typical judgment that information density takes priority over material quantity. But all of this is design intent and expert judgment, without A/B controlled quantitative evidence provided. [Uncertain]

### [OmniHuman Dataset + OHBench](../models/OmniHuman.md) ⚠️

This work does not directly argue that "small and refined beats large and messy," but it provides two pieces of indirect evidence:
[Evidence 1: A 20% subset produces across-the-board improvement] Fine-tuning used only one-fifth of the dataset (200,000 / 1,000,000), yet achieved improvement on all metrics of OHBench, with improvements of 11%–26% on audio and alignment dimensions. Had the paper intended it, this could have served as direct evidence for "the efficiency of curated data," but the paper does not make this claim and does not provide a comparison curve between 20% and 100%, so strictly speaking this is only "a small amount of data is also effective" rather than "a small amount of refined data beats a large amount of messy data." [Uncertain]
[Evidence 2: Improvements are concentrated on dimensions corresponding to fine-grained annotation] The metrics with the largest improvements (KL 25.9%, T-A 25.0%, FD 12.3%, V-A 11.1%) all fall on audio quality and audio-visual alignment — precisely the two directions where OmniHuman invested the most in annotation precision relative to existing datasets; while the improvement on the visual quality dimension is relatively mild. This correspondence indirectly supports the claim that "annotation quality determines the direction of capability gains."
[This dataset's own thesis is not "small and refined"] The paper's diagnosis of the problem is "structural deficiency" (insufficient scene diversity, sparse interaction modeling, insufficient attribute alignment) rather than "the data is too dirty," and its solution is "filling in missing structural dimensions" rather than "cleaning the data more thoroughly." Therefore this work's stance in the quality-quantity debate is closer to "dimensional completeness first" — i.e., it holds that the bottleneck is not how much data there is or how clean it is, but which structural dimensions are covered (human-object interaction, speaker attribution, cinematography, music attributes). This is a third claim orthogonal to "small and refined," and it is also the most distinctive viewpoint of this entry relative to other dataset works.
[Lacking a direct control] There is no controlled experiment of "OmniHuman's 200,000 vs. another dataset's 200,000" at the same scale, so its data's quality advantage over OpenHumanVid, SpeakerVid-5M, etc. is supported only qualitatively by Table 1's capability matrix (whether a certain type of annotation exists or not), with no quantitative evidence. [Uncertain]

### [The Open-Sora Series](../models/Open-Sora.md)

This is the most persuasive overall evidence in the Open-Sora series, despite lacking a rigorous controlled experiment:
1. **Open-Sora 2.0, at $199.6k, with 11B parameters, and using only 5M videos at the HD stage, achieves VBench and human-preference levels comparable to the 11B HunyuanVideo and the 30B Step-Video-T2V**, at a training cost 5–10x lower than comparable models. Its core mechanism is precisely a "layered data pyramid + graded tagging + progressive curriculum" — spending most of the compute on low-resolution, coarsely filtered data, and using only the most strictly filtered 5M data (7% of the base layer's 70M) for the HD finishing stage.
2. **Open-Sora Plan v1.3 cuts Panda70M down to a 27% retention rate** (or 42% per the paper's funnel table) and then significantly improves quality with only 30k steps of fine-tuning; and v1.5, using 40M videos and an 8B model, reaches a level comparable to HunyuanVideo — likewise a route of "screen hard, train little."
3. **Open-Sora 1.2** screens 30M clips / 80k hours down to 2M clips / 5k hours (about 6.7%) as HD-stage data.
Across the three versions, the final high-quality subset is consistently controlled to about **7% of the original pool**, and this ratio is an empirical value that converges across projects and versions — this in itself is strong empirical evidence for "quality over quantity." Counter-annotation: it lacks a control of "what would happen if 5M random, unfiltered data were used for stage three," so strictly speaking this is **engineering-practice evidence rather than scientific evidence**.

### [Ovi](../models/Ovi.md) ⚠️

There is an explicit qualitative claim, but no comparative experimental figures for "a small, refined dataset beats a large, messy dataset."
[Positive evidence (author's own statement)] The one direct statement appears at the synchronization-filtering step: "even a small quantity of out-of-sync data can impede lip-sync abilities, so strict criteria were chosen to minimize mismatch risk." This statement expresses the typical quality-first principle — willing to substantially reduce the amount of usable data rather than tolerate a small amount of dirty data contaminating synchronization ability. The design orientation of the entire pipeline (a hard threshold of >720×720, RAFT static-shot removal, aesthetic removal, strict dual SyncNet thresholds) is consistent with this.
[Counter-evidence (the cost)] Quality-first is not free: the paper acknowledges that after joint training, video quality slightly degrades relative to the Wan2.2 base model, precisely because "the audio-video paired dataset is narrower than Wan2.2's large-scale pretraining corpus" — i.e., the high purity gained through strict screening comes at the cost of insufficient coverage breadth. The authors consider this trade-off marginal and acceptable.
[Evidence that scale is still pursued] One of Ovi 1.1's main improvements is precisely that "the dataset includes 100% more videos for greater diversity," showing that the team still primarily uses expansion of volume as the main improvement lever while maintaining the quality bar — quality is the threshold, quantity is the lever.
[No quantitative control] No parallel training results are provided for "a strictly filtered subset vs. a loosely filtered large set." [Uncertain]

### [Script-a-Video](../models/Script-a-Video.md) ⚠️

What this work provides is not traditional "small data beats large data" evidence, but a more fundamental variant — "the same data, organized differently, produces vastly better results":
[Core evidence: zero-cost format gains] LTX-2-AV and LTX-2-AV-MTSS use exactly the same architecture, exactly the same training data, and the same training paradigm; the only difference is the structural organization of the conditioning text, yet this brings +45% human-rated identity consistency, +56% audio-video alignment, +71% multi-shot controllability, and an 85% reduction in WER. This shows that, with data volume and compute held constant, the "representational quality" of annotation is itself an independent and powerfully effective performance dimension. For data-side teams, the practical implication of this finding is: before considering expanding data scale, examining the degree of structuring in existing annotation may be a higher-return investment.
[Second piece of evidence: structuring boosts small models' data efficiency] The paper positions "Learnability" as the second major selling point of Figure 1 — MTSS substantially narrows the gap between small and large models. Qwen3-Omni improves from 0.1806 to 0.4117 on Daily-Omni (+127%), a far larger increase than Gemini-2.5-Pro's 0.6825→0.7568 (+11%). That is, structured captions benefit weaker-capability models more; the mechanism is shifting the burden of "disentangling relationships" from the model to the data representation. This is essentially "trading annotation structure for model capacity," directly valuable to compute-constrained teams.
[Third piece of evidence: the efficiency of 500K distillation data] Using only 500K Gemini-annotated data for a single simple SFT round reduces Qwen3-Omni's Video-SALMONN-2 overall error rate from 0.5853 to 0.3913, surpassing the teacher model Gemini-2.5-Pro's native performance (0.3959). A student surpassing its teacher on a specific format task shows that the return on format-specialized data is extremely high.
[Fourth piece of evidence: an annealing-style high-quality small set] The final stage on the generation side uses 60K "high-fidelity" data (only 1/14.5 of stage 3's 870K) + 15K steps to complete the final coordination — a typical small-scale, high-quality finishing touch, but the paper does not conduct a controlled experiment with/without this stage. [Uncertain]
[Limitation] The paper does not conduct a data-scale scaling experiment (e.g., comparing 100K vs. 500K), so it cannot answer the practical question of "how much MTSS data is needed to be enough."

### [Seedance 2.0 and Seedance 1.5 pro](../models/Seedance_20_Seedance_15_pro.md) ⚠️

There is no direct "small and refined beats large and messy" controlled experiment. It is indirectly reflected in the overall methodological orientation: Seedance 1.0's funnel centers on strict quality and safety filtering + semantic deduplication (keeping only the single instance with the highest quality score in each near-duplicate cluster) + distribution rebalancing; the CT stage actively shrinks to a subset with higher aesthetics and richer motion; the SFT stage further converges to a manually verified set of a few hundred curated categories, emphasizing early stopping to prevent overfitting and preserve text controllability — the whole chain shows a progressive design of "trading quantity for quality." Seedance 1.5 pro sets the priority goals of the data pipeline as consistency, motion expressiveness, and curriculum scheduling rather than scale. [Uncertain: lacks quantitative evidence]

### [The SkyReels Series](../models/SkyReels.md) ⚠️

The SkyReels series provides two fairly clear pieces of "better none than bad" evidence:
(1) Concept balancing cuts the data in half: SkyReels-V2, during post-training, performs concept balancing by subject category, explicitly stating this "resulted in a 50% reduction in data volume," and the team still chose to execute it, reasoning that it eliminates head-category bias and improves generalization — trading half the data for distributional balance, a typical case of quality/distribution taking priority over quantity;
(2) 20% retention for the tagging set: SkyCaptioner-V1's training set was curated from 10 million down to about 2 million (about 20% retention) to ensure concept balance and annotation quality; and using a small 7B model on professional fields surpasses the 72B general-purpose model, showing that the value of data quality (sub-expert annotation) exceeds that of model scale;
(3) SkyReels-V4's pyramid curriculum: converging step by step from 3 billion images down to a final 1 million manually curated videos for the final SFT, with the final image quality determined by these 1 million — again embodying the idea of "large scale builds the prior, small scale sets the ceiling."
But neither generation's paper conducts a "small and refined vs. large and messy" controlled experiment; the above are all strategic evidence rather than ablation evidence. [Uncertain]

### [Sora 2](../models/Sora_2.md) ⚠️

Completely undisclosed. There is no experimental evidence or discussion whatsoever of "small and refined data beating large and messy data." The System Card contains only the qualitative statement "rigorous filtering to maintain data quality," implying a stance that values data quality, but with no supporting evidence at all. [Uncertain]

### [SpeakerVid-5M](../models/SpeakerVid-5M.md) ⚠️

The paper provides no direct quantitative evidence for "small and refined beats large and messy," but its data design implicitly adopts this philosophy in practice:
[Implicit practice] The 571K clips / 1,368-hour HQ subset is cut out separately from the 8,743 hours and used specifically for the dialogue fine-tuning of the third stage, while pretraining uses only the remaining 7,375 hours — this "mutually exclusive partition" design itself embodies the judgment that "high-quality data should be reserved for the training stage closest to the final capability." The HQ thresholds are several times stricter than the full-volume thresholds on every dimension (blur score 0.1 → 0.5/0.7, DOVER 0.25 → 0.6, a newly added motion-score lower bound), showing that the team considers data quality more critical than quantity at the post-training stage.
[Missing validation] There is no controlled experiment of "HQ subset trained alone vs. full-volume training," so the question of whether 1,368 hours of curated data can surpass 8,743 hours of the full volume cannot be answered. All the ablations in Table 2 are architectural-component ablations, unrelated to data volume.
[Indirect corroboration from end-to-end retention rate] Screening from 64,386 hours of raw material down to 8,743 hours (about 13.6%), then further down to 1,368 HQ hours (about 2.1%) — this nearly fiftyfold convergence ratio is quite aggressive among similar datasets, reflecting the team's leaning in the quality trade-off. But aggressive filtering by itself does not equal evidence that "quality beats quantity."
[Comparison with similar work] MOVA, through a three-stage scale reduction (61.5k → 37.6k → 11k hours) paired with metric improvements at each stage, provides curriculum-level evidence of increasing quality; SpeakerVid-5M has a design of the same form but does not provide the corresponding quantitative validation. [Uncertain]

### [Step-Video-T2V](../models/Step-Video-T2V.md) ⚠️

There is no rigorous controlled experiment, but there is one fairly strong piece of indirect evidence and one clear counter-example:
[Positive indirect evidence] The data volume monotonically shrinks from the pipeline's output of 2B down to SFT's 30M (about 1.5%), together with the team's observed phenomenon that "loss drops sharply when training data quality improves," constituting empirical support for "small, refined data in the later stage brings significant gains." The overall design rationale of the four-step cascaded training is itself "making full use of video datasets of different quality" — using large-scale low-quality data at the early, low-resolution stage to establish a diversity prior, and using small-scale high-quality data at the later, high-resolution stage for polishing, which is "quality and quantity each playing their own role" rather than simple quality-first.
[Counter-example (more valuable)] The derivative model Step-Video-TI2V officially states that anime-style videos account for over 80% of the training data, and that only anime data was used in the initial stage; the result was that anime-scene performance benefited significantly while real-world-scene capability was constrained. This is an officially acknowledged qualitative case where "data mixture imbalance directly causes lopsided capability," providing counter-evidence for the decisive role of data distribution (rather than pure data volume) in shaping the final capability boundary.
[Gap] There is no controlled comparison experiment with "the same model, the same compute, different data tiers," so strictly speaking this does not constitute experimental proof. [Uncertain]

### [UniTalking](../models/UniTalking.md) ⚠️

The paper provides no direct evidence of the quality-quantity trade-off and conducts no related controlled experiment: no data-scale scaling curve, no "small and refined vs. large and messy" control, no analysis of the relationship between filtering strictness and effectiveness.
[Observable orientation: an explicit quality-first stance] The entire pipeline is chained from six hard Boolean elimination rules (static / text overlay / low image quality / silent / no speech / low SNR / off-screen sound source / lips out of sync), none of which is soft-weighted or tiered retention — i.e., any sample that fails a criterion is discarded outright, with no degraded usage. This stands in sharp contrast to UniVerse-1: the latter degrades no-speech samples to "general data" and retains them, and isolates low-image-quality VGGSound/AudioSet with an LQLS loss and continues to use it — a typical "don't waste anything" orientation; UniTalking, by contrast, is purely "better none than bad."
[But the team itself does not believe the data is refined enough] Future Work explicitly states: "the performance of our model is limited by the available training resources and data scale, especially compared to closed-source models" — i.e., the team lists data scale as a performance bottleneck, believing more data is needed rather than more refined data. This self-assessment is in tension with the "quality-first" pipeline design, and can be interpreted as: under its own self-built filtering criteria, only 2.3 million qualifying data points could be found, and scale has become a constraint rather than an actively chosen small-and-refined approach.
[A piece of indirect empirical "quality-first" evidence] The only ablation (omitting stage one causes significant audio-quality degradation) demonstrates the "quality of the training curriculum" rather than the "quality of the data," and so does not count as evidence for this field.
[A piece of counter-evidence] The paper acknowledges that attention maps show "mismatched highlighted regions that vary randomly across training iterations," and speculates that one cause is "data noise" — the only place in the paper that acknowledges residual data noise, showing that even after six rounds of filtering, the 2.3 million data points still contain observably impactful noise. But the paper does not further analyze the type of noise or propose a countermeasure.
[Conclusion] UniTalking's data strategy is "high threshold, pure elimination, single domain," which is quality-first in orientation, but there is no evidence supporting that this orientation is superior to alternatives, and the team itself believes scale remains the main bottleneck. [Uncertain]

### [UniVerse-1](../models/UniVerse-1.md) ⚠️

UniVerse-1 provides evidence pointing in the opposite direction — it embodies "a small base model + a moderate amount of data can also produce usable joint generation," but its internal evidence actually supports "low-quality data need not be discarded — isolating its use is enough to add value," which is inconsistent with the classic "small and refined beats large and messy" narrative:
[Evidence supporting quality-first]
- The entry filtering threshold is set aggressively (≥1080p, bitrate ratio ≥600, DOVER≥0.6); even at a scale that yields only 7.6k hours of final output, the team still insists on high admission standards, showing that in the quality-quantity trade-off the team leans toward quality;
- The speech subset, after triple serial verification ("has an audio track + has a face + SyncNet"), is left with only 1,187 hours, 15.4% of the total — a typical high-cost fine screening;
- The SoE paradigm itself is the idea of "replacing large-scale training from scratch with a high-quality pretrained expert" — at 7,685 hours, it is two orders of magnitude below the million-hour scale that would be needed to train a video generation model from scratch, relying on base-model quality rather than data quantity.
[Evidence pointing in the opposite direction (more noteworthy)] The LQLS ablation precisely proves the opposite: the 44.5% of low-quality data (VGGSound/AudioSet) should not be discarded, but rather safely utilized by limiting its scope of influence in the diffusion process — keeping it and applying LQLS raises AS from 0.44 to 0.47 and ID from 0.78 to 0.89. This points to a data-utilization paradigm different from "filtering means discarding": turning the binary decision of "use it or not" into the continuous decision of "which noise segment to use it in." In scenarios where total data is limited, this may be more valuable than aggressive filtering.
[Evidence not provided] The paper does not conduct a direct controlled experiment of "small-scale finely screened data vs. large-scale coarsely screened data," nor does it provide a data-scale scaling curve, so no quantitative conclusion on the quality-quantity trade-off can be given. In fact, the Limitations section lists both "larger scale" and "more refined data curation" as future directions, i.e., it considers there is still room for improvement in both. [Uncertain]

### [Unison](../models/Unison.md) ⚠️

Unison provides no direct experimental evidence for "small and refined data beats large and messy data" — it conducts no data-scale or data-quality controlled experiment at all. But it provides a different kind of evidence, worth discussing separately:
[What the paper actually claims is "architectural efficiency beats parameter and data scale," not "data quality beats quantity"] The paper's core efficiency argument is: with a 5B video backbone (nearly 4x smaller than LTX-2's 19B), it achieves better cross-modal synchronization (DS 0.08 vs. 0.10, AV 0.91 vs. 0.89, PQ 6.34 vs. 6.30, CU 5.61 vs. 5.58, WER 0.22 vs. 0.25), and explicitly summarizes this as "our architectural innovations effectively capture high-fidelity audiovisual associations without the need for massive parameter scaling." This is a parameter-efficiency argument, not a data-efficiency argument — the paper never compares its own 3,000 hours with LTX-2's corpus scale (the latter's scale is not disclosed in this paper), so a data-side quality-first conclusion cannot be drawn from this.
[Observation indirectly supporting "curated data is enough"] The 3,000+ hours of audio-video corpus is at the small end among comparable works (0.4x of UniVerse-1, 1/20 of MOVA Phase 1), yet achieves SOTA or near-SOTA on multiple metrics. But the attribution of this result is highly entangled: the frozen video backbone means visual capability comes from Wan2.2's massive pretraining, 130,000 hours of audio ensures acoustic capability, and the 3,000 hours of audio-video only needs to handle the relatively lightweight task of "learning to align." Therefore the true meaning of "small data can also work well" here is "alignment itself doesn't require much data," not "curated data can replace large-scale data."
[Evidence pointing in the opposite direction: the audio side takes the scale route] An internal tension worth pointing out — Unison piles up 130,000 hours / 50 million segments on the audio side, a fairly thorough scale route, with no quality-screening explanation given (covered only by the word "high-quality"). If the team truly believed in quality-first, the audio side should have been the part with the most conditions for fine screening (audio-quality assessment tools are mature and cheap, and Audiobox-Aesthetics is literally in their hands for evaluation). What was actually done is a three-part approach where "audio pursues quantity, audio-video pursues alignment, vision relies on inheritance" — the data philosophy differs across all three.
[Conclusion] There is no reliable evidence for this field in Unison. The paper conducts neither a data-scale scaling curve nor a fine-screening vs. coarse-screening comparison, so any inference of "quality beats quantity" lacks support. [Uncertain]

### [Veo 3 / Veo 3.1](../models/Veo_3_Veo_31.md) ⚠️

[Uncertain] No controlled evidence of quality vs. quantity whatsoever. The official materials only broadly state that the training data is "large" and quality-filtered, with no case or ablation conclusion showing small, refined data beating large, messy data.

### [Vidu S1](../models/Vidu_S1.md)

There is no direct quantitative evidence, but the overall design route is clearly "quality and distribution purity take priority over quantity": data scale is not mentioned at all, while the filtering conditions are extremely strict (single person, single shot, static or slow camera motion, must have clear action, no watermarks/subtitles/ads, no overlapping voices, no segments with low speech-energy proportion, 3–60 seconds), and the final data distribution is highly vertical. The paper's qualitative claim is that "training data quality substantially affects training performance and model generalization ability," and it emphasizes that strict filtering rules are the means of ensuring training data quality. Indirect circumstantial evidence: as a 540p real-time model on HDTF, Vidu S1 comprehensively surpasses offline open-source models such as Wan2.2-S2V-14B, OmniAvatar, Hallo3, and StableAvatar-1.3B, as well as commercial systems such as HeyGen, LemonSlice, and Kling Avatar 2.0, on all three metrics CSIM (0.9192), Sync-D (7.8470), and DOVER (0.5660).

### [Wan 2.5 / 2.6 / 2.7](../models/Wan.md) ⚠️

There are several methodological manifestations and one quasi-quantitative piece of evidence, but a direct "small and refined vs. large and messy" controlled experiment is lacking.
[Quasi-quantitative evidence (the strongest)] Synthetic-image contamination as low as <10% significantly degrades model performance — this directly shows that the marginal impact of data purity far exceeds that of data volume, a typical case of "a small amount of dirty data can destroy the benefit of a large amount of good data," and the most citable statement in this entry.
[Methodological manifestations]
1) The base-dimension filter eliminates about 50% of candidate data in one step, willing to cut the data in half to first ensure basic quality;
2) Post-training uses only images with an expert score in the top 20% and videos screened out by the quality classifier at the top; the scale plunges from the billions down to the millions;
3) V2A is strictly filtered from a video parent set of billions down to O(1) thousand hours — accepting extreme scale compression for the sake of audio-visual usability;
4) Personalization data is screened from O(100)M down to O(10)M;
5) The final stage of caption-model training is dedicated end-to-end training on "small-scale, high-quality data," and manually annotated captions are defined as the highest-quality data, used only in the final stage.
[Counterbalance] It should be noted that Wan simultaneously cares a great deal about "not losing distribution for the sake of quality" — 100-cluster quota sampling to prevent long-tail loss, downsampling (rather than deleting) static and camera-motion categories, and post-training's emphasis on category balance and manually filling in missing concepts are all hedges against "one-sidedly pursuing refinement causing distributional collapse." This dual orientation of "high standards + preserved distribution" is more worth learning from than pure quality-first alone.
No "full volume vs. curated" controlled experiment or quantified benefit is given. [Uncertain]

### [Audio-Video Generation Evaluation Benchmark Collection](../models/av_benchmarks.md)

[PhyAVBench is the strongest evidence] Using only 337 paired prompts and 11,605 carefully recorded videos (25.5 hours, an extremely small scale), it pushed the CPRS of all 17 SOTA models below 0.45, exposing the systematic failure of commercial large models trained on massive web data at basic audio physics. Its core methodological claim is "newly recorded to guarantee zero overlap with any training set" — emphasizing that data purity and controllability take priority over scale, and it explicitly requires at least N≥20 real samples per pair to average out and suppress noise, embodying the idea of "small and refined + repeated sampling."
[AVBench's 30K→300K augmentation approach] Rather than collecting more data, it applies controlled degradation to augment 30K high-quality real clips into 300K training pairs, using the diversity of synthetic negatives to substitute for the scale of real data — a quality-first route of "a small seed + controllable synthesis."
[AV-SyncBench] 3,269 clean videos, initially screened by Gemini and then cross-checked by 5 people, are expanded into 38,390 evaluation samples; its strict requirement for on-screen sound sources means a large amount of raw data is eliminated, in exchange for absolute label reliability.
[VABench] 1,299 samples covering 15 evaluation dimensions, winning through the structured design of its taxonomy rather than sample volume.
[Omni-Judge] With only 300 prompts / 600 videos, it reaches a conclusion strong enough to change data-pipeline design (Omni-LLM cannot be used for perceptual-type scoring).
Shared implication: the "small and refined" paradigm on the evaluation side has already been validated; mapped onto the training side, investment in domain coverage and label reliability may yield a higher return than simply expanding data volume.

### [Video Captioning Model Ecosystem](../models/caption_models.md)

[Evidence of quality beating quantity within the captioner ecosystem]
· ShareGPT4Video is the most classic example: using only 40K dense GPT-4V captions for SFT, it trained ShareCaptioner-Video, which is capable of annotating 4.8 million videos — an extremely small, high-quality seed set leveraging a hundredfold-scale output.
· AVSCap's core argument: "the gain from RL exceeds that of scaling up SFT data volume" — i.e., at a 130K data scale, investing in reward design is more cost-effective than continuing to expand annotation volume.
· Script-a-Video: after fine-tuning on 500K Gemini-2.5-Pro finely annotated data points, 8B/30B-class open-source models already slightly outperform the teacher Gemini-2.5-Pro's native output on video-SALMONN-2's overall error rate (0.3913 vs. 0.3959).
· SkyCaptioner-V1: a 7B model trained on only 2 million concept-balanced data points has professional-field accuracy (average 76.3%, shot type 93.7%) significantly exceeding general-purpose models 10x larger, such as Qwen2.5-VL-72B — direct evidence of "small model + matched data > large model + general data."
[Evidence of quality beating quantity at the downstream dataset level]
· Koala-36M (36 million) vs. Panda-70M (70 million): only half the scale, yet the highest VBench overall score, with subject consistency +1.1% and background consistency +2.4%. The core difference lies in caption length (202.3 words vs. 13.2 words) and splitting accuracy — this is the most direct "small and refined beats large and messy" case in this ecosystem.
· CineDance-1M's artifact non-compliance rate is 2.8% vs. Koala-36M's 37.4% (a 13.4x improvement), representing a further tightening of quality by the newer generation.
· JavisDiT++'s statement is worth noting: not simply "quality beats quantity," but "ensuring good data quality is the prerequisite foundation for increasing sample quantity to improve training effectiveness" — i.e., when quality is substandard, simply scaling up is ineffective or even harmful.
· Movie Gen audio SFT vs. PT: overall +41.7±15.3, professionalism +43.0±14.7, with the paper's comment "highlighting the importance of high-quality data curation at the fine-tuning stage."
[Counter-warning] CogVideoX reports a two-sided effect: removing subtitles and watermarks slightly improves visual quality, but semantic capability slightly degrades — over-cleaning can cause a loss of semantic diversity, a boundary condition of "quality-first."

### [Geometric/Structured Annotation Dataset Collection](../models/geometric_datasets.md)

SpatialVID provides the most direct evidence chain: the 21,789 hours of targeted material actively retrieved using motion keywords has usability far exceeding that of the passively crawled general-purpose large library — Panda-70M is much larger in scale, but over 80% of it fails to complete geometric reconstruction, showing that for geometric-annotation tasks, "large and messy" is nearly useless; further, the 0.37M SpatialVID-HQ subset, tightened along the three dimensions of aesthetics/brightness/motion plus category balancing, is positioned as a better training choice than the full 2.71M volume. SceneScribe-1M's evidence is in its scale-comparison table: its volume of 1 million clips / 156.7M frames is smaller than Koala-36M's (36 million videos) and SpatialVID's (about 2 million clips / 123.6M frames), yet by virtue of its complete annotation combination of "camera + depth + 3D point trajectory + text," it brings gains across six downstream tasks, embodying the substitutability of annotation depth for sample quantity. WildWorld, using about 1,800 hours of single-game-domain data, leverages 119 columns of frame-level precise ground truth to nearly double SkelCtrl's action-following performance — an extreme case of trading annotation precision for data breadth. Action100M conversely shows that scale still has value, but its deduplication (141.8 million duplicate instances) and semantic resampling are precisely operations that "turn big data into refined data."

### [Video Generation Post-Training Data Strategy](../models/post_training_data.md) ⚠️

This topic is the most concentrated band of evidence for the "quality over quantity" thesis in the video generation domain:
[The strongest quantitative evidence]
· NAVA: from 15M training corpus, through coordinated multi-operator filtering, 160K (1.07%) is taken for SFT, and this subset's captions are regenerated by the more expensive Gemini-3-Pro — "one-in-a-thousand curation + upgraded annotation" is the most aggressive quality-first practice in this survey;
· Allegro: 500M → 2M (0.4%);
· Movie Gen: video SFT training uses only 512 H100s, an extremely small investment relative to its pretraining scale, yet it bears the final calibration of aesthetics and cinematic feel;
· Cosmos-Predict 2.5: SFT on 730K robot-manipulation data points brings a crushing 72.6% vs. 8.3% human win rate — direct empirical proof that "a small dataset in the target domain can beat a large, messy pretraining set";
· AVSCap's argument that "RL gain > expanding SFT data volume" advances the thesis from "small, refined data" to "optimization paradigm > data scale";
· ALIVE's SFT trains only 0.5 epoch — a counter-footnote that "curated data, too, should not be trained to full convergence"; quality-first does not mean letting the model fully fit the curated distribution.
[The anchor paper's stance] Its core argument, "SFT as the foundation for RLHF," is actually a more advanced version of the quality-quantity thesis: SFT's value lies not in directly improving subjective quality (the paper explicitly states "SFT is not intended to fully solve alignment or optimize subjective quality"), but in providing a stable reference policy and "expanding RLHF's exploration space." In other words, the ultimate value of SFT's curated data can only be realized through RLHF — this repositions "small and refined data" from an endpoint to middleware. This is the most notable shift in perspective in this topic, though the paper provides no ablation evidence for it [Uncertain].
[Counter-evidence/caution] Apollo explicitly uses manually curated high-quality data for its Stage III quality refinement but performs no preference alignment at all, with all of its alignment capability coming from data quality and architecture; Step-Video-T2V points out that DPO's benefit saturates once the model can easily distinguish positive from negative samples — together these show that "data quality" and "preference optimization" have differently shaped benefit curves: the former's marginal returns diminish earlier, while the latter requires continuously updated reward signals to sustain its gradient.

### [Merged Survey of Mainstream Video Pretraining Datasets: Panda-70M, InternVid, Koala…](../models/pretraining_datasets.md)

**This survey has accumulated a horizontal evidence chain across seven datasets on "quality over quantity," of a strength far exceeding any single model report**:
[The strongest single-point evidence · Koala-36M] **Sharing the same source and architecture and compute as Panda-70M, with only half the data volume (36M vs. 70M), VBench overall score is 9.67 points higher and semantic score is 28.2 points higher.** This is the most tightly controlled comparison — the same batch of raw HD-VILA-100M material, differing **only in splitting method, caption quality, and filtering strategy**, ruling out confounding from differences in data source. **This may be the cleanest "same source data, different processing" controlled experiment in the entire industry.**
[The most extreme ratio evidence · UltraVideo] **42K clips / 62 hours**, about 2,700x smaller than Koala-36M by hour count, yet in human blind evaluation its bad-video rate is **2.3% vs. Koala-36M's 41.5%**, and its UltraWan-1K overwhelms the official Wan on video aesthetics at **81.10% vs. 18.90%**. This shows that, on a specific capability dimension (high resolution, high image quality), **data volume can yield to data purity by three orders of magnitude**.
[The most extreme funnel evidence · LVD-2M and MiraData] LVD-2M goes **220M → 2M (a 0.91% retention rate)**, and MiraData filters HD-VILA-100M's **100 million clips down to 195,000 (about 0.2%)**. Both independently show that: **the cost of the constraint set "long shots + high dynamics + no cuts" is 2–3 orders of magnitude of data loss**, in exchange for a human-evaluated long-shot purity rate of 77.5% (vs. Panda-70M's 50.0%, InternVid's 47.5%) and a "highly dynamic" proportion of 30.0% (vs. Panda-70M's 7.5%).
[The most direct counter-evidence · Panda-70M's self-assessment] Its desirability annotations added in October 2024 show that, even by its own standards, **about 19.5% of the full volume of samples are still undesirable** (low desirability score 5.28%, static foreground 6.82%, tiny camera motion 1.20%, picture-in-picture 5.03%, screen recording 1.13%). Combined with LVD-2M's human evaluation showing that **50% of its clips contain shot cuts and 25% are judged "not dynamic,"** the actually usable proportion of Panda-70M may be less than half — **this explains why VidGen-1M, OpenVid-1M, and Koala-36M — three subsequent works — were all motivated by "refining/redoing Panda-70M."**
[Quality beats quantity on the caption dimension · MiraData] At the same data volume, simply switching captions from short to structured **nearly doubles text alignment (7.73→15.36), increases dynamics by +107%, and improves temporal consistency by +149%, while image quality stays flat** — showing that "caption quality" is a dimension for improving quality that is orthogonal to "video quality," and at far lower cost than re-screening video.
[An important counter-annotation] **Resolution is the one dimension that cannot be obtained through screening alone.** UltraVideo's comparison shows that pushing Wan-T2V-1.3B directly to 1K causes severe collapse (scene score 0.00), and OpenVid's comparison shows that native 1080p (VQAA 73.46) is significantly better than low-resolution + 4x super-resolution (69.26/63.25). **That is, within "quality," image quality, dynamics, and semantics can be obtained through screening and re-annotation, but pixel-level resolution must come from the source capture** — this is the one shortfall among the seven that cannot be remedied by cleaning alone, and it is also the entire reason UltraVideo exists.
[Methodological caveat] Except for Koala-36M, most of the above are **cross-dataset comparisons** rather than strict "same data, different processing" controls, with confounding factors from differences in data source; and evaluation protocols differ across works (VBench subset, prompt length, model scale all vary), so **figures across papers cannot be directly compared horizontally**.
## Alignment between the domain distribution of training data and the taxonomy of evaluation benchmarks (e.g., VABench's seven categories)

`benchmark_taxonomy_alignment` · Detail level: brief

### [Allegro](../models/Allegro.md) ⚠️

No explicit alignment relationship has been established between the domain distribution of the training data and the taxonomy system of the evaluation benchmark. Evaluation uses the general-purpose VBench (946 prompts, 4,730 generated videos) and a self-built 46-prompt user study (six dimensions: video-text relevance, appearance distortion, appearance aesthetics, motion naturalness, motion magnitude, and overall quality).
There is no mapping or dimension-targeted data supplementation between the training-side category statistics (dominated by people / objects / landscapes under Tag2Text labels) and VBench's sixteen-dimension taxonomy; the paper explicitly attributes the category distribution to the natural composition of the dataset rather than to a deliberate alignment design. The six dimensions of the user study have a naive correspondence with the data filtering metrics (aesthetics ↔ LAION Aesthetics, motion magnitude ↔ UniMatch, appearance distortion ↔ DOVER), but the paper does not formalize this correspondence as a design principle. [Partially uncertain]

### [Apollo](../models/Apollo.md) ⚠️

【Benchmark used】Follows the third-party benchmark Verse-Bench to evaluate the T2AV task (paper's original text: "Following Universe-1, we use Verse-Bench for T2AV tasks"); no self-built evaluation benchmark. Verse-Bench is the audio-video joint generation evaluation benchmark proposed by Universe-1.
【Evaluation metric system】Video quality is measured with Motion Score (RAFT optical flow) and Aesthetic Score (MANIQA, aesthetic-predictor-v2-5, Musiq); audio quality with FD (Fréchet distance on PANNs mel spectrograms), KL divergence, and MOS; synchronization with AV-A / DeSync (Synchformer) and SyncNet Confidence; cross-modal semantics with CLAP and ImageBind score; speech content with WER. Table 1, "Main comparisons of audio-visual joint generation," compares cascaded schemes against joint-generation schemes across a total of 10 metrics; the paper claims to reach a level comparable to Veo 3.
【Alignment with the training data distribution】There is a clear correspondence that the paper never states explicitly: the four training-data splits (single-speaker speech / multi-speaker speech / singing / natural sound) map onto the four evaluated capability groups (video / audio / TTS / audio-video consistency, see Figure 5) — a "data category → capability category → metric group" chain: the sound split corresponds to the audio group of metrics (FD/KL/CLAP), the speech and singing splits correspond to the TTS group of metrics (WER/Sync-conf), and the whole corresponds to the consistency group (DeSync/IB). Stage II's metric-driven rebalancing mechanism is built precisely on top of this mapping chain: a weak score on one metric group triggers the addition of the corresponding data category. This is actually one of the relatively rare cases in this survey where "the data taxonomy and the evaluation taxonomy form an explicit closed loop."
【Gap】The paper does not give an enumeration of Verse-Bench's categories (compare with VABench's seven major categories), nor does it report scores per category, so it is impossible to verify whether the training data distribution matches the benchmark categories in coverage, or whether there are category blind spots. [Uncertain]

### [CineDance / CineDance-1M](../models/CineDance.md)

There is partial correspondence, but not a strict one-to-one alignment, between the training data taxonomy and the category system of the CineBench evaluation benchmark:
【Training-side taxonomy (8 dimensions)】Genre, Format, Region, Modality, Story Logic, Era, Tone, Audience.
【CineBench's stratification axes (3 axes)】
  · Theme/Style — corresponds to the training-side dimensions such as Genre/Tone/Era;
  · Duration/Shot Count — split into three tiers: 10s / 2–3 shots, 30s / 4–9 shots, 60s / 10–20 shots, directly corresponding to the core differentiating dimension of the training data, and also strictly aligned with the model's two-stage curriculum (10–12s/2–3 shots → 30s/8 shots);
  · Difficulty — within each tier, split into Easy/Medium/Hard by tertiles, with a difficulty formula D = n_char + 1.5·n_scene + 0.4·log(1 + n_spk·L_ASR), where n_char is the number of characters, n_scene the number of scenes, n_spk the number of speakers, and L_ASR the length of the dialogue lines — every term in this formula is taken directly from fields in the training-data annotation schema (the global character table, the global scene table, the ASR-binding results), making this the point of tightest coupling between the data schema and the evaluation system.
【Correspondence between the six evaluation dimensions and the data dimensions】
  1) Video Quality (Aesthetic Quality, MUSIQ imaging quality, AMT motion smoothness) — corresponds to the data-side DOVER/AMT;
  2) Audio Quality (AudioBox-Aesthetics' PQ/CE/CU, Whisper-large-v3's WER/CER) — corresponds to the data-side DNSMOS and ASR annotation;
  3) AV Sync (Sync-C/Sync-D, ImageBind IB-Score) — corresponds to the data-side SyncNet/ImageBind;
  4) Prompt Alignment (ViCLIP at shot level, VideoScore-v1.1 at video level, IB-A Score at audio level);
  5) Narrative Continuity (identity continuity uses ArcFace clustering against the ⟨charₖ⟩ token; scene continuity uses DINOv2 cosine similarity against shots sharing a ⟨sceneₖ⟩) — directly consumes the anchor tokens in the data annotation;
  6) Shot Structure Response, SSR = S_cnt^0.35 · S_seg^0.65, where S_cnt = min(N,M)/max(N,M) (shot-count matching) and S_seg is bidirectional temporal IoU (shot-boundary-position matching) — tests whether the model follows the shot structure specified by the prompt.
【Human alignment validation】10 independent evaluators per video, a 5-point Likert scale, double-blind randomized presentation; Spearman rank correlation is used to verify agreement between the automatic metrics and human judgment.
【Overall assessment】CineBench was designed in reverse around CineDance-1M's annotation schema, and the two are extremely tightly coupled — data-side design elements such as anchor tokens, shot attributes, and ASR binding become directly the criteria for evaluation. This is a standout feature of this work's "joint data-evaluation design," but it also means CineBench may not be entirely fair to models trained on other datasets.

### [CogVideoX](../models/CogVideoX.md)

The alignment is weak and passive — CogVideoX adopts an external general-purpose benchmark rather than building its own taxonomy, and there is no domain-mixture strategy on the training-data side to align against:
· Automatic evaluation uses VBench (with quality-irrelevant sub-metrics like color and bitrate removed) as well as the Dynamic Quality proposed by Devil, CLIP4Clip, etc.; the dimensions the paper focuses on — Human Action, Scene, Dynamic Degree, Multiple Objects, Appearance Style, etc. — are those deemed "consistent with human perception," and are presented via a radar chart.
· The training data has no corresponding category-mixture control (no concept clustering, no long-tail resampling, no human-proportion target), so there is no one-to-one correspondence of the "training taxonomy ↔ benchmark taxonomy" kind seen in Movie Gen.
· The only identifiable alignment is a "negative" one: on the data side, three negative tags — Lecture Type, Text Dominated, Noisy Screenshots — actively exclude static talking-head content, text-dominated content, and screen recordings, which is directionally consistent with the evaluation side's emphasis on Dynamic Degree / Human Action — i.e., the data filtering and the evaluation goals point the same way on the dimension of dynamism.
· A four-dimension human evaluation system was built in-house (sensory quality, instruction following, physical simulation, cover-image quality; 100 prompts, 0–5 scale, with the total score capped at 2 if the instruction is not followed), compared against Kling, with CogVideoX-5B winning in the human evaluation; this system has no direct mapping to the training data's category distribution.
· No adoption of a taxonomy such as VABench for joint audio-video generation (the model has no audio dimension).

### [Cosmos-Predict2.5](../models/Cosmos-Predict25.md) ⚠️

### [Data-Juicer 2.0](../models/Data-Juicer.md) ⚠️

【DJ's evaluation-alignment approach: embedding the evaluation benchmark directly into the search loop of the data recipe】This is the most fundamental difference between Sandbox and other works — most teams "first fix the data distribution, then test on benchmarks after training," whereas DJ "uses benchmark scores to determine the data distribution in reverse." On the video side, VBench's 16 metrics are used as the ranking criterion in the Analyze stage — i.e., the data recipe is directly shaped by VBench's taxonomy.
【Benefits and costs of this strong alignment】
  · Benefit: the data distribution and the evaluation criteria are inherently consistent, maximizing evaluation gains — this is the direct reason it tops VBench.
  · Cost: there is a risk of overfitting to the benchmark. If VBench's 16 metrics fail to cover certain real needs (e.g., long-term temporal consistency, multi-shot narrative, audio-visual coordination), the data recipe searched based on its feedback will systematically neglect those dimensions. The paper does not discuss this risk, which is worth methodological attention. DJ's mitigating evidence is cross-architecture transferability (the recipe is effective on both EasyAnimate and T2V-Turbo), but this only demonstrates cross-model generalization, not cross-benchmark generalization.
【Relationship to other benchmarks in this survey】
  · Sandbox's video evaluation uses only VBench, and does not connect to VABench (the seven-category taxonomy), AVBench, AV-SyncBench, or other audio-video benchmarks — consistent with its positioning as a purely visual case study.
  · To migrate the Sandbox methodology to joint audio-video generation, one need only swap the evaluator in the Analyze stage for VABench/AV-SyncBench, etc. — no architectural change is required; but as noted above, candidate operators on the audio-video side (sync detection, audio-track classification, audio perceptual quality) would need to be added first, otherwise there are no candidates to rank during the probing stage.
【Benchmark work from the same team】HumanVBench (CVPR 2026, arXiv:2412.17574) was built by this team based on Data-Juicer, covering four dimensions — emotion perception, person recognition, behavior analysis, and speech-visual alignment — using 20+ operators to build a person-centric video annotation pipeline and a QA-synthesis pipeline with distractor options. Among these, the "speech-visual alignment" category is the only part of the DJ ecosystem that touches audio-video alignment evaluation, and can be regarded as a future interface for extending DJ toward AV. The team also has DetailMaster (ICML 2026, arXiv:2505.16915), which focuses on long-prompt processing for text-to-image.
【Measurability of the data distribution】One implicit advantage of DJ is that, since every operator writes statistics into the sample's stats field, the distribution of any dataset along any dimension can be quantified and plotted as a histogram — this makes an alignment analysis of "training data distribution vs. evaluation benchmark taxonomy" technically executable — it is just that DJ has not officially published such an analysis for video generation.
[Uncertain] No published distribution profile of the final data pool along the dimensions corresponding to VBench's 16 metrics; no cross-alignment analysis against other benchmark taxonomies such as VABench has been done.

### [Foley-Omni](../models/Foley-Omni.md) ⚠️

The organizing dimension of the training data is highly aligned with the taxonomy of the evaluation benchmark, and the alignment axis is "audio component type" rather than visual domain — this is the fundamental difference between Foley-Omni and text-to-video models (which align to VBench/VABench's seven visual categories).
【Alignment relationship】
  · Training-side task groups: TTS / TTA / TTM (three single-task types) → VisualTTS / V2A (video-conditioned single tasks) → V2ST (multi-component joint task)
  · Annotation-side fields: [WORDS] / [AUDIO] / [MUSIC]
  · Verification-side stems: Bandit's three-way separation of speech / effects / music
  · Evaluation-side V2ST-Bench tiers: speech + sound effects (150 items) / speech + music (120 items) / all three present (30 items)
  All four are strictly isomorphic to the same speech-SFX-music three-way classification, forming a closed loop. This full-pipeline alignment ensures that "the evaluation tests exactly what the training data was annotated for," giving strong consistency.
【Correspondence between evaluation metrics and categories】WER corresponds to the [WORDS] speech field, CLAP/IB corresponds to the semantic matching of [AUDIO] sound effects, DeSync corresponds to cross-category temporal alignment, and A/S/T-MOS provide overall subjective supplementation.
【Imbalance within the alignment】Speech appears in 100% of the three V2ST-Bench tiers (150+120+30 all include speech), while the combination of pure sound effects + music without speech is completely absent — the evaluation benchmark's speech-centric tendency is consistent with, and mutually reinforces, the training data's 66% speech proportion imbalance. This means the benchmark lacks coverage of "complete scoring for non-speech scenes" (such as nature documentaries or sound effects + score for action sequences), so the model's capability in this scenario has not been tested.
【Alignment with external benchmarks】Single-task performance is evaluated on general-purpose benchmarks: VGGSound (FD_VGG, the standard V2A benchmark), GRID (WER, the standard lip-sync speech benchmark), ensuring comparability with expert systems. The paper states that on single tasks it "achieves competitive performance with expert systems."
[Uncertain] No cross-alignment analysis has been done against VABench, the official AudioCaps leaderboard, the Kling-Foley evaluation set, or other benchmarks; whether V2ST-Bench is strictly non-overlapping with the training set has also not been clearly stated.

### [HunyuanVideo-Foley](../models/HunyuanVideo-Foley.md) ⚠️

【There is no verifiable alignment relationship between the training-data taxonomy and the evaluation taxonomy】This is the core conclusion for this field, because the training-side taxonomy has never been disclosed:
【Training side】Stage 7 of the pipeline uses an audio classification model to tag categories and manage the distribution, but what the taxonomy is, how many categories it has, and what ontology it is based on (the 527 AudioSet classes? a custom one?) are all completely undisclosed, so it cannot be compared against any evaluation taxonomy. [Uncertain]
【Evaluation side: three benchmarks, all built by others】This work does not publish its own evaluation benchmark and relies entirely on third-party benchmarks, unlike UniVerse-1 (self-built Verse-Bench) or MOVA (self-built Arena evaluation set):
1) Kling-Audio-Eval — from Kuaishou Kling's Kling-Foley work (arXiv:2506.19774, June 2025), the most recent V2A-specific evaluation set at the time, with its own taxonomy. HunyuanVideo-Foley achieves FD_PANNs 6.07 (MMAudio 9.01, a 32.6% reduction), FD_PASST 202.12, KL 1.89 (MMAudio 2.17, a 12.9% reduction), IB 0.38 (MMAudio 0.30, a 26.7% increase), DeSync 0.54 (MMAudio 0.56), and PQ 6.12 (MMAudio 5.94) on this benchmark.
2) VGGSound-Test — the classic V2A evaluation set, a test split of roughly 550 hours of source data, with categories based on VGGSound's ontology of 300+ sound-event classes. Results: PQ 6.40 / IB 0.36 / DeSync 0.53. The paper specifically notes that the relative disadvantage on FD/KL metrics on this set stems from VGGSound being recorded with "non-professional equipment" — i.e., the reference audio in this set is itself of lower audio quality, whereas this model, having been trained on data filtered strictly for bandwidth and aesthetics, tends to generate high-fidelity audio, and thus loses out on distribution-distance metrics compared against low-quality references. This is a rather notable observation: when the quality orientation of the training data does not match the quality level of the evaluation set, distribution-matching metrics (FD/KL) will systematically penalize a higher-quality model. This constitutes a substantive mismatch between the training-data distribution and the evaluation benchmark, and is the only training-evaluation distribution issue this work discusses explicitly.
3) MovieGen-Audio-Bench — from Meta's Movie Gen, geared toward film-grade sound-effect scenarios, and the closest of the three to actual application (film post-production foley). Results: PQ 6.59 / IB 0.35 / DeSync 0.74, subjective MOS-Q 4.14±0.68 / MOS-S 4.12±0.77 / MOS-T 4.15±0.75, comprehensively ahead of MMAudio (3.58 / 3.63 / 3.47).
【Substantive judgment on category alignment】Since the training-side taxonomy is undisclosed, the most that can be said is: the three evaluation benchmarks cover three tiers — general sound events (VGGSound), V2A-specific scenarios (Kling-Audio-Eval), and film-foley scenarios (MovieGen-Audio-Bench) — with fairly complete coverage; this work shows its greatest advantage on MovieGen-Audio-Bench, the tier closest to real applications, and is at a partial disadvantage on some metrics on VGGSound-Test, the tier with the lowest audio quality — a pattern highly consistent with the strategy of "the training data was screened for high audio quality." But there is no evidence as to whether the training-side category-distribution management was aligned with these evaluation categories by design. [Uncertain]

### [InstructAV2AV](../models/InstructAV2AV.md) ⚠️

The organizing dimensions of the training data show partial alignment with the evaluation system, but the alignment axis is "editing failure mode" rather than visual content category, and fine-grained per-task alignment is missing.

【Alignment 1: data acceptance dimensions ↔ evaluation metrics (well aligned)】
  The five dimensions of automatic verification correspond clearly to the 11 evaluation metrics:
  · instruction fidelity ↔ TV-A (text-video alignment, VideoCLIP-XL), TA-A (text-audio alignment, CLAP)
  · content preservation ↔ SSIM (structural similarity), TC (temporal consistency, inter-frame CLIP similarity), LPAPS (audio perceptual similarity)
  · perceptual quality ↔ FVD (Fréchet Video Distance), FAD (Fréchet Audio Distance, VGGish)
  · audio-video synchronization ↔ AV-A (ImageBind), PEAVS, Sync-C, Sync-D (SyncNet, vshift=15)
  · safety ↔ no corresponding evaluation metric (safety is not tested at the evaluation stage — the only unaligned dimension)
  This "the model is evaluated the same way the data is filtered" design consistency is one of the more methodologically coherent aspects of this work; the 11 metrics are grouped into three areas — video (4), audio (3), and audio-video (4) — providing complete coverage.

【Alignment 2: training task types ↔ evaluation task types (insufficiently aligned)】
  The training data is split into five subsets (add_and_remove / clone_id / clone_voice / clone_id_voice / general_editing), which the paper's main text summarizes into four scenario types (identity-preserving voice modification / AV instance editing / instance insertion / instance removal), but the evaluation only reports overall metrics on a mixed evaluation set, without breaking results down by task type. This means it is impossible to see which type of editing the model is strong or weak at — for example, "instance insertion" (which requires generating a new object and its sound out of nothing) is obviously different in difficulty from "instance removal" (which requires repairing the background), and the mixed metric obscures this difference. This is a clear shortcoming in the evaluation design.

【Alignment with external benchmarks】Besides its self-built 1K evaluation set, this work also performs zero-shot generalization evaluation on the public AvED-Bench (FVD 227.82 / FAD 4.32 / AV-A 23.71, compared with AVI-Edit's 372.37 / 7.65 / 23.21). This step is important: since the training set and the self-built evaluation set come from the same data engine, there is a systematic risk that "the evaluation favors the model's own synthesis style"; the leading results on the external benchmark provide independent evidence of the model's genuine generalization ability.

【Relationship with the visual-domain taxonomy】This work does not involve a VBench/VABench-style visual content taxonomy (people/actions/scenes/styles, etc.) — the training data is neither organized by this nor evaluated by this — because the capability boundary of the editing task is defined by "type of editing operation" rather than "content category." This is the fundamental divide between editing-oriented and generation-oriented works in terms of evaluation systems.
[Uncertain] No alignment analysis was done against mainstream video-generation benchmarks such as VBench or VABench; no per-task breakdown of metrics is reported; it is not explicitly stated whether the 1K evaluation set and the 79K training set share no overlap at the source-material level.

### [2026 Other Joint Audio-Video Generation Works](../models/JAVG_2026_misc.md) ⚠️

【ALIVE — partial alignment between the training label system and the evaluation taxonomy (the closest to a closed loop in this batch)】
(1) Training-side taxonomy: a top-level speaking / non-speaking binary split plus a three-tier hierarchy of nine Level-1 domains (Animals, Home Sounds, Entertainment, Environment, Food, Nature, Sound Effects, Vehicles, Sports), with the proportion of each category actively adjusted based on "concept frequency + expected application scenarios."
(2) Evaluation-side taxonomy: Alive-Bench 1.0 contains 264 general prompts that "cover a wide range of scenarios, including single-person speech, multi-people conversations, sports, daily activities, animals" — plus another 90 reference-character prompts specifically for testing reference-character ability. Evaluation covers both Chinese and English.
(3) Alignment relationship: the evaluation categories overlap noticeably with the training Level-1 domains (sports ↔ Sports, animals ↔ Animals, daily activities ↔ Home Sounds/Food), and the distinction between "single-person speech vs. multi-person conversation" directly corresponds to the training-side speaking/non-speaking split and the multi-person scenarios targeted by SubjectID correction. ALIVE can be said to have the clearest correspondence between training distribution and evaluation taxonomy in this batch.
(4) The 22 fine-grained metrics fall into six major categories [specific categories not extracted, uncertain]; no results grouped by domain were found, so the alignment remains at the "taxonomy design level" rather than the "results-grouping-and-reporting level" [uncertain].
【NAVA — training classification and evaluation benchmark not aligned】
The training side uses YAMNet's five audio labels (single-speaker speech / multi-speaker speech / ambient sound / music / singing) to organize the corpus; evaluation uses Verse-Bench (from UniVerse-1) and Seed-TTS, both of which are "quality-dimension" evaluations rather than "content-category" evaluations. Verse-Bench reports Sync-C / Sync-D / video quality / audio WER, and Seed-TTS reports speech metrics. The training side's five audio labels have no corresponding grouping in the evaluation [uncertain]. The user study covers four dimensions — video quality, audio-video synchronization, audio fidelity, and timbre controllability — of which "timbre controllability" directly echoes the training-side single-/multi-speaker distinction and the Timbre-in-Context design, the only point of alignment.
【OmniCustom — the evaluation-set design is deliberately "reverse-aligned" with the training data】
The composition of the 100-example evaluation set is deliberate: 30 real people outside the training set + 70 YouTube video clips, with a 1:1 gender ratio. Deliberately selecting identities that do not exist in the training data is precisely meant to verify zero-shot timbre/identity transfer — i.e., the evaluation set deliberately does not overlap with the training distribution along the identity dimension. This is a kind of "reverse alignment": not pursuing distributional consistency, but pursuing verification of out-of-distribution generalization. The 1:1 gender control is the only explicit demographic-mixture control on the evaluation side.
【CCL — a fine-grained metric system but no category alignment】
The five evaluation metrics — WER / Sync-C / Sync-D / DeSync / IB — cover speech intelligibility, lip-sync (two SyncNet-family metrics), general audio-video synchronization (Synchformer-family), and cross-modal semantics (ImageBind); the metric dimensions are finely divided and happen to correspond to the separation of "temporal synchronization vs. semantic matching." But the training data's domains (interviews/short dramas/films) have no corresponding grouped reporting in the evaluation [uncertain].
【StreamChar — the evaluation design closely matches its long-duration streaming positioning】
On the EMTD dataset, "150 clips generating 10s audio-video pairs" (short-duration capability) + "50 clips paired with randomly sampled transcripts (>300 words) to produce 5-minute continuous streams" (long-duration streaming capability) — the evaluation deliberately sets up short and long tiers, with the long tier (5 minutes, >300-word dialogue) far exceeding the training data's 20-second cap, specifically testing generalization ability. Human evaluation involves 24 evaluators × 50 cases. The evaluation categories have no correspondence with the training data's domain distribution (all are people talking, a single category).
【Baton — self-built Sem100 supplements the semantic-reasoning dimension】
Besides Verse-Bench, a self-built Sem100 is used: "100 unseen videos (10 seconds long) with more complex prompts, selected from the internet to assess the semantic reasoning capability" — 100 unseen 10-second videos paired with more complex prompts, specifically for evaluating semantic-reasoning capability. This benchmark is tailor-made to validate VA-Planner's advantage in semantic planning, i.e., the evaluation benchmark is aligned with the methodological claim (rather than with the training-data distribution). Construction details are undisclosed [uncertain].
【ITS-JAVG — the circularity risk between evaluation benchmark and verifier (the point most worth watching in this batch)】
Evaluation uses JavisBench-mini (testing JavisDiT) and the VGGSound test set (testing MMDisCo). There is a structural problem: its verifiers (JavisScore, ImageBind, AVHScore, etc.) overlap heavily with, or are even the same source as, the evaluation metrics — using a metric as the verifier to guide the search, and then using the same metric to evaluate the outcome, is bound to "improve" the score. The paper is clearly aware of this, which is precisely the motivation behind its proposed concept of verifier hacking and its multi-verifier combination scheme: only when the set of verifiers being optimized and the set of metrics being evaluated do not fully overlap is the improvement genuine. This methodological warning applies equally to the data side — if a model with the same origin as the final evaluation is used to filter the data (e.g., filtering data with SyncNet and then evaluating with Sync-C), part of the resulting gain is circular reasoning. Both NAVA (filtering with SyncNet, evaluating with Sync-C/Sync-D) and OmniCustom (the same situation) in this batch have this potential circularity, and neither paper discusses it [uncertain].

### [Joint Audio-Video Generation Baseline Collection](../models/JavisDiT_baselines.md) ⚠️

In this collection, both JavisDiT and Harmony built their own evaluation benchmarks, but the degree of alignment with the training-data distribution differs sharply between the two:
【JavisDiT's JavisBench — a complete classification system, but deliberately not aligned with the training-data distribution (an intentional "stress test" design)】
- Classification system: five evaluation dimensions, 19 scenario categories — Event Scenario, Video Style, Sound Type, Spatial Composition, Temporal Composition. This is the most systematic audio-video evaluation taxonomy in this collection, and belongs to the same class of effort as VABench's seven categories.
- Difficulty design: "over 50% of the videos belong to highly complex and challenging scenarios," "75% of the samples contain multiple sounding events" — deliberately going beyond the simple settings of earlier baselines (Landscape's single natural sound, AIST++'s single dance).
- Relationship with the training data: the training data comes from TAVGBench (a general YouTube distribution, with speech removed), while JavisBench mixes the Landscape/AIST++/FAVDBench test sets with new YouTube videos from June–December 2024, organized in a balanced manner across the 19 categories — there is no correspondence between the two taxonomies, and the training side does not even control mixture ratios by the 19 categories [uncertain]. This means JavisBench is more like an "out-of-domain generalization stress test" than a "mirror evaluation of the training distribution"; the authors indeed use it to expose the shortcomings of existing models in complex scenarios.
- The temporal-isolation design is worth noting: the evaluation videos are taken from content uploaded in June–December 2024, later than the training-data cutoff of most models, reducing the risk of training-data leakage — this is a sound benchmark-construction practice.
- Scale stratification: the full version, 10,140 items, is used for the main experiments; JavisBench-mini (a random sample of 1,000 items) is used for ablations, balancing rigor and iteration cost.
【Harmony's Harmony-Bench — training mixture strictly aligned with evaluation categories (the only aligned case in this collection)】
- Three tiers of 50 items each, 150 total: ambient-sound-video (synchronization of non-speech acoustic events), speech-video (lip-sync fidelity and multilingual robustness), and complex scenarios (co-occurrence of speech + ambient sound).
- The correspondence with the training data is very direct: the training data is strictly mixed at a speech : ambient-sound ratio of 1:1 → the first two evaluation tiers correspond exactly to these two categories; the ability to "handle speech and ambient sound simultaneously," which the mixed training of the two categories aims to cultivate → the third, complex-scenario tier is a targeted test of exactly this ability. This is a one-to-one mapping of "training mixture ↔ evaluation category," unique to this collection.
- The conditioning setup also varies by category: the ambient-sound subset uses audio/video captions, the speech subset relies mainly on transcripts, and the complex-scenario subset uses the full set of multimodal prompts — the evaluation protocol is likewise aligned with the three-field caption schema.
【JavisDiT++'s multi-dimensional metric system】11 metrics cover 4 dimensions (quality FVD/FAD, text consistency TV-IB/TA-IB/CLIP-Score/CLAP-Score, audio-video consistency AV-IB/AVHScore/JavisScore, synchronization DeSync); the dimension split corresponds to its architectural design (MS-MoE handles single-modality quality, TA-RoPE handles synchronization, AV-DPO handles the overall combination of all three) — this is an "architecture-metric alignment" rather than a "data-metric alignment."
【UniAVGen】Evaluation uses a self-built 100-item test set (a 50% real-image / 50% AIGC-anime-image split, partially aligned with the training data's real-person focus) plus two public benchmarks — GRID (dubbing) and EMTD (audio-driven animation) — organized by task rather than by content category; no taxonomy has been established [uncertain].
【MM-Diffusion / AV-DiT】No taxonomy; evaluation is split by dataset (Landscape / AIST++), with only FVD and FAD as metrics; training and evaluation use different splits of the same dataset, constituting strict in-domain evaluation — this is also the reason the generalization ability of this early work was never tested.

### [Movie Gen](../models/Movie_Gen.md)

Partially aligned, and the alignment relationship is traceable:
· Movie Gen Video Bench (1,003 prompts, more than three times the size of prior work's prompt sets) sets up 5 concept categories: ① human activity (body and mouth movement, emotion, etc.) ② animals ③ nature and landscapes ④ physical phenomena (fluid dynamics, gravity, acceleration, collisions, explosions, etc.) ⑤ unconventional subjects and unconventional actions; and each prompt is additionally tagged with a high/medium/low motion-level label. ### [OmniHuman Dataset + OHBench](../models/OmniHuman.md) ⚠️

The alignment between the training-data distribution and the evaluation taxonomy is the most structurally rigorous point in this work — the dataset and the benchmark are threaded through by the same three-tier ontology, a textbook example of "the dataset and the benchmark co-designed":
【The three-tier ontology runs through everything】The paper's problem diagnosis, dataset annotation, and evaluation system correspond strictly one-to-one:
- Diagnosis 1, "limited global scene and camera diversity" → data-side video-level annotation (scene type/shot scale/camera motion/background/lighting/background sound/music attributes) → evaluation-side global layer (video quality IQ/DD/BP, audio quality AbS/KL/FD, multimodal alignment V-A/T-A);
- Diagnosis 2, "sparse interaction modeling (person-person + person-object)" → data-side frame-level annotation (person-person interaction labels including speaker ID and emotion, person-object interaction annotation, 134 keypoints) → evaluation-side relational layer (person-person: IN/LR/ES/IC*, person-object: OC/CN);
- Diagnosis 3, "insufficient individual-attribute alignment" → data-side individual-level annotation (ID, appearance, action, expression, speech transcription and emotion, speech quality) → evaluation-side individual layer (subject-video: ID Fidelity/Attribute Consistency/Lip-Sync, subject-audio: WER/SQ).
This triple alignment of "problem — data — evaluation" gives the benchmark diagnostic power rather than just a scoring function: a drop in a particular metric can be traced back directly to the corresponding annotation dimension in the dataset.
【Correspondence between the evaluation set's composition and the dataset's categories】OHBench's 509 videos consist of 331 single-person / 128 two-person / 50 person-object, corresponding one-to-one to the dataset's three types of person configurations, with the ratio broadly reflecting a progression in task difficulty rather than the natural data distribution.
【An important piece of reverse design: a deliberate domain gap】Although the evaluation videos are selected from OmniHuman, they are explicitly required to have a "domain gap relative to the training set" — i.e., a deliberate avoidance of the same distribution between the evaluation set and the training set. This is a necessary self-constraint in works where "the dataset and the benchmark share the same source," to prevent the benchmark from becoming an overfitting test on one's own data. However, the paper does not explain how the domain gap is defined or measured (by scene category? by content type? by source channel?), nor does it quantify the size of the gap. [Uncertain]
【Relationship to VBench-style benchmarks】The paper explicitly criticizes that "existing metrics (such as VBench's aesthetic score) are often inconsistent with human perception," and its improvement path is to bring in large models such as Gemini-3 for semantic/physical-layer judgment in place of shallow scorers. This is a shared trend among 2026 evaluation benchmarks (of the same origin as MOVA's use of a large model as adjudicator), but as noted in human_in_loop, the quantitative evidence for its "consistency with human perception" is absent from the main text, which limits the persuasiveness of this core claim.
【The full list of seven dimensions (an analogy to VABench's seven categories)】OHBench's three layers, seven dimensions: three global-layer dimensions (video quality, audio quality, multimodal alignment) + two relational-layer dimensions (person-person interaction, person-object interaction) + two individual-layer dimensions (subject-video quality, subject-audio quality). Compared to traditional benchmarks organized only by "quality/consistency/diversity," organizing by the spatial hierarchy of "global-relational-individual" is a new organizational paradigm, especially suited to multi-subject person scenarios.

### [Open-Sora Series](../models/Open-Sora.md) ⚠️

### [Script-a-Video](../models/Script-a-Video.md)

This work does not build its own evaluation benchmark, so there is no explicit design for "alignment between the training-data domain distribution and a proprietary benchmark taxonomy." But several layers of alignment worth recording do exist:
【Alignment between the training-data domains and the internal evaluation set】The training side's 500K covers three domains — film / television / lifestyle; the internal 225-item evaluation set covers four categories — movie and TV drama clips, short-form videos, indoor scenes, outdoor scenes. The first two categories (film/TV, short-form video) correspond directly to the training domains; the latter two (indoor/outdoor scenes) are an orthogonal scenario-dimension supplement. Overall this is an in-distribution evaluation, with no explicit out-of-distribution generalization test.
【Alignment between schema design and evaluation dimensions (this work's clearest alignment relationship)】The four MTSS streams have a near one-to-one mapping to the five human-evaluation dimensions on the generation side:
- Reference stream ↔ Identity Consistency (Cons.) + Reference ID Similarity (ArcFace)
- Shot stream ↔ Multi-Shot-Consistency (MSC) + Shot Boundary Deviation + Intra-Shot Subject Consistency
- Event stream ↔ Audio-Video Synchronization (A-V) + WER + UTMOS + SyncNet
- Global stream ↔ the Scene and Style sub-dimensions of Semantic Following
This "every stream in the annotation schema has a corresponding evaluation metric" design lets the ablation analysis pinpoint specific structural components, an advantage of this work's experimental design. Likewise, the four sub-dimensions of Semantic Following (Subject / Action / Scene / Style) correspond closely to the information organization of MTSS — Subject corresponds to the Reference stream, Action to the visual_description of the Shot stream, and Scene and Style to the Global stream.
【The selection logic for external benchmarks】The four external benchmarks have a clear division of labor: Video-SALMONN-2 testset and UGC-VideoCap test "description fidelity" (the former breaking down errors into Miss/Incorrect/Hallucination, the latter into Visual/Audio/Details dimensions); Daily-Omni and WorldSense test "reasoning fidelity." UGC-VideoCap's Visual/Audio/Details three-way split happens to allow observing MTSS's differentiated gains on the visual versus auditory sides (results show the audio-side gain is much larger than the visual-side gain), which is a good alignment between benchmark selection and the method's claims.
【Parts with no correspondence】No alignment has been done with the taxonomy of audio-video-specific benchmarks such as VABench, nor has the distribution of the training data under any standard taxonomy been reported.

### [Seedance 2.0 and Seedance 1.5 pro](../models/Seedance_20_Seedance_15_pro.md)

### [SpeakerVid-5M](../models/SpeakerVid-5M.md) ⚠️

SpeakerVid-5M is one of the samples in this survey with the highest degree of alignment between the training-data taxonomy and the evaluation benchmark — because the two were built by the same team from the same source, and VidChatBench is carved directly out of the dataset:
【Construction homology】VidChatBench contains 500 test pairs, using unseen speaker IDs that did not appear in training — i.e., the test set and the training set are strictly non-overlapping along the identity dimension. It is not an independently collected external benchmark, but a held-out set produced by the same pipeline, under the same annotation system.
【One-to-one correspondence between the six-dimension metric system and the data annotation】Every dimension of VidChatBench's metrics can find a corresponding construction basis in the dataset's annotation fields:
  1) Video quality (FID, FVD, PSNR, SSIM) ← corresponds to the data-side DOVER, clarity, luminance, and blur filtering.
  2) Identity preservation (ArcFace cosine distance) ← corresponds to the same tool and feature space used on the data side for ArcFace-based ID correction.
  3) Dialogue coherence (CLIP distance between the generated result and the LLM-ranked candidates) ← corresponds to the data-side ASR transcription and multi-turn context aggregation.
  4) Audio-video synchronization (SyncNet confidence) ← corresponds to the same metric used on the data side for audio-visual binding and synchronization annotation via SyncNet.
  5) Emotion alignment (expression FID extracted via Deep3DFaceRecon) ← corresponds to the facial-expression summary field in the data-side captions.
  6) Speaker identity/timbre (SIM-o timbre similarity, based on UniSpeech) ← corresponds to the data-side 3D-Speaker voiceprint ID system.
【Alignment of interaction-form categories】The dataset's four branches (talking / listening / dialogue / multi-turn) directly define the downstream task's capability breakdown; VidChatBench's test pairs are organized in a dyadic conversational form, consistent with the organization of the dialogue branch.
【Difference from VABench-style external benchmarks】VABench and similar benchmarks divide by seven major content categories (people, animals, nature, musical instruments, etc.), examining the breadth of content coverage; VidChatBench, by contrast, divides by "capability dimension" rather than "content category," because SpeakerVid-5M's content domain is highly singular (entirely real-person conversation), so breadth alignment is not applicable, and it instead performs fine-grained alignment along the depth dimension.
【Potential risk】The high alignment brought by same-source construction also means the evaluation may overestimate generalization ability — the test set and training set share the same collection preferences, the same cleaning thresholds, and the same systematic biases from the same annotation models; external validity across datasets has not been verified (the paper does not report results on external benchmarks such as HDTF or CelebV-HQ). [Uncertain]

### [UniTalking](../models/UniTalking.md) ⚠️

UniTalking has not released a companion evaluation benchmark; evaluation mainly relies on external test sets plus a small, undisclosed self-built set, so there is no self-consistent design for "alignment between training-data distribution and evaluation taxonomy":
【Three evaluation setups and their relationship with the training data】
1) T2AV joint generation — a self-built (undisclosed) set of 50 speaker-generation test prompts, used for a 20-person blind pairwise-preference study (three dimensions: video quality/audio quality/audio-visual synchronization), as well as objective Sync-C/Sync-D lip-sync evaluation. This is the only evaluation fully aligned with the training-data domain (also a speaker scenario), but with only 50 items it cannot constitute a taxonomy, nor is it stratified along any dimension (personal attributes/scene/language/duration);
2) TR2AV voice cloning — adopts Qwen3-Omni's experimental setup, using the MiniMax Multilingual Test Set to evaluate timbre similarity, reported separately for English and Chinese. This test set is a pure-audio benchmark designed for TTS voice cloning; its language and speaker distribution have no correspondence with UniTalking's training-data distribution and is entirely an external borrowing;
3) TTS generation — follows OVI's evaluation protocol, evaluating WER on Seed-TTS test-en (English only). Also an external pure-audio benchmark, covering English only, and not aligned with the training data (whose language distribution is unknown but may include a large amount of Chinese).
【The absence of alignment and its impact】
- The training data has never been classified or statistically analyzed under any taxonomy (no distribution data along any dimension such as personal attributes, scene, language, or single-/multi-person), so even where the evaluation side has category splits there is nothing to align them with;
- Both external benchmarks (MiniMax Multilingual, Seed-TTS test-en) are pure-audio TTS benchmarks, capable of evaluating only the model's speech quality and timbre ability, not the core capability of joint audio-video generation — meaning UniTalking's core capability (joint generation + lip-sync) is actually covered only by the subjective evaluation on the 50 self-built prompts and the two objective metrics Sync-C/Sync-D, a clearly insufficient evaluation coverage;
- No public benchmark for joint audio-video generation is used (for example, Verse-Bench, released by UniVerse-1, is not used, even though UniVerse-1 is one of its main comparison baselines) — meaning the comparison with UniVerse-1 can only be conducted on the self-built 50 prompts, limiting comparability.
【A contribution to evaluation methodology】The paper raises a valuable question about the Sync-C metric itself: OVI achieves an unusually high Sync-C (6.56, higher than Sora2's 5.35), and the authors speculate that this metric favors "exaggerated mouth movement" over "natural articulation." This observation is a warning for the entire field of speaker-generation evaluation (and for the practice of using SyncNet scores as a data-filtering threshold) — if the filtering threshold is based on the same metric, it will systematically favor training samples with exaggerated mouth movement.
【Conclusion】Between the training-data domain and the evaluation system there is "domain consistency without category alignment": both focus on speaker scenarios, but the training side has no category statistics and the evaluation side has no category design, so there is no analyzable correspondence structure between the two. [Uncertain]

### [UniVerse-1](../models/UniVerse-1.md)

UniVerse-1 released both training data and an evaluation benchmark; there is partial alignment between the two taxonomies, but it is not a strictly designed correspondence:
【Structure of Verse-Bench】600 image-text prompt pairs, divided into three subsets:
- Set1-I: 205 items, with image sources being AI-generated images, web-crawled images, and media screenshots — testing generalization to synthetic/diverse visual inputs;

【Verse-Bench's audio category distribution (nine categories)】Natural environment 36.1%, music and instruments 19.3%, daily life 10.2%, human voice 7.6%, transportation 4.5%, industrial and urban 3.9%, weapons and explosions 2.5%, special-effect sounds 2.3%, uncategorized 15.8% (a more detailed sub-category table is given in the appendix).
【Alignment relationship with the training data】
- Strong alignment: human voice makes up only 7.6% of the evaluation set, and speech data makes up 15.4% of the training set — both are "non-speech-dominant," a consistent orientation; music and instruments make up 19.3% of the evaluation set, and the training side has corresponding musical-variety-show and classical-performance material plus the Ace-step base model, another correspondence;
- Weak/no alignment: the training data has never been classified, statistically analyzed, or mixture-controlled by these nine audio categories — the training side's classification is only the binary speech/non-speech split, while the evaluation side has nine fine-grained categories. This nine-category taxonomy was therefore designed independently for evaluation rather than derived from the training-data mixture. Categories such as weapons and explosions (2.5%) and special-effect sounds (2.3%) have no corresponding source described in the training data at all;
- Set3-Ted corresponds directly to the "public speaking, interviews" genre in the training data, the clearest training-evaluation correspondence among the three subsets.
【Evaluation task coverage】Verse-Bench supports four types of tasks: joint audio-video generation, audio-to-video, video-to-audio, and text-to-speech. This multi-task design directly benefits from its factorized three-field caption schema (which can mask off various conditioning paths to construct different tasks) — a genuine structural alignment between the data schema and the evaluation design.
【Conclusion】The alignment between UniVerse-1's training-data distribution and its evaluation taxonomy is "same-source but not deliberately aligned" — the evaluation taxonomy is finer and more complete, while the training side remains at a coarse binary split, lacking a mixture-control mechanism by category.

### [Unison](../models/Unison.md) ⚠️

Unison has not released a public evaluation benchmark, nor has it built any taxonomy, so there is no strict-sense "alignment between training-data distribution and evaluation taxonomy":
【Composition of the evaluation set】1,000 held-out samples, described as "curated," with ground-truth annotation generated by Gemini, covering both T2AV and TI2AV tasks. The paper does not disclose: which data sources these 1,000 items come from, how they were sampled, whether they are stratified by category, the proportion of each category, or whether they are strictly isolated from the training data (the term "held-out" implies a same-source holdout, which if true would mean the evaluation set shares the same distribution as the training set, leaving generalization ability untested). This is a gap in the evaluation design worth questioning. [Uncertain]
【Comparison with works like VABench/Verse-Bench】UniVerse-1's Verse-Bench gives explicit proportions for nine audio categories (natural environment 36.1%, music and instruments 19.3%, etc.) and a three-subset structure; MOVA built a bilingual Chinese-English Arena evaluation set. Unison has no taxonomy, no subset division, no language division, and its evaluation set is not open-sourced — clearly weaker than these two in terms of benchmark construction.
【The only place category-awareness exists: instance-level analysis of SCG gating】Fig. 8c shows "mean gate values across semantic categories," indicating that the team did in fact divide samples into semantic categories internally and computed gating behavior by category. But the paper neither lists these categories nor gives the proportion of samples in each, nor does it state the correspondence between these categories and the composition of the training data — this is a place that could have filled the taxonomy gap but was not elaborated on. [Uncertain]
【An implicit three-way scenario split (the only reconstructible "taxonomy")】From the paper's qualitative discussion and the design intent of SCG, a three-way classification based on the nature of the acoustic scene can be reconstructed: narration-dominant scenes (where SCG suppresses the sound-effect stream to protect speech purity), complex-soundscape scenes (where SCG amplifies cross-stream influence to enrich non-speech acoustics), and difficult scenes where speech and sound effects are equally important (the paper gives examples: singing while playing an instrument, sports commentary, beach conversation, piano performance, a running locomotive). This split is the true organizing principle behind Unison's data and method design — it explains both why the YuE singing corpus and VGGSound were introduced and the gating logic of SCG, and it also corresponds to the original evaluation dimension of "speech-sound-effect harmony" in the user study (Unison wins this dimension with a score of 1.55, and its lead over the runner-up LTX-2's 1.95 is the largest of any dimension). But this is a reconstruction made by this entry from the paper's discussion — the paper itself never formalizes it as a taxonomy, nor does it give a corresponding data mixture.
【The actual state of training-evaluation alignment】The three evaluation dimensions in the user study (lip-sync, speech-sound-effect harmony, motion-audio alignment) correspond one-to-one to the paper's three technical contributions (lip-filtering + three-frame cross-attention, SGHS/Bi-ACA/SCG, CMFS) — a genuine structural alignment between the evaluation design and the method design; but there is no verifiable correspondence at all with the training data's category distribution.

### [Veo 3 / Veo 3.1](../models/Veo_3_Veo_31.md) ⚠️

[Uncertain] The alignment relationship between the training-data domain distribution and the evaluation-benchmark taxonomy is undisclosed; officially, no proprietary category-based evaluation benchmark has been built (compare with VABench's seven categories). The confirmed evaluation system is: (1) Meta's published MovieGenBench — 1,003 prompts on the video side, 527 prompts on the video+audio side — compared against MovieGen, Kling 2.0, MiniMax T2V-01, Sora Turbo/OpenAI Sora, Runway Gen-3, WAN 2.1, and Kling 2.0 + MMAudio; (2) VBench-I2V — 355 image-text pairs, compared against Runway Gen-4, Kling 2.0 Pro, WAN 2.1, and MiniMax I2V-01. Evaluation uses head-to-head human-rater preference comparisons; Veo 3 achieves SOTA on overall preference and prompt-adherence; Veo 3.1 is officially stated to perform best on overall preference, text alignment, and visual quality. In addition, safety evaluation uses a standardized bias-evaluation set of 140 occupations × 16 videos, plus a number of adversarial safety prompt datasets; the correspondence between these evaluation categories and the training-data mixture is not stated.

### [Vidu S1](../models/Vidu_S1.md) ⚠️

There is a fairly clear alignment: the self-built benchmark Vidu-StreamBench contains 500 samples, each consisting of a triple of "action instruction + reference first frame + audio clip," covering diverse action instructions, reference-image styles, speaker attributes, emotions, and application scenarios — this corresponds item-by-item with the training data's filtering dimensions (interactivity/clear action, subject, emotion, multiple visual styles, speaker voice). The five dimensions of human evaluation (Overall, Motion Dynamics, Identity Consistency, Audio-Video Sync, Subject Controllability) also correspond respectively to the training-data-side interactivity filtering, single-subject filtering + shot stability, diarization-based audio-visual correspondence, and the controllability brought by speech-aware chunk captioning. The nine quality dimensions tagged by the omni-model labeler (editing/subject/action/emotion/face/speech/scene/shot/tone) can be regarded as the training side's taxonomy, but the paper gives no explicit mapping table or category-proportion comparison against the benchmark's categories [partially uncertain]. On the public-benchmark side, only HDTF (the standard audio-driven avatar benchmark) is used; the paper points out that public benchmarks such as HDTF cannot adequately evaluate instruction-following and natural motion in real-time interaction, which is precisely the motivation for the self-built Vidu-StreamBench.

### [Wan 2.5 / 2.6 / 2.7](../models/Wan.md) ⚠️

There is a clear but not explicitly stated correspondence between Wan's self-built Wan-Bench and its training-data taxonomy, and there is an obvious gap on the audio-visual dimension.
【Structure of Wan-Bench】Three core dimensions, 14 fine-grained metrics, with a dedicated algorithm designed for each dimension (traditional detectors for simple tasks, MLLMs for complex tasks):
1) Dynamic quality: large-motion generation (scored via normalized RAFT optical-flow magnitude), human artifacts (a YOLOv3-style model trained on 20,000 manually annotated AI-generated images, scoring based on predicted likelihood, bounding box, and duration), physical plausibility and smoothness (video QA via Qwen2-VL, detecting physical violations such as object clipping, unrealistic collisions, and anti-gravity motion), pixel-level stability (identifying static regions via optical flow, then computing inter-frame differences), ID consistency (three sub-dimensions — person/animal/object — via DINO frame-level feature similarity);
2) Image quality: overall image quality (the average of MANIQA fidelity + LAION aesthetic predictor + MUSIQ), scene-generation quality (inter-frame CLIP similarity for consistency + frame-text CLIP similarity for alignment), stylization (frame-level QA via Qwen2-VL);
3) Instruction following: single-object/multi-object/spatial position (Qwen2-VL predicts category, count, and spatial relationship, scored by per-frame hit rate), camera control (five types — pan, crane, dolly, aerial, tracking — 130 custom prompts, the first three analyzed via RAFT optical flow, the latter two via Qwen2-VL QA), action-instruction following (three types of motion — human/animal/object — key frames are given to Qwen2-VL to query action alignment, action completion, and artifacts).
The final score is synthesized via a human-feedback-guided weighting strategy.
【Correspondence with the training data】Dynamic quality ↔ the six-tier motion-quality grading and optical-flow motion score; image quality ↔ the aesthetic scorer and LAION classifier (the evaluation directly reuses the LAION aesthetic predictor, sharing its origin with the training side); ID consistency ↔ ArcFace/SigLIP consistency filtering; camera control ↔ the manually annotated camera-angle-and-motion dataset and expert-labeled expansion; object/count/spatial-relationship ↔ the caption model's three self-built datasets for counting, fine-grained categories, and spatial relationships. It can be said that Wan-Bench's categories are almost a mirror of its data-construction dimensions — but the report never states this correspondence as a design principle.
【Key gap】None of Wan-Bench's three dimensions or 14 metrics include an audio-video synchronization category — the audio dimension is entirely absent; the three capabilities emphasized starting from 2.5 — "audio-visual sync," "multi-shot narrative," and "role-play" — have no corresponding category in Wan-Bench. Audio-visual sync evaluation appears only in the Wan2.2-S2V paper, via the EMTD dataset + the single metric Sync-C. External category-based audio-video benchmarks such as VABench's seven categories are not involved. [Uncertain]

### [Audio-Video Generation Evaluation Benchmark Collection](../models/av_benchmarks.md)

This field is the core output of this survey — the taxonomies of five benchmarks can serve directly as a standard coordinate system for the training-data domain distribution, in reverse; it is recommended to combine them along three orthogonal axes:

【Axis 1: content-domain axis — adopting VABench's seven categories】Animals / human voice (linguistic · non-linguistic) / music / ambient sound (nature · urban · indoor) / synchronized physical sound / complex scenarios (complex soundscapes · subjective feeling · world knowledge · symbolic association · off-screen sound sources) / virtual worlds. This is the most complete content-side taxonomy and can be used directly as the primary row label for a training-data mixture table. Two key design choices should be carried over together: ① human voice must distinguish linguistic from non-linguistic (the former requires lip-sync and ASR annotation, the latter does not); ② the virtual-worlds category should be exempted from the physical-plausibility quality bar, with filtering standards set separately from realistic categories.

【Axis 2: audio-type-and-sync-difficulty axis — adopting AV-SyncBench's three-way split + ten scenarios】Under the three major categories Voice / Music / Sound, ten scenarios are defined: action, animal sounds, object sounds, ambient sounds, group vocalization, single-person speech, dialogue, singing, single instrument, and ensemble. This system splits by synchronization difficulty rather than by theme; its distinction between single sound source and multiple sound sources (single-person speech vs. dialogue/group vocalization, single instrument vs. ensemble) corresponds directly to the mixture requirements for multi-source-overlap samples in the training data, and also determines the choice of sync filters (Synchformer-style models become less reliable in multi-source scenarios).

【Axis 3: physical-correctness-coverage axis — adopting PhyAVBench's six dimensions, 41 test points】Sound-source mechanics / mechanical structure / fluid and aerodynamics / sound-propagation environment / observer physics / time and causality (plus complex coupling and extreme physics). This is a checkable checklist for auditing blind spots in the training data's coverage of physical acoustics. Empirical evaluation has already identified fluid dynamics and sound-propagation environment as blind spots shared across the entire industry, which can serve as priority directions for data supplementation.

【Orthogonal constraint layer — adopting AVBench's quota-balancing mechanism】Hard Quota-Based Greedy Sampling forces any single attribute to make up ≤50% of the total, along with a Normal : Hard difficulty ratio of roughly 3:1. Both can be transplanted directly as concept-balancing strategies and hard-case mixture anchors when sampling training data.

【Real-distribution calibration — adopting Omni-Judge's VidProM prompt distribution】The first four axes are all expert-designed idealized classifications, prone to drifting from real user needs; Omni-Judge directly samples real user prompts from VidProM, providing an empirical distribution of "what users actually want to generate," which can be used to calibrate the weights of the aforementioned taxonomies and avoid over-investing in long-tail categories.

【Implementation recommendation】Build a three-dimensional mixture matrix (content domain × audio type × physical-test-point coverage), weight it using VidProM's real distribution, constrain single-attribute proportions using AVBench's quota rules, audit blind spots using the PhyAVBench checklist, and use AV-SyncBench's scenario tags to drive domain-specific selection of sync filters. Also note the shared gaps across all five benchmarks — multi-shot long-form narrative, multilingual accents, and cross-shot audio-track continuity currently have no benchmark to test against, so training-data investment in these domains cannot yet be validated against public benchmarks.

### [Video Captioning Model Ecosystem](../models/caption_models.md)

【Taxonomies of caption-specific benchmarks】
· VDC (companion to AuroraCap): structured captions split into five fields — camera / short / background / main object / detailed — paired with VDCScore (a divide-and-conquer approach that converts them into multiple short QA pairs judged by an LLM). These five fields effectively define the caption schema used by a large amount of subsequent work.
· AVSCapBench (2026-07, the newest and most fine-grained): 1,226 manually annotated videos (30–120 seconds, from YouTube / TikTok / Video-MME), doing fine-grained event recall along five dimensions — Visual / Speech / Music / SFX / Synergy — designed to prevent "gaming the score with a single modality." These five dimensions correspond closely to the training-data domain needs of AV generation models.
· Omni-Cloze (Alibaba, 2026-03): 2,000 clips / 70,000 fine-grained cloze questions / 9 major domains, 47 subcategories, using cloze questions to avoid LLM-judge noise. The most complete taxonomy among public benchmarks.
· UGC-VideoCap (2025-07): 1,000 TikTok videos + 4,000 QA pairs, with a three-stage human-in-the-loop process separately annotating audio-only / visual-only / joint-AV semantics — categories split by "modality availability" rather than content theme.
· OmniCap-IF (2026-06): the first instruction-following benchmark for audio-video captioning, with 50 types of constraints covering pure-visual/pure-audio/audio-video, including Temporal Grounding, with two-dimensional scoring (format correctness + content correctness). This dimension is critical for production pipelines — the labeler must be able to output according to your schema.
· DREAM-1K (companion to Tarsier): centers on event recall for dynamic actions; Tarsier2 is the first model to exceed 40% overall recall.
· VidCapBench (2025-02): explicitly geared toward video captioning evaluation for controllable T2V, one of the few benchmarks to explicitly tie "caption quality" to "generation controllability."
· video-SALMONN 2 testset: oriented around error rate (missing + hallucination), complementing the recall-oriented benchmarks above.
【Alignment with generation-side evaluation systems】
· Caption-benchmark dimensions (camera / background / main object / motion) correspond clearly to VBench's evaluation dimensions (subject consistency, background consistency, motion smoothness, semantic score); Koala-36M's structured captions boosting VBench's semantic score by +14.1 percentage points is a direct manifestation of this correspondence.
· AVSCapBench's Speech / Music / SFX three-way split is fully isomorphic to the audio categories of AV evaluation benchmarks such as VABench, and to Foley-Omni's three-field schema — indicating that "annotation schema, evaluation taxonomy, and generation-capability dimensions" are converging on the same ontology.
· Methodological caution: AVSCap is both the author of AVSCapBench and its top scorer (60.44); with benchmark and model sharing the same origin, there is a risk of overfitting; the paper was published only two weeks ago (2026-07-14) with no third-party reproduction, so when selecting a captioner, cross-validation with third-party benchmarks such as UGC-VideoCap or Omni-Cloze is necessary. Panda-70M reports a human-agreement rate of only 44.9%, setting a ceiling on the reliability of all caption benchmarks.

### [Geometric/Structured Annotation Dataset Collection](../models/geometric_datasets.md) ⚠️

SceneScribe-1M has the most systematic alignment: the four dimensions of data annotation (text, camera parameters, depth, 3D point trajectories) correspond one-to-one with the six evaluation tasks it establishes — depth annotation corresponds to the monocular-depth-estimation benchmark, camera parameters correspond to the 3D/4D reconstruction benchmark (VGGT/MonST3R), 3D point trajectories correspond to the 2D/3D point-tracking benchmark (CoTracker3/SpatialTrackerV2), and text + camera correspond to the text/pose-to-video generation benchmark (AC3D), forming a closed-loop design of "annotation as task." The alignment of WildWorld and WildBench is explicitly designed: the data-side action ID corresponds to the benchmark's Action Following dimension, skeleton and world state correspond to the State Alignment dimension (evaluated at multiple pixel thresholds — 4/8/16/32), camera intrinsics/extrinsics correspond to the ATE/RPE camera-control dimension (using the ViPE tracker for SfM), and image quality is covered by four VBench metrics (motion smoothness, dynamic degree, aesthetic quality, image quality); the authors explicitly note that VBench-style general-purpose metrics have become "saturated" for interactive tasks, an example of the data-annotation system pushing the evaluation system to expand. SpatialVID's structured label system (weather/time of day/crowd density/lighting/scene type) provides a foundation for splitting evaluation by category, but the paper does not establish a fixed evaluation taxonomy aligned with it [uncertain]. Action100M's hierarchical action annotation corresponds to two major families among its 16 downstream benchmarks — action recognition (8 benchmarks: SSv2, EPIC-KITCHENS-100, EgoExo4D Keysteps, Kinetics-400, COIN, CrossTask, etc.) and text-video retrieval (8 benchmarks: MSR-VTT, ActivityNet, DiDeMo, MSVD, YouCook2, PVD-Bench, Dream-1K, VDC-1K, etc.), corresponding respectively to the supervisory power of the action field and the caption field.

### [Post-Training Data Strategies for Video Generation](../models/post_training_data.md) ⚠️

【Anchor paper】Its evaluation taxonomy has three dimensions: visual quality (overall appearance, sharpness, absence of artifacts), motion quality (temporal coherence, smoothness, plausibility of motion patterns), and text alignment (semantic consistency between the generated video and the input prompt) — corresponding strictly one-to-one with three of its four reward models (video aesthetics/image aesthetics merged into visual quality, motion quality, and text-video alignment). This design of "reward dimension = evaluation dimension" is standard practice in post-training work; its advantage is that the training objective and the evaluation criteria are fully consistent, but its drawback is an inherent risk of self-validation: a model optimized with an RM is then verified with human evaluation along the same dimensions, which cannot rule out the possibility that reward hacking simply is not captured by this set of dimensions. The paper does not report results on external benchmarks such as VBench or EvalCrafter (despite citing them and criticizing that their "evaluation signals are often noisy") [uncertain].
【Industry convergence on the three-dimensional system】VQ/MQ/TA has become the de facto standard taxonomy for video-generation post-training: VideoReward (VQ/MQ/TA annotated separately), LongCat-Video (three VQ/MQ/TA reward models), the anchor paper (three-dimensional GSB), HunyuanVideo 1.5 (aesthetic appeal/clarity/motion fluency), Seedance 1.0 (three RMs: Foundational/Motion/Aesthetic) — this three-dimensional system is precisely a mirror of the training data's SFT filtering criteria (aesthetics + motion + text-image consistency), i.e., the filtering dimensions, reward dimensions, and evaluation dimensions have become highly aligned.
【The absence of the AV dimension】Audio-video evaluation systems such as VABench's seven categories have not yet been connected with post-training reward dimensions: the three-dimensional system is entirely visual, with audio fidelity, lip-sync, event alignment, and timbre consistency all absent from it. JavisDiT++'s six rewards (audio quality / text-audio / video quality / text-video / cross-modal similarity / temporal synchronization) are the only post-training system to extend the evaluation taxonomy to the full AV dimension, and can be regarded as a candidate successor to the three-dimensional system for the AV era. AV evaluation categories such as Movie Gen's audio-side MOS-Q/S/T, Unison's 11 objective metrics (VA/ID/PQ/CU/WER/TA/TV/AV/LSE-C/LSE-D/DS), and UniVerse-1's Verse-Bench (MS/AS/ID/FD/KL/CLAP/LSE-C/AV-A/WER) have not been adopted as reward dimensions by any post-training work [uncertain].

### [Combined Survey of Mainstream Video Pretraining Datasets: Panda-70M, InternVid, Koala…](../models/pretraining_datasets.md) ⚠️

**Alignment between the training-data taxonomy and evaluation-benchmark taxonomy is generally poor across the seven datasets, with only two showing substantive correspondence**:
【Use of evaluation benchmarks】The seven overwhelmingly rely on **VBench** (Koala-36M, LVD-2M, and UltraVideo all use VBench as their primary benchmark), followed by FVD/FID/IS/CLIPSIM (Panda-70M, InternVid, LVD-2M); OpenVid-1M uses DOVER-derived VQAA/VQAT plus Blip_bleu and Clip_temp_score; **MiraData is the only one to build its own benchmark, MiraBench**.
【MiraBench — the only benchmark tailor-made for its own caption structure, and the only genuine case of taxonomy alignment】17 metrics across 6 major aspects: **temporal motion intensity** (Dynamic Degree via optical flow, **Tracking Strength (CoTracker, newly proposed)**), **temporal consistency** (DINOv2 structural consistency, CLIP semantic consistency, AMT motion smoothness), **3D consistency (newly proposed)** (MAE and RMSE of GVGC reconstruction), **image quality** (LAION aesthetics, MUSIQ imaging quality), **text-video alignment** (five items computed via ViCLIP: **camera alignment/subject alignment/background alignment/style alignment/overall alignment**), and **distribution similarity** (FVD/FID/KID). **The key point: the five sub-items of text alignment correspond one-to-one to MiraData's six-field caption (camera_caption → camera alignment, main_object_caption → subject alignment, background_caption → background alignment, style_caption → style alignment) — this is the only design among the seven that achieves a true one-to-one mapping between "the training data's field system ⇄ the evaluation metric system"**; the other six cannot perform dimension-split alignment evaluation at all because their captions are not addressable in this way. Two design details worth noting: Tracking Strength was proposed to correct misjudgments in optical-flow-based dynamic degree (the paper's Figure 5 gives a counterexample: optical flow ranks 1.2 vs. 0.7 incorrectly, while Tracking ranks 4.1 vs. 11.8 correctly); the three temporal-consistency metrics are **multiplied by Tracking Strength** before being reported (because "the greater the motion, the naturally lower the consistency"), **so MiraBench's consistency numbers are not comparable with VBench's**. One more note: the actual code output produces 19+ values (3D consistency outputs 5 sub-metrics, and KVD is also computed though not reported in the paper), not entirely matching the paper's stated 17.
【UltraVideo — has the most complete data taxonomy, yet it is not aligned with the evaluation】Its 7 major themes (video scene/subject/action/temporal event/camera movement/video type/emotion) × 108 subcategories form the most complete data taxonomy among the seven, and sampling was done uniformly across these categories accordingly, **but the evaluation still uses the general-purpose VBench, and with only about 96 prompts (1/10 of VBench)**, leaving the two systems completely disconnected. The authors instead level sharp criticism at VBench: background consistency and dynamic degree can **contradict human perception**, human action and color show **no discriminative power** (multiple models tying at 66.66 and 100.0), and motion smoothness and dynamic degree **directly OOM under true 4K** — i.e., **existing benchmarks cannot evaluate high-resolution generation**, a genuine benchmark gap.
【The remaining five show no alignment】Panda-70M, InternVid, Koala-36M, OpenVid-1M, and LVD-2M **all fail to build a data-side domain taxonomy** (LVD-2M has 8 BART-derived categories, but only for post-hoc statistics, not used for mixture control nor mapped to the evaluation), so there is nothing to align. Only **indirect, low-level correspondences** can be observed: VBench's aesthetic quality ↔ LAION aesthetic filtering, imaging quality ↔ DOVER/clarity filtering, dynamic degree and motion smoothness ↔ bidirectional motion filtering via optical flow/LPIPS, temporal flickering ↔ transition-and-consistency filtering. **That is, the filter design is in fact built around VBench's low-level image-quality and motion dimensions, with no data-side mixture or coverage guarantee at all for VBench's semantic-category dimensions (object class, multiple objects, human action, spatial relationship, scene)** — two sides of the same issue as the "absence of category governance" noted in the domain_distribution field.
【Relationship with VABench-style audio-video benchmarks】**All seven are irrelevant** (none has audio capability), and cannot be used for training-data-side alignment with joint audio-video generation benchmarks.
