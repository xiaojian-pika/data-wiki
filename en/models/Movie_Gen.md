## Basic Information

### Name

Movie Gen (including Movie Gen Audio)

### Releasing institution/company

Meta (Meta AI / GenAI team, a contributor list of over one hundred).

### Release date (technical report/paper/open-source date)

Released alongside the official blog post and technical report on October 4, 2024; arXiv preprint 2410.13720 (v1, posted October 17, 2024), titled "Movie Gen: A Cast of Media Foundation Models."

### Type (model/dataset/toolchain/evaluation benchmark)

Model (a family of media generation foundation models: Movie Gen Video 30B text-to-video, Personalized Movie Gen Video, Movie Gen Edit video editing, Movie Gen Audio 13B audio generation) + evaluation benchmark (Movie Gen Video Bench, Movie Gen Audio Bench).

### Degree of openness (whether weights/code/data/pipeline are each open source)

Semi-open: model weights are not open-sourced, training/inference code is not open-sourced, and the training data is not disclosed. However the technical report (92 pages) discloses the data-cleaning pipeline, per-tier filtering thresholds and funnel retention rates, the captioning scheme, and the training recipe in extreme detail, making it one of the most thorough publicly available data-engineering documents in the industry. The open-sourced portion is the evaluation assets: Movie Gen Video Bench (1,003 prompts) and Movie Gen Audio Bench, together with non-cherry-picked generated result videos/audio on these two benchmarks, hosted at https://github.com/facebookresearch/MovieGenBench . The paper explicitly states the model is for research purposes only and requires further improvement before deployment.

### Whether joint audio-video generation is supported, and the implementation approach (native joint/cascade/MoE fusion)

Does not support native joint audio-video generation; a cascade scheme is used: Movie Gen Video (30B, a Flow Matching Transformer) first generates a silent video, then Movie Gen Audio (13B, a Flow Matching DiT + DAC-VAE latent space) generates a synchronized audio track conditioned on the video and text (V2A / TV2A), supporting audio extension to achieve a full soundtrack up to several minutes long. On the audio side internally, "a single model jointly generates all audio categories" (diegetic sound effects, non-diegetic sound effects, and instrumental music are generated together), rather than splitting into multiple models by category, on the grounds that correlations also exist among different audio categories. The paper's conclusion explicitly states that video and audio are currently trained separately, and "enabling the model to jointly generate both modalities is an important direction for future research." In addition, the model deliberately does not generate human voice/dialogue (reasoning that it can be supplemented by TTS, and is difficult to generate without a script).

## Data Scale and Distribution

### Training data scale (video count/hours/token count, pretraining vs SFT split separately) ⚠️

Pretraining (video): O(100)M video-text pairs + O(1)B image-text pairs; the raw video pool ranges from 4 seconds to 2 minutes in duration (average 28 seconds), and after cleaning each clip is a 4–16-second single-shot clip.
Post-training (video SFT): a small-scale, manually curated set of high-quality videos and manual captions; the paper does not give a specific count [Uncertain]; training uses only 512 H100s (64 nodes), two orders of magnitude smaller than pretraining's up to 6,144 H100s.
Personalization (PT2V): O(1)M single-person videos filtered from the pretraining set; sampled to get O(10)M paired samples, O(10)K real cross-paired, and O(1)M synthetic cross-paired; the SFT set is O(1000) high-quality single-person videos.
Audio pretraining: a total of O(100)M samples / O(1,000)K hours (i.e., on the order of a million hours), of which the Sound category alone accounts for O(100)M / O(1,000)K, while Music, Sound+Music, Sound+Voice, and Sound+Music+Voice each account for O(10)M / O(100)K.
Audio fine-tuning: cinematic-grade audio-video O(100)K samples / O(1)K hours; high-quality audio-only (music O(10)K hours + sound effects O(10)K hours) O(1,000)K samples / O(10)K hours.

### Data source composition (proprietary/public datasets/web scraping/licensed procurement/synthetic data) ⚠️

The paper does not disclose the specific source channel or licensing method of the data, describing it only as "a large pool of videos" / "sourcing data from a large volume," from which one can judge it to be Meta's own large-scale internal video/audio data pool [data-source composition uncertain]. The image-text data cleaning strategy follows Meta's own Emu (Dai et al., 2023) approach. Confirmable compositional characteristics: the raw video covers multiple domains including humans, nature, animals, and objects; the audio pretraining data comes from videos' native audio tracks; audio fine-tuning additionally uses high-quality music-only and sound-effect-only material libraries without video (professionally produced material). Synthetic data is explicitly used in personalization (the personalization image-generation model creates reference images) and video editing (affine-animated image-edit pairs, generative instruction-guided segmentation, backtranslation reverse pairing). No public academic dataset is used as the primary training body (VGGSound etc. are used only for evaluation).

Data-side compliance processes, face/privacy licensing, and generated-content watermarking are all undisclosed.

### Clip duration distribution and segmentation strategy

The raw video ranges from 4 seconds to 2 minutes, averaging 28 seconds; since training needs 4–16-second clips, FFmpeg scene-change detection is first used, sampling 1–2 scenes longer than 16 seconds per video, then randomly extracting one 4–16-second clip from each scene, explicitly avoiding random sampling that ignores shot boundaries (otherwise the generated video would show frequent abrupt cuts). Over 50% of the training clips have a duration in the 15–16-second range.
Bucketing strategy (Table 2, five duration buckets, with consistent latent frame count within each bucket for easy batching): 10.67s@24fps→256 frames/32 latent frames; 16s@16fps→256 frames/32 latent frames; 12–16s@21–16fps→256 frames/32 latent frames; 8–12s@24–16fps→192 frames/24 latent frames; 4–8s@32–16fps→128 frames/16 latent frames. The first two buckets are taken from the middle segment of videos 10.67–12 seconds and ≥16 seconds long. Frame rate is controlled via an FPS token in the caption (16–32 FPS).
SFT video duration is limited to 10.6–16 seconds, with 50% at 16 seconds and 50% at 10.6–16 seconds; the 16-second ones are trained at 16FPS, and the 10.6–16-second ones at 24FPS.
Audio side: video length is limited to 4–120 seconds, with a pretraining sequence cap of 30 seconds (750 frames), randomly chunked if exceeded; fine-tuning randomly samples 10-second and 30-second clips; captions are also made for both the 10-second and 30-second chunk lengths, sampled during training at a 5-batch : 1-batch ratio.

### Resolution/aspect-ratio distribution and bucketing strategy

The resolution threshold tightens as training stages progress: the low-resolution training stage requires minimum width and height ≥720px; the high-resolution training stage requires minimum width and height ≥768px (Table 44 shows this step cutting the data from 100% to 25%). Training resolution progresses from 256px to 768px, with an independent spatial super-resolution model additionally producing 1080p HD output.
Aspect ratio: the pretraining set is controlled at 60% landscape + 40% portrait (favoring landscape, since it has longer duration, better aesthetics, and more stable motion); the high-resolution set is adjusted to 80% landscape + 20% portrait; the "width≥height" step in the cleaning funnel cuts the data from 25% to 7%.
Bucketing: images and videos each use five aspect-ratio buckets, with identical latent shapes within a bucket for batching, so the model can output multiple ratios, such as landscape 1024×576 and portrait 576×1024.
Black-bar issues are handled separately: an in-house border detector (first-derivative to find high-gradient pixels + a scanline algorithm to locate the border) removes videos with black bars, which are especially common in portrait video.
The audio side has a lower requirement for video quality: it is sufficient to remove videos with resolution <480px.

### Category/domain distribution and mixture strategy (proportion control and concept balancing for people, actions, scenes, styles, etc.)

The pretraining raw pool covers domains including humans, nature, animals, and objects. Concept balancing uses a two-step process: ① a video-text joint-embedding model extracts semantic embeddings, clustered into fine-grained concept clusters; ② after merging duplicate clusters, samples are drawn from each cluster at a rate inversely proportional to the square root of cluster size (1/sqrt(cluster size), following Mahajan et al. 2018), suppressing head-dominant concepts and boosting the long tail — this step in the funnel drops the data from 1.15% to 0.94%.
People are a heavily weighted domain: at least 60% of the videos in the final high-resolution training set contain people. To this end a dedicated taxonomy of 600 human verbs and expressions was built, used for zero-shot text→video retrieval to specifically select videos containing people, with these people videos' frequency deliberately preserved during the concept-resampling stage to prevent them from being diluted by the balancing strategy.
Concept balancing at the SFT stage is more refined: the same 600-verb taxonomy is first used for text k-NN to retrieve videos for each concept from the candidate pool, then a few visually outstanding seed videos are manually picked for each concept, and these seeds are used for video k-NN, ultimately yielding a concept-balanced subset small enough to be fully manually reviewed; the k-NN uses the video and text embeddings from the video-text joint-embedding model.
The corresponding concept distribution on the evaluation side is five categories: human activities, animals, nature and landscapes, physical phenomena, and unconventional subjects and unconventional actions.

### Audio category distribution and mixture (proportions and control strategy for speech/foley/music/ambient sound/silence) — a dimension unique to AV models

Movie Gen Audio establishes a two-dimensional classification system of "audio type × diegetic attribute" (Table 23), the core innovation of its data engineering.
First axis, audio type: voice (speech + singing), non-vocal music (pure instrumental music), general sound (general sound effects), automatically labeled by an AED (audio event detection) model based on the AudioSet 527-class ontology, where a sample may hit multiple classes simultaneously (in AudioSet, the speech/singing subclasses → voice; the music subclass → music; the rest → sound).
Second axis, diegetic (on-screen, causally related to the visuals, e.g. live speech, a live band, wave sounds, off-screen birdcalls) / non-diegetic (off-screen, e.g. documentary narration, background score, canned laughter, a riser). This is determined using the cosine similarity between the audio and video embeddings from CAVTP (a contrastive audio-video-text pretraining model) — because this model is mainly trained on diegetic data, the audio-video embedding of on-screen audio that matches the visuals is closer.
Bucketing thresholds (Table 24, determined by manual inspection): when AED is sound / voice / voice+sound, CAVTP>0.2 is judged diegetic; when AED is music / music+sound / voice+music+sound, CAVTP>0.3 is judged diegetic; when AED is music and CAVTP<0.1, judged non-diegetic; when AED is sound+music and 0.1<CAVTP<0.25, judged non-diegetic; when AED is sound+voice+music and 0.1<CAVTP<0.25, judged mixed.
Mixture and trade-offs: first, all videos dominated by silence are discarded; pretraining uses only diegetic or diegetic/non-diegetic mixed data, plus a small amount of non-diegetic background music; general sound is explicitly prioritized (because low-level physical regularities are the hardest to learn and errors are most easily noticed), and in practice the Sound category, at O(100)M samples/O(1,000)K hours, absolutely dominates, while the other four categories each account for only O(10)M/O(100)K — i.e., sound effects outnumber music/vocal-containing categories by roughly a 10:1 order of magnitude.
Deliberately excluded: not generating diegetic speech (hard without a script, and generated video has artifacts) or non-diegetic speech (can be substituted with TTS); in the fine-tuning cinematic split, clips with vocals are entirely excluded.
Fine-tuning-stage mixture: cinematic-grade audio-video (Cin-AV) and high-quality audio-only (HQ-A) are mixed at a 10-batch : 1-batch ratio.

### Narrative structure distribution (single-shot vs. multi-shot, average clip duration, shot-count distribution, whether a native audio track is present)

The video training data is entirely single-shot clips: FFmpeg scene-boundary detection + PySceneDetect jitter detection ensures no transitions or jump cuts within a clip, requiring non-trivial motion; only 1–2 scenes are taken per source video, one clip per scene, with average clip length falling in the 15–16-second range (>50%). The model is therefore a single-shot generator and does not model multi-shot narrative.
Whether a native audio track is present: Movie Gen Video's training does not use the audio track at all (purely visual + text); Movie Gen Audio's pretraining, in contrast, relies on the video's native audio track as the supervisory signal. The two data streams are separate.
Multi-shot only appears on the evaluation side: Movie Gen Audio Bench divides evaluation videos into single-shot (large quantity, broad sound-effect coverage, testing robustness and generalization) and multi-shot (taken from short films, containing scene transitions, stronger emotion and narrative, used to evaluate video-music alignment — such as when music enters, how it evolves with the plot, whether it aligns with editing cut points, and whether the mix of sound effects and music is harmonious).

### Language/accent distribution (the data basis for multilingual lip-sync capability)

Not applicable and not modeled: Movie Gen Audio deliberately does not generate any human voice/dialogue (both diegetic speech and non-diegetic narration are excluded from the generation targets), so there is no language, accent, or lip-sync-related data dimension, nor any multilingual lip-sync capability. The data-side handling of speech goes no further than using AED to determine whether speech/singing is present in a sample, as a binary control label in the caption and as a bucketing basis. On the text-conditioning side, the paper explicitly states this research is "limited to English text input."

## Cleaning Pipeline

### Cleaning funnel overall structure (how many filtering tiers, order of each tier)

[Video side] A three-tier filter + one-tier captioning funnel (paper Figure 9):
1) Visual filtering (6 filters): minimum-width/height threshold → aspect-ratio mixture → video OCR removal of subtitles/on-screen text → FFmpeg scene-boundary detection cutting 4–16-second clips → a lightweight visual model trained to predict and filter frame-level aesthetics/image quality/large borders/visual effects → following Panda-70M, removing the first few seconds of clips that overlap with the video's opening (openings often have unstable camerawork and transition effects).
2) Motion filtering: an internal static-video detection model removes videos with no motion → FFmpeg VMAF motion score and motion vector determine "reasonable motion" → PySceneDetect's per-second shot count identifies and removes jitter → removes slideshow and other special-effect motion.
3) Content filtering: copy-detection embedding perceptual deduplication → video-text joint-embedding clustering + merging duplicate clusters + 1/sqrt(cluster size) resampling for concept balancing.
4) Captioning: LLaMa3-Video 8B/70B generates dense captions averaging 100 words + a 16-class camera-movement classifier result prepended + an FPS token.
There is also a "multi-stage curation": progressively stricter visual/motion/content thresholds are used to cut out 3 subsets — the 720px low-resolution training set, the 768px high-resolution training set, and a newly expanded high-resolution set after additional data augmentation.
[Video SFT side] Four sequential stages: ① automated strict-threshold screening (strict thresholds on aesthetics, motion, and scene changes + using Detic object detection to remove videos where the subject is too small) yields several million candidates that are conceptually unbalanced; ② concept balancing (the 600-verb taxonomy for text k-NN plus manually picked seeds for video k-NN) narrows this to a size that can be manually reviewed; ③ manually selecting cinematic-feeling videos (requiring angled or natural light, or studio lighting; vivid but not over-saturated color; an uncluttered frame; non-trivial motion; no camera shake; no editing effects or overlaid text), with annotators manually cropping out the most compelling segment; ④ manually refining and completing the captions generated by LLaMa3-Video.
[Audio side]: AED labels per the AudioSet 527-class ontology → discarding silence-dominated samples → mapping to voice/music/sound three classes → using CAVTP cosine similarity to bucket into diegetic/non-diegetic/mixed → visual-side quality filtering (OCR removal of videos with text, removal of static videos, removal of low-resolution videos <480px) → duration limited to 4–120 seconds → copy-detection embedding visual deduplication → multi-model synthesized structured captioning. The fine-tuning data additionally uses a "cinematic audio-video classifier + AED" for automated initial screening, followed by manual annotation for the final selection.

### Quantitative funnel retention rate (input/output volume of each tier and the final retention rate, e.g., Apollo's 27%) ⚠️

The paper's Table 44 gives an industry-rare tier-by-tier quantitative funnel (for the strictest high-resolution-set curation thresholds, numbers are the remaining volume percentage relative to the raw pool):
Duration 4s≤duration≤120s → 100%
Resolution width≥768 and height≥768 → 25% (a single step removing 75%)
Aspect ratio width≥height → 7% (a single step removing 72%, the steepest single cut)
No text (word-detection score × word-recognition score <0.6 across all sampled frames) → 1.94% (a single step removing 72%)
No black bars → 1.87%
No scene changes (sampling one 12–16-second clip from 1 scene) → 1.78%
Aesthetics (LAION aesthetic score of the clip's middle frame ≥4.0) → 1.57%
Non-slow-motion (motion score>2.0, motion vector mean >0.5 and <7) → 1.32%
Non-jittery (shots per second <0.85) → 1.22%
No content duplication (embedding cosine similarity <0.99) → 1.15%
Concept resampling (per-cluster retention 1/sqrt(cluster size)) → 0.94%
The paper's original text explicitly states that under this set of thresholds, "the data acceptance rate is less than 1%" — i.e., about one percent of the raw video enters the high-resolution training set. The audio side does not give a corresponding quantitative funnel [Uncertain].

### Shot-segmentation method (PySceneDetect/in-house model/shot-aware splitting)

A combination of two tools: ① FFmpeg's scene change detection performs the primary segmentation — first detecting scene boundaries, sampling 1–2 scenes longer than 16 seconds per source video, then randomly extracting one 4–16-second clip from within each scene as a training clip; the paper explicitly states it avoids "random sampling that ignores scene boundaries," otherwise the generated video would show frequent, abrupt scene cuts. ② PySceneDetect's Shot Boundary Detection is used as a jitter detector rather than a segmenter — because FFmpeg's motion score and motion vector struggle to identify frequently shaky camerawork, and a shaky video would be split by SBD into a large number of false-positive shots, so "shots detected per second" is used as a jitter proxy metric, removing videos exceeding 0.85 shots/second. ③ Following Panda-70M's approach, if a clip's start point coincides with the very beginning of the full video, the first few seconds are discarded (openings often contain unstable camerawork or transition effects).

### Quality filtering (aesthetic score, sharpness, OCR text filtering, black-bar/watermark/logo detection)

Visual filtering has 6 filters in total, with per-item details given in Appendix J.1 (model and threshold):
· Resolution: removed if minimum width or height <720px (768px is mentioned for the high-resolution stage).
· Aspect ratio: filtered to achieve a target mixture of 60% landscape/40% portrait (80%/20% for the HD set).
· OCR text filtering: an in-house OCR model adaptively samples frames → detects words → recognizes word text; only videos where "word-detection score × word-recognition score <0.6 across all sampled frames" are kept, used to remove material with excessive subtitles/decorative text/overlay text. This step is one of the harshest cuts in the funnel (7%→1.94%).
· Black-bar/border detection: an in-house lightweight detector, first using the first derivative to find positions with large pixel differences in the vertical/horizontal directions, then a scanline algorithm to locate the border; the motivation is that training data with borders would cause the generated video to also have black bars, especially common with portrait material.
· Aesthetic score: applies the publicly available LAION aesthetics image model to the middle frame of each clip, removing scores <4.0, used to remove blurry clips with severe compression artifacts; the paper specifically compares this — computing the average aesthetic score across multiple frames did not significantly improve recall for low-quality clips, so only the middle frame is used (to save compute).
· Visual effects and image quality: several additional lightweight visual models were trained, outputting frame-level "visual aesthetics, visual quality, large borders, visual effects" prediction signals used for filtering.
· Opening segment: removes the first few seconds of clips overlapping with the video's opening.
Additional steps for the SFT stage: uses the Detic object-detection model to remove videos where the subject is too small; manual review of whether the frame is cluttered, whether colors are over-saturated, and whether there is overlaid text or editing effects.
The audio side's video-quality filtering is lighter: OCR removal of videos with text, removal of static videos, removal of videos with resolution <480px.

### Motion filtering (optical-flow/motion-score thresholds, removal of static and shaky footage)

Four steps in series: ① an internal static-video detection model directly removes videos with no motion at all; ② FFmpeg's VMAF motion score and motion vector determine "reasonable motion," with the quantitative threshold given in the appendix being motion score > 2.0, motion vector mean > 0.5 and < 7 (the lower bound removes slow-motion/near-static footage, the upper bound removes footage with excessively violent motion); ③ PySceneDetect's Shot Boundary Detection identifies frequently shaky camerawork — shaky video gets mistakenly split into a large number of shots, so a threshold of "detected shots per second ≥ 0.85" is used to remove it, the motivation being that shaky training data causes the generated video's frame to also shake; ④ removes videos with special motion effects, such as slideshow-style videos. The overall approach follows the low-motion automated filtering idea of Emu Video (Girdhar et al., 2024). The SFT stage uses a stricter motion threshold and manually confirms "non-trivial motion present and no camera shake."

### Deduplication method (exact deduplication and embedding-based semantic deduplication recorded separately)

Two tiers, roughly corresponding to the "exact/semantic" split requested by the field:
· Perceptual/near-duplicate deduplication: uses similarity in a copy-detection embedding space (SSCD, Pizzi et al., 2022) to remove perceptually duplicate clips; the appendix Table 44 gives the threshold "embedding cosine similarity < 0.99 retained," a step that drops the funnel from 1.22% to 1.15% (about 6% removed). This same method is also used for the visual deduplication of Movie Gen Audio's pretraining data.
· Semantic-level deduplication and balancing: a video-text joint-embedding model extracts semantic embeddings and clusters them into fine-grained concept clusters, first merging duplicate clusters, then sampling from each cluster at 1/sqrt(cluster size). This step is both semantic de-redundancy and concept balancing, further dropping 1.15% to 0.94%.
· The evaluation side also cares about duplication: the paper notes that significant duplicate or near-duplicate content was found between the train/eval splits of certain public datasets (e.g., versions with a static watermark or text added).

### VLM/LLM as quality inspector (multimodal-large-model quality scoring and mismatch removal — the 2026 trend from shallow scorers toward large-model semantic judgment)

Movie Gen is representative of the "2024 paradigm": data quality inspection relies almost entirely on a set of dedicated lightweight discriminative models/classifiers and scorers, rather than using a general-purpose VLM/LLM for holistic semantic quality judgment and image-text mismatch removal; the large model used here (LLaMa3-Video) plays the role of captioning (caption generation) rather than judge. The specific model inventory:
Visual side — an in-house OCR model (dual word-detection + word-recognition scores); an in-house black-bar detector (non-learned); the LAION aesthetics image aesthetic prediction model; several self-trained frame-level visual models (aesthetics/image quality/large borders/visual effects); an internal static-video detection model; PySceneDetect and FFmpeg (scene/motion); a copy-detection embedding model (SSCD); a video-text joint-embedding model (for clustering and k-NN retrieval); a 16-class camera-movement classifier; the Detic object-detection model (removing small subjects); the ArcFace face-recognition model (PT2V identity consistency, inter-frame threshold 0.5, synthetic-reference-image threshold 0.7); face-detection and face-region segmentation models.
Audio side — the AED audio event detection model (AudioSet 527-class ontology); CAVTP, a contrastive audio-video-text pretraining model (outputting audio-video cosine similarity, used for diegetic determination and bucketing); an audio-quality prediction model (outputting a continuous 1–10 score, annotated in a manner modeled on LAION aesthetics); a general audio-captioning model; a music-captioning model; a cinematic audio-video classifier.
Notably, the audio quality score is not used as a hard filtering threshold, but is instead written into the caption as a controllable condition (specifying quality 7.0/8.0 at inference time) — a "turning judge scores into a condition" approach. Semantic-level judgment at the post-training stage, on the other hand, is handed to human annotators rather than a large model.

## Captioning Approach

### Captioning model used (in-house VLM/open-source model, model scale)

Video side: uses LLaMa3-Video (Dubey et al., 2024) as the captioning model, fine-tuning two scale variants, 8B and 70B, separately for the video-captioning task, then using them to label the entire training clip set. The scale mixture is explicit: 70% of the captions in the training set come from the 8B model, 30% from the 70B model (balancing cost against quality). This is a native video-captioning model (directly consuming video), rather than per-frame image captioning stitched together.
Auxiliary model: a self-trained 16-class camera-movement classifier, with high-confidence predictions prepended to the caption.
Audio side: uses multiple models working together to generate a synthesized caption — an audio-quality prediction model (outputting a 1–10 score), an AED model (determining voice/singing presence and a music posterior probability), a general audio-captioning model (freeform sound description), and a music-captioning model (supplementing mood and genre detail). The paper specifically notes that the music-captioning model is trained mainly on music samples and tends to hallucinate when there is no music, so both the AED's music probability and the music caption are retained as two signals; in practice this combination gives the best controllability.
Post-training-stage captions are manually refined based on the model's output.

### Caption density and structuredness (short/long/dense description, structured fields such as camera movement, style tags)

Video caption characteristics: dense and detailed (detailed generated captions), averaging about 100 words in length, with a unified style of "high detail density + consistent paragraph structure." Structured components include:
· Camera-movement prefix: results from a 16-class camera-movement classification (zoom in / zoom out / push in / pull out / pan right / pan left / truck right / truck left / tilt up / tilt down / pedestal up / pedestal down / arc shot / tracking shot / static shot / handheld shot) are prepended to the caption when confidence is high, allowing users to explicitly specify camera movement at inference time.
· FPS token: frame rate is added to the caption as a token, achieving sampled-frame-rate control from 16–32 FPS.
· Fields manually completed at the SFT stage: camera control, character expressions, subject and background information, detailed motion description, and lighting information; 6 additional shot-type/framing classes were newly annotated (wide angle, close-up, aerial, low angle, over the shoulder, first person view), for a total of 22 camera-related tags.
· Inference-side alignment: because users' actual prompts are typically fewer than 10 words, differing greatly in length and style from the training captions, an inference prompt rewrite was specifically designed to rewrite short prompts into the training-caption style.
The audio caption is a highly templated, structured four-segment format (see joint_av_caption_schema).

### Joint audio-video caption structure (whether both visual and auditory tracks are covered, whether split into independent fields — e.g., LTX-2's full soundscape description, Script-a-Video's factorized streams, Foley-Omni's three fields)

Movie Gen has no unified joint audio-video caption schema — visual and auditory belong to two independent data streams: Movie Gen Video's caption is purely visual and does not describe sound at all; Movie Gen Audio's caption is purely auditory and does not describe the picture (visual information is fed directly as a condition via video embedding, with text serving only as a supplement).
However the audio caption itself is a structured, factorized four-field template (Table 27), which can be viewed as a "factorized schema within the auditory track":
① Audio quality: "This audio has quality: 8.0." (a continuous 1–10 score given by the audio-quality prediction model, annotated in a manner modeled on LAION aesthetics)
② Presence of voice and music: "This audio does not contain speech. This audio does not contain vocal singing." + "This audio contains music with a 0.90 likelihood." (speech/singing use AED posterior-thresholded binary outputs; music, due to inherent ambiguity — e.g. a riser in film sounds like both a sound effect and music — is kept as a continuous posterior probability)
③ Sound description: "This audio has a description: 'gentle waves lapping against the shore, and music plays in the background.'" (a freeform description from the general audio-captioning model)
④ Music-style description: "This audio has a music description (if applicable): 'A beautiful, romantic, and sentimental jazz piano solo.'" (mood and genre output by the music-captioning model; this field is unconditionally appended regardless of whether the sample contains music, working together with ②'s music probability to achieve the best controllability)
The paper emphasizes "prioritizing video alignment over text alignment," because text is only supplementary, cannot cover all the detail in the video, and text is not visible to the final audience.

### Dialogue transcription and speaker attribute annotation (ASR transcription + speaker identity/language/accent/emotion)

None. Movie Gen Audio does not perform ASR transcription and does not annotate speaker identity, language, accent, or emotion — because the model deliberately does not generate speech: diegetic speech is difficult to generate without a dialogue script and when generated video has artifacts; non-diegetic speech (narration) is considered substitutable by TTS given a script. All processing of speech is limited to: using an AED model (the speech/singing subclasses of the AudioSet ontology) to determine whether voice and singing are present in a sample, with the output binarized against a preset posterior threshold and written into the caption as a control label; and using voice as one of three major audio types during data bucketing to participate in the CAVTP threshold determination. The cinematic split used for fine-tuning goes further and directly excludes clips containing vocals entirely. The paper's limitations section explicitly acknowledges that the model "currently does not support voice generation, due to a design choice."

### Geometric and structured annotation (camera parameters, depth, 3D point tracks, action annotation, explicit state)

Dominated by semantic camera annotation, with no explicit geometric quantity annotation:
· Camera movement: a self-trained classifier predicts 16 classes of camera movement (push/pull/pan/tilt/pedestal, arc, tracking, static, handheld), with high-confidence predictions prepended to the caption; the SFT stage additionally manually annotated 6 classes of shot type and framing (wide angle, close-up, aerial, low angle, over the shoulder, first person view). This is the most central structured-annotation system in the entire paper.
· Action/behavior: a taxonomy of 600 human verbs and expressions was built, used for zero-shot text→video retrieval and text k-NN retrieval, indirectly constituting an action-category annotation.
· Face/identity: PT2V data uses a face detector (extracting frames per second), ArcFace identity similarity (>0.5 between adjacent frames judged as the same person persisting throughout), and face-region cropping and segmentation (to prevent the model from attending to the background or other non-key regions); synthetic reference images with ArcFace <0.7 are removed.
· Objects: SFT uses Detic object detection to judge whether the subject is too small.
· Motion intensity: FFmpeg motion score and motion vector serve as scalar motion-quantity values; evaluation prompts are additionally manually labeled with high/medium/low motion-level tags.
No camera intrinsics/extrinsics, depth maps, 3D point tracks, or optical-flow fields are used as explicit geometric annotation [confirmed not adopted].

### Synthetic data construction (controlled perturbation/editing to construct training pairs, e.g., InstructAV2AV)

Synthetic data is used extensively and systematically, but concentrated on the two capabilities of personalization and editing, with the T2V backbone still using real data:
· Personalization (PT2V) cross-paired synthesis: directly training on paired data would let the model learn a copy-paste shortcut (the generated person always replicates the reference image's expression and head pose, staring directly at the camera). Beyond collecting O(10)K real cross-pairs (taken from different-camera-angle clips of the same scene in the pretraining data), a pretrained personalization image-generation model (He et al., 2024b) is used to generate a reference image for the first frame of each video in the paired data, varying expression, head pose, lighting, etc. through diverse prompts; ArcFace similarity <0.7 removes generated images with identity drift, ultimately yielding O(1)M synthetic cross-paired samples, used specifically in the third pretraining stage to improve naturalness.
· Video editing (Movie Gen Edit) three-stage synthesis: the paper's core claim is that SOTA video-editing capability is achieved "without relying on any supervised video-editing data." Stage one is multi-task training, alternating between treating image editing as single-frame video editing and text-to-video generation; stage two introduces two synthetic multi-frame editing tasks — Animated Frame Editing (applying random affine transforms to image-edit sample pairs to generate "animated" multi-frame edit pairs) and Generative Instruction-Guided Video Segmentation — fine-tuned jointly with text-to-video multi-task training for about a thousand steps; stage three transplants the NLP idea of backtranslation into video editing: the stage-two model is used to generate edited video, which after filtering is used to construct triplets (a noisy generated edited video x̂, a reverse editing instruction c_instruct-bwd, a clean real video x), training the model to recover the clean real video from the noisy edited result, thereby training on multi-frame, high-quality real output videos. Ablation shows backtranslation clearly outperforms standard fine-tuning.

### Human-in-the-loop degree (manual annotation, manual QC, model pre-screen + manual review)

Pretraining is essentially fully automated (human involvement limited to threshold determination, e.g. the audio CAVTP threshold "determined by manual inspection," and the aesthetic threshold verified manually); post-training, in contrast, is a heavily human-involved paradigm of "model pre-screen + manual review + manual relabeling":
· The last two of the video SFT's four stages rely entirely on humans: the third stage has humans judge cinematic feel (angled or natural light or studio lighting; vivid but not over-saturated color; an uncluttered frame; non-trivial motion; no camera shake; no editing effects or overlaid text) — the paper explicitly states that "many aspects of high-quality fine-tuning data cannot be reliably captured by automated filters with both high precision and high recall"; in the same stage, annotators also manually crop the videos to the target duration, actively selecting the most compelling, most impactful segment from the whole. The fourth stage has humans correct erroneous details and add missing key information (camera control, character expression, subject and background, detailed motion, lighting) on top of the LLaMa3-Video-generated caption, and additionally annotate the 6 shot-type classes.
· Personalization SFT: starting from single-person videos filtered from the T2V fine-tuning set, humans select videos with diverse actions to cover a wide range of action behaviors, ultimately yielding O(1000) videos.
· Audio SFT: after automated filtering by the cinematic audio-video classifier + AED, human annotation performs the final selection.
· The evaluation side relies almost entirely on human A/B testing (the paper argues that automated metrics are unreliable), with annotators specially trained and continuously audited; the same batch of 381 prompts was also annotated 4 times to estimate the standard deviation of each evaluation dimension (overall quality 5.07%, frame consistency 4.08%, etc.), used to judge whether a win is statistically significant.

## Audio-Video Alignment

### Audio-video synchronization-detection method (lip sync, event alignment)

No lip-sync detection is performed (the model does not generate speech, so SyncNet/LSE-C-type lip-sync metrics are entirely inapplicable). The core detection of audio-video correlation is CAVTP (contrastive audio-video-text pre-training): computing the cosine similarity between the audio embedding and the video embedding, used as a proxy score for "how likely this audio is diegetic" — because CAVTP is trained mainly on data dominated by diegetic sound, on-screen audio that matches the visual content has an audio-video embedding distance that is closer. This score, combined with the AED type label, forms the bucketing basis for the pretraining data (Table 24).
The paper also breaks the audio-video correspondence relationship into three difficulty tiers from a modeling perspective: ① on-screen diegetic sound — the audio-visual correspondence is extremely strong, with what sound occurs at what moment being deterministic, requiring strong video understanding and dense action recognition (e.g., a golf club hitting a ball); ② off-screen diegetic sound — requires understanding what environments produce what sounds and the logical order between events (e.g., birdcalls in a forest, a crowd cheering after a difficult move is completed), requiring stronger reasoning; ③ off-screen/non-diegetic sound — only semantically related (background music must match mood, a riser is used to build tension), requiring modeling human emotion beyond the physics of the world.
Evaluation-side uses a human evaluation of "diegetic sound synchronization" together with the objective ImageBind score metric. The limitations section acknowledges: when actions are dense (tap dancing), the target is small or occluded (footsteps), or fine-grained visual understanding is needed (identifying guitar chords), the generated audio will be out of sync.

### Specific synchronization metrics and thresholds (SyncNet/Synchformer/LSE/in-house, threshold values, e.g., MOVA's LSE-D≤9.5 and LSE-C≥4.5, SkyReels-V4's SyncNet |offset|≤3∧conf>1.5)

Uses the cosine similarity of the in-house CAVTP audio-video embeddings as the sole quantitative alignment metric, with thresholds given by audio-type tier (Table 24, all determined by manual inspection):
· AED = sound / voice / voice+sound → cosine similarity > 0.2 judged diegetic
· AED = music / music+sound / voice+music+sound → cosine similarity > 0.3 judged diegetic (a higher bar for the music category, because music's natural correlation with the visuals is weaker and more prone to misjudgment)
· AED = music and similarity < 0.1 → judged non-diegetic (background score)
· AED = sound+music and 0.1 < similarity < 0.25 → judged non-diegetic
· AED = sound+voice+music and 0.1 < similarity < 0.25 → judged mixed (a mix of on-screen and off-screen)
Pretraining retains only the diegetic and mixed buckets, plus a small amount of non-diegetic background music.
No public synchronization metric such as SyncNet or AV-align is used; on the evaluation side, objective metrics use the ImageBind score (audio-video alignment) and CLAP score (audio-text alignment), while the subjective metric is further broken into synchronization (Sync.) and correctness (Corr.).
Other related thresholds: PT2V uses an ArcFace cosine similarity of > 0.5 (same person across adjacent frames) and > 0.7 (identity preservation for a synthetic reference image).

### Separate handling of temporal vs. semantic synchronization (temporal alignment and content-semantic matching as two independent filtering conditions)

Conceptually a clear three-layer separation is made, but in practice, data filtering approximates the distinction using the same combination of CAVTP score + AED label, without training two independent temporal/semantic scorers:
· Conceptual layer: the paper splits the audio-video relationship into three layers — "on-screen (deterministic temporal correspondence, the strongest temporal constraint)," "on-screen/off-screen mix (environmental plausibility and causal event ordering, semantic + weak temporal)," and "non-diegetic (emotional and narrative semantics, no temporal constraint)" — noting that the demands on model capability rise across these three layers in turn.
· Data layer: the diegetic/non-diegetic division is in essence a division of "whether there is a temporal-causal constraint," realized via the CAVTP threshold; the audio-type division (voice/music/sound) carries the semantic-content dimension. Crossing the two produces Table 23's six-cell classification, effectively managing the data mixture along two orthogonal axes of "temporal alignability" and "semantic category."
· Evaluation layer separation is the most thorough: diegetic sound correctness (should this sound occur at all — semantic matching) and diegetic sound synchronization (does it occur at the right time — temporal alignment) are split into two independent human-evaluation dimensions; the non-diegetic side is likewise split into music mood alignment (emotional semantics) and music motion/scene alignment (alignment with action/scene/editing cut points).

### Audio quality filtering (SNR, silence detection and silence-ratio thresholds, no-audio-track removal, off-screen sound-source removal, background-music separation)

· Silence handling: after AED labeling, videos whose dominant class is silence are discarded directly; no specific numerical threshold for silence proportion is reported.
· Audio quality: an audio-quality prediction model was trained, outputting a continuous 1–10 score (annotated in a manner modeled on LAION aesthetics' collection process, with 10 being the highest). The key design is that this score is not used as a hard filtering threshold, but is instead written into the caption as a controllable condition — the inference-time condition is set to quality 7.0 for pure-sound-effect generation and 8.0 for joint sound-effect+music generation. Ablation shows that a higher conditioning quality score leads to a higher predicted quality score, but subjective preference saturates after about 6.5 (samples containing non-diegetic music can still continue to benefit at higher scores).
· Off-screen/irrelevant sound removal: low CAVTP similarity is used to identify and route non-diegetic audio out of the main pretraining set; the cinematic split used for fine-tuning explicitly pursues the professional mixing characteristic of "suppressing ambient noise and irrelevant off-screen sound."
· Definition of cinematic-grade audio quality: the paper summarizes the gap between cinematic original sound and low-end device recordings (phone, surveillance camera) into two aspects — sound quality (how it sounds, requiring professional microphone recording + mixing and mastering, removing pop noise and wind noise) and sound design (what sounds to include, highlighting narratively relevant events such as explosions/dialogue, with ambient music mixed in with fade-in/fade-out) — bridged using a cinematic-feel classifier + AED for automated screening plus manual annotation.
· Vocal removal: the cinematic split excludes clips containing vocals.
· Associated visual-side filtering: removes videos where OCR detects text, videos that are static, and videos with resolution <480px, to reduce visual-modality noise.
· No SNR threshold is reported, and no background-music separation (BGM separation) is performed — on the contrary, non-vocal music is deliberately retained and modeled as one of the generation targets.

### Classification and separate handling strategy for speech/sound effects/music

Classification: AED (the AudioSet 527-class ontology) maps each sample to one of three types — voice (the speech + singing subclasses), non-vocal music (the music subclass), and general sound (everything else) — allowing multiple labels to coexist; crossed with diegetic/non-diegetic, this produces Table 23's six cells (e.g., diegetic voice = live speech, non-diegetic voice = narration; diegetic music = live performance, non-diegetic music = background score; diegetic sound = waves crashing, non-diegetic sound = canned laughter and a riser).
Handling strategy:
· Not split by model in modeling — the paper explicitly chooses to "use a single model to jointly generate all audio categories," reasoning that correlations exist not only between video and audio but also among different audio categories (if generated separately, sound effects and music could not be harmoniously mixed).
· Mixture-wise, thresholds and quotas are adjusted by category: general sound is given the highest priority (low-level physical regularities are hardest to learn, and errors are most jarring), at an order of magnitude about 10× higher than the other categories; the diegetic-determination threshold for music is raised to 0.3; the voice category is excluded from the generation target (used only as a presence label).
· Caption-wise, control is split by field: speech/singing use a binary label, music uses a continuous probability, general sound effects use a freeform description, and music style is a separate segment appended unconditionally.
· Fine-tuning-wise, split by data source: cinematic-grade audio-video (containing both diegetic sound and off-screen ambient/theme music) + high-quality music-only O(10)K hours without video and sound-effect-only O(10)K hours, the latter being more readily available and used to raise audio quality. Ablation shows that adding large-scale text-sound-effect and text-music paired data lets the model effectively disentangle different audio types.

## Training Coordination

### Multi-stage training curriculum and data curriculum scheduling (basis for stage division: resolution/duration/quality score/modality; low-res→high-res, image→video, short→long)

· Movie Gen Video's three-step curriculum (with 3 progressively stricter data subsets on the data side): ① first train pure text-to-image (T2I) as a warmup, then jointly train T2I + T2V (training jointly from scratch directly converges significantly slower, and under equal GPU-hours both visual and temporal quality are worse); ② progressive resolution, training from 256px low resolution up to 768px high resolution, switching the data from the "minimum width/height 720px low-resolution set" to the "minimum width/height 768px high-resolution set"; ③ continuous training with an improved dataset and optimization recipe (continuous training under compute and time constraints). A validation set of unseen videos is maintained during training; validation loss correlates strongly with human-evaluated visual quality, and the learning rate is reduced once loss plateaus. Bucketing (5 duration buckets × 5 aspect-ratio buckets, with consistent latent shapes within a bucket) enables batched training with variable length and shape, with the FPS token providing a frame-rate curriculum. Up to 6,144 H100s are used (700W TDP, 80GB HBM3, the Grand Teton platform).
· PT2V three stages: Stage-I identity injection (truncating TAE embedding to 8 latent frames, i.e., 64 RGB frames, training only the backbone with the visual encoder frozen, using paired samples to quickly inject identity) → Stage-II expanding to long videos → Stage-III using cross-paired (real + synthetic) samples to eliminate the copy-paste effect and improve the naturalness of expression and motion.
· Movie Gen Edit three stages: single-frame editing multi-task → synthetic multi-frame editing → backtranslation video editing.
· Movie Gen Audio two stages: pretraining (sequence cap 30 seconds/750 tokens, batch of 1536 sequences, 500K steps, 384 GPUs × 14 days, constant lr 1e-4 + 5K-step warmup) → fine-tuning (batch 256, 50K steps, 64 GPUs × 1 day, lr linearly raised to 1e-4 then linearly decayed to 1e-8, EMA decay 0.999).

### Data mixture change across training stages (pretraining/annealing/SFT high-quality subset)

· Video pretraining: the low-resolution stage uses the 720px threshold set (aspect ratio 60% landscape/40% portrait); the high-resolution stage switches to the stricter 768px set with newly augmented data, with the mixture adjusted to 80% landscape/20% portrait and ≥60% containing people — i.e., as stages progress, the resolution threshold, the aesthetic/motion threshold, and the people proportion all tighten simultaneously, a typical "quality monotonically increases by stage" data curriculum.
· Caption mixture: across the full training set, 70% of captions come from LLaMa3-Video 8B, 30% from the 70B model.
· Video SFT: the data is entirely switched to a manually curated set, with duration limited to 10.6–16 seconds (50% exactly at 16 seconds), and the training frame rate switched by duration (16 seconds trained at 16FPS, 10.6–16 seconds at 24FPS), so the model supports both 10-second and 16-second output; multiple SFT models are trained (with different versions of fine-tuning data, different hyperparameters, different pretraining checkpoints) and then combined via model averaging (following LLaMa3) to merge the different strengths of each model in motion, consistency, and camera control.
· PT2V SFT: paired and real cross-paired reference images are mixed at a 1:1 ratio.
· Audio fine-tuning: cinematic-grade audio-video (Cin-AV) and high-quality audio-only (HQ-A) are mixed at a 10-batch : 1-batch ratio; 10-second chunks and 30-second chunks are mixed at a 5-batch : 1-batch ratio.
· Audio training's conditional dropout also constitutes a form of "modality mixture": the overall condition (video + text + audio context) is fully dropped with probability 0.2, while text and video are each independently dropped with probability 0.1 to reduce over-dependence on any single modality; masked audio is fully masked (pure generation) with probability 0.5, otherwise 75%–100% is masked (audio extension).

### Post-training data (scale and selection criteria of the curated SFT set, number of preference pairs and annotation method, reward-model training data) ⚠️

· Video SFT set: the goal is a high-quality video set with "good motion, strong realism, good aesthetics, broad concept coverage, and high caption quality." Produced from the large-scale pool via four stages (automated strict threshold → concept-balanced k-NN → manual selection of cinematic feel → manual captioning), leaving "several million but conceptually unbalanced" clips after the first stage; the final scale is undisclosed by the paper [Uncertain], but training uses only 512 H100s, an extremely small scale. Duration is 10.6–16 seconds, with 50% at 16 seconds.
· PT2V SFT set: O(1000) high-quality single-person videos, manually selected to ensure diversity of human actions, with paired and real cross-paired reference images at 1:1.
· Audio SFT set: the cinematic split (professionally produced, containing both diegetic sound and off-screen ambient/theme music, excluding clips with vocals, automatically screened by the cinematic-feel classifier + AED then manually selected) is O(100)K samples / O(1)K hours; the high-quality audio split (video-free high-quality music O(10)K hours + sound effects O(10)K hours) is O(1,000)K samples / O(10)K hours.
· No preference data used: throughout the pipeline there is no preference-pair annotation, no reward model, and no RLHF/DPO; post-training is entirely SFT + model averaging. Human-evaluation data is used only for assessment, not for training.

### Data-processing infrastructure and throughput (NeMo Curator/Data-Juicer/in-house, GPU speedup ratio, processing scale, cost) ⚠️

[Uncertain] The paper does not disclose the data-processing-side infrastructure, framework (no mention of NeMo Curator / Data-Juicer, etc.), GPU speedup ratio, per-unit throughput, or cost. The only inferable information from the text is the tool-stack composition: open-source tools include FFmpeg (scene detection, VMAF motion score, motion vector) and PySceneDetect (shot boundary detection), with the rest being a series of Meta internal models (OCR, static-video detection, aesthetics, copy-detection embedding, video-text joint embedding, camera-movement classifier, AED, CAVTP, audio quality scoring, audio/music captioning, etc.). Training-side infrastructure is disclosed thoroughly: up to 6,144 H100s (700W TDP, 80GB HBM3, Grand Teton AI server platform), an in-house PyTorch model-parallel implementation compiled into CUDAGraphs, with an analytical framework modeling compute and communication time to eliminate redundant cross-GPU communication for repeated activations; audio pretraining used 384 GPUs × 14 days, fine-tuning 64 GPUs × 1 day. Given that the acceptance rate under the strictest thresholds is <1%, the compute cost of its data cleaning should be considerable, but the paper gives no numbers.

## Effectiveness Comparison

### Quantified impact of data-strategy ablations (distinguishing: filtering-strictness ablation / caption-density-style ablation / data-mixture ablation, and corresponding evaluation metrics)

The paper gives several groups of quantified ablations directly related to data (all human A/B net win rate, win%−lose%):
[Caption density and style ablation] LLaMa3-Video 8B's native video caption vs. LLaMa3-FramesRewrite (image-captioning the first/middle/last frames then having LLaMa rewrite and merge them). ① Caption quality itself: in human A/B testing, the video caption is preferred 67% of the time, the frame-by-frame rewrite scheme only 15%; ② impact on the generative model: overall prompt alignment improves +10.8%, with the vast majority of the gain coming from motion alignment +10.7%, rising to +16.1% on prompts requiring high motion. The conclusion is that native video captions accurately describe finer-grained motion detail, providing a stronger supervisory signal for training.
[Filtering-strictness / high-quality-SFT ablation (T2V)] fine-tuned model vs. pretrained model (24FPS, 10.6 seconds): overall quality +34.65, frame consistency +8.14, motion completeness +18.38, motion naturalness +10.5, text alignment +9.97.
[High-quality-SFT ablation (audio)] FT vs. PT: overall +41.7±15.3, naturalness +37.8±16.3, professionalism +43.0±14.7, diegetic sound correctness +31.0±16.0, synchronization +24.9±17.7; the paper's comment is that "after fine-tuning the generated results are markedly more cinematic-feeling, highlighting the importance of high-quality data curation at the fine-tuning stage."
[Data-mixture ablation (audio)] Cin-AV + HQ-A vs. Cin-AV only, on the SFX task: overall +21.5±18.7, naturalness +24.3±17.3, professionalism +22.8±18.7, diegetic sound correctness +11.7±17.4, synchronization +9.6±18.1; on the SFX+music task, the improvement in video-music alignment is significant. The explanation is that large-scale text-sound-effect and text-music paired data lets the model effectively disentangle different audio types, correspondingly improving audio-video alignment.
[Synthetic-data ablation (PT2V)] cross-paired training slightly lowers ArcFace identity similarity but yields significantly more diverse head poses and more natural expressions in exchange; the ablation shows that cross-paired data and SFT are both critical to video naturalness, quality, and text alignment.
[Synthetic-data ablation (editing)] backtranslation training outperforms standard fine-tuning at the same stage; multi-task learning (text-to-video + image editing) outperforms training a ControlNet on a frozen T2V model.
[Conditional-quality-score ablation (audio)] raising the conditioning audio quality score continuously improves the objective quality score, with subjective preference saturating at about 6.5 (samples containing non-diegetic music can continue to benefit at higher scores).

### Evidence for quality vs. quantity (cases where small, curated data outperforms large, messy data)

The evidence is very strong, and is the paper's central methodological thesis throughout. ① Pretraining side: under the strictest thresholds, the data acceptance rate is below 1% (0.94% at the end of the funnel) — i.e., only about one percent of the raw pool is selected for high-resolution training — trading an extremely low retention rate for quality. ② Post-training side: video SFT uses only a single manually curated small-scale set (training with only 512 H100s, compared with pretraining's up to 6,144 H100s), yet brings a massive +34.65 net-win-rate improvement in overall quality, one of the largest single-item quality gains in the entire paper. ③ Audio side: pretraining uses O(1,000)K hours (on the order of a million hours) of data, while fine-tuning uses only O(1)K hours of cinematic-grade audio-video + O(10)K hours of high-quality audio-only (together less than 1.1% of pretraining), yet achieves net win rates of +24.9 to +43.0 across all subjective dimensions — an extremely vivid contrast of "small and curated beating large and messy." ④ The paper's conclusion summarizes this recipe directly: "we focus on curating high-quality, large-scale data for pretraining, and relatively smaller-scale but higher-quality data for fine-tuning. This general recipe is effective for improving image, video, and audio generation quality." ⑤ At the same time, the paper also emphasizes the necessity of scale — "scaling data, training compute, and model size together is what brings the significant improvement" — so its stance is "first lay a foundation with large-scale strictly filtered data + then set the tone with an extremely small-scale manually curated dataset," rather than a purely small-data route.

### Training-data domain distribution and evaluation-benchmark category-taxonomy alignment (e.g., VABench's seven major categories)

Partially aligned, and the alignment relationship is traceable:
· Movie Gen Video Bench (1,003 prompts, more than 3× the scale of prior work's prompt sets) sets 5 concept categories: ① human activities (body and mouth movement, emotion, etc.) ② animals ③ nature and landscapes ④ physical phenomena (fluid mechanics, gravity, acceleration, collisions, explosions, etc.) ⑤ unconventional subjects and unconventional actions; each prompt is additionally tagged with one of three motion-level labels — high/medium/low. Categories ①②③ directly correspond to the "humans, nature, animals, objects" domains the training data claims to cover; the motion-level tags correspond one-to-one with the training-side motion-filtering dimensions (motion score / motion vector / jitter rate). Categories ④ physical phenomena and ⑤ unconventional subjects are deliberately set as out-of-distribution tests, used to examine generalization; the training data is not specifically mixed for them.
· The consistency of the weight given to people is the most obvious: the training high-resolution set's ≥60% people-containing requirement + the 600-verb/expression taxonomy for targeted retrieval fully matches the benchmark's listing of human activities as the largest category, with a specific breakdown of "body and mouth movement, emotion."
· Movie Gen Audio Bench's category system corresponds to the two main lines of the audio training data: single-shot video (large in quantity, broad sound-effect coverage, testing the robustness and generalization of diegetic sound effects, corresponding to the absolutely dominant general sound / diegetic data in pretraining) and multi-shot video (taken from short films, containing transitions and stronger narrative, testing video-music alignment, when music enters, how it evolves with the plot, whether it aligns with editing cut points, and how harmoniously sound effects and music are mixed, corresponding to the cinematic split and non-diegetic music data in fine-tuning). The evaluation metric system (three major axes of audio quality / video alignment / text alignment, with video alignment further split into diegetic sound correctness, diegetic sound synchronization, non-diegetic music mood alignment, and non-diegetic music motion/scene alignment) is highly isomorphic to the data-side two-dimensional classification system of AED × CAVTP.
· The video side does not adopt an external unified category system such as VABench's seven categories; both benchmarks are self-built by Meta and open-sourced alongside the paper.
