## Basic Information

### Name

Merged Survey of Mainstream Video Pretraining Datasets: Panda-70M, InternVid, Koala-36M, MiraData, OpenVid-1M, UltraVideo, LVD-2M (7 public video-text datasets in total, spanning 2023-2025, with emphasis on comparing filtering methods and captioning strategies)

### Publishing Institution/Company

Panda-70M: Snap Inc. (Snap Research) + University of California, Merced (UC Merced). InternVid: Shanghai AI Laboratory / OpenGVLab (jointly with Nanjing University, University of Hong Kong, Nanyang Technological University, Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology). Koala-36M: Kuaishou (repository now under KlingAIResearch, formerly KwaiVGI) + Shenzhen University + Tsinghua University. MiraData: Tencent PCG ARC Lab + Chinese University of Hong Kong. OpenVid-1M: Nanjing University PCA Lab + ByteDance + Nankai University. UltraVideo: Zhejiang University APRIL Lab + Shanghai Jiao Tong University + Huazhong University of Science and Technology + Nanyang Technological University. LVD-2M: University of Hong Kong + ByteDance.

### Release Date (technical report/paper/open-source release)

Panda-70M: first arXiv release February 29, 2024 (arXiv:2402.19479), CVPR 2024; desirability filtering and shot-boundary annotations added in October 2024. InternVid: v1 on July 13, 2023, v2 on January 4, 2024 (arXiv:2307.06942), ICLR 2024 spotlight. Koala-36M: v1 on October 10, 2024, v2 on April 26, 2025 (arXiv:2410.08260), CVPR 2025. MiraData: July 8, 2024 (arXiv:2407.06358, v1 only), NeurIPS 2024 D&B; v1 data released July 10, 2024, v0 beta earlier. OpenVid-1M: released July 1, 2024 (arXiv:2407.02371), accepted at ICLR 2025 (January 2025); OpenVidHD-0.4M added as a separate download package on May 30, 2025. UltraVideo: June 16, 2025 (arXiv:2506.13691, v1 only), NeurIPS 2025 D&B poster. LVD-2M: arXiv October 14, 2024, data released October 15 (arXiv:2410.10816), NeurIPS 2024 D&B.

### Type (model/dataset/toolchain/evaluation benchmark)

All are "datasets," but each comes with a toolchain and companion models to varying degrees: Panda-70M ships splitting code + a distilled captioning student model; InternVid ships the ViCLIP video-text model; Koala-36M ships transition-detection code + a VTSS scoring model (simplified version); MiraData ships a GPT-4V labeling script + the MiraBench evaluation benchmark (also qualifying as an "evaluation benchmark"); OpenVid-1M ships the MVDiT generative model and training code; UltraVideo ships the UltraWan-1K/4K generative models (LoRA); LVD-2M ships only a YouTube download script. None of the seven has released a commercial-grade generative model.

### Degree of Open Source (whether weights/code/data/pipeline are each open) ⚠️

The key differentiator is "whether the video content itself is hosted" — this is the watershed for reproduction cost.
【Metadata only (URL + timestamp + caption); videos must be crawled independently】Panda-70M (CSV, Google Drive, includes matching_score/desirability/shot_boundary columns); InternVid (jsonlines, 40.9GB on HF, gated — requires filling in name/institution/email); Koala-36M (10 CSV shards totaling 48.9GB, no dataset card and no license tag on HF); MiraData (4 CSVs: 330K/93K/42K/9K); LVD-2M (3 CSVs totaling ~5.08GB, hosted on S3).
【Actually hosts the video content itself】OpenVid-1M (HF nkp37/OpenVid-1M, ~12.4TB, 74+ zip shards, those over 50GB need cat to reassemble; OpenVidHD-0.4M separately ~4.5TB); UltraVideo (HF APRIL-AIGC/UltraVideo, clips_short_{1..36}.zip in native resolution + 1920/960 downsampled versions). These two are the only ones among the seven where pixels can be obtained directly.
【Degree to which cleaning code is open】Koala-36M open-sourced the complete transition-detection implementation (including the fitted SVM coefficients) and VTSS inference code, but explicitly states the released version is a "base version" — the actually tested configuration is a fragments-only FAST-VQA architecture (PLCC 0.8684), not the full model with a ConvNeXt static branch + WCGB described in the paper (PLCC 0.8974), meaning **the released scorer is not the one used to build the data**; MiraData open-sourced the GPT-4V labeling prompt and MiraBench evaluation code, but **the filtering thresholds are said by the paper to be in supplementary material that in fact does not exist**; LVD-2M did not open-source any filtering code at all, only showing the PLLaVA prompt in a paper figure; UltraVideo open-sourced only inference code — training and cleaning pipelines were never released (GitHub issue #8 asked repeatedly without response); Panda-70M open-sourced splitting code (cutscene_detect/event_stitching/video_splitting) and the student captioning model weights, but not the teacher-model inference scripts or the UMT selector weights; InternVid open-sourced ViCLIP, with the cleaning pipeline described only in text; OpenVid-1M open-sourced MVDiT training/inference code and checkpoints at 256-1024 resolution.
【Licenses】Panda-70M: Snap non-commercial research license + inherits the HD-VILA-100M license; InternVid: CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0; Koala-36M: Kuaishou non-commercial research license + inherits the HD-VILA-100M license; MiraData: code under GPL-3.0, data terms are self-contradictory (the README's "License Agreement" section prohibits all commercial use, but the same README's final sentence says it "is supported for commercial usage"); OpenVid-1M: CC-BY-4.0 but states it is for research and non-commercial use only, and must comply with the respective upstream licenses of Panda/ChronoMagic/CelebvHQ/Open-Sora-Plan; UltraVideo: a custom license-april-lab.txt, non-commercial, prohibits redistribution of raw video, must comply with YouTube ToS and GDPR/CCPA; LVD-2M: no independent LICENSE file, states it is consistent with the HD-VILA license. All seven are non-commercial research licenses (OpenVid's nominally permissive CC-BY-4.0 is narrowed by its own statement), and none actually owns the copyright to the YouTube footage.

### Whether Simultaneous Audio-Video Generation Is Supported, and How (native joint / cascaded / MoE fusion)

All seven datasets **do not support simultaneous audio-video generation** — they are all purely visual + text datasets. Audio handling falls into three tiers: (1) Not touched at all — for InternVid, Koala-36M, MiraData, OpenVid-1M, and LVD-2M, the word "audio" in the papers appears only in reference-list titles (confirmed by full-text grep for Koala-36M and LVD-2M, each appearing exactly once, both in citation titles), and the metadata has no audio field of any kind; (2) Audio track passively retained but unprocessed — Panda-70M's download config panda70m.yaml sets download_audio: True, so the mp4 files users obtain are muxed with the original YouTube audio track, but the dataset itself contains no audio annotation, audio QC, or speech labels, and its teacher model Video-LLaMA explicitly disabled the audio branch ("We only use the vision branch"); UltraVideo retains the native audio track and, in its Limitations section, notes "thanks to the preservation of native resolution, frame rate, and audio ... can be used for tasks such as music generation," but likewise has no audio-side filtering or annotation of any kind; (3) Koala-36M's re-splitting code writes output via cv2.VideoWriter/mp4v, which **directly discards the audio track**. Consequently, all audio and audio-video-alignment fields under this entry (audio_category_distribution, joint_av_caption_schema, dialogue_transcription_attributes, av_sync_detection, sync_metric_and_threshold, temporal_vs_semantic_sync, audio_quality_filtering, audio_type_handling) are "not applicable."

## Data Scale and Distribution

### Training Data Volume (video count / hours / token count, pretraining and SFT reported separately) ⚠️

The seven datasets span four orders of magnitude, and **scale and quality are arranged in a clearly inverse ranking** (listed below in descending order of clip count, per each paper's Table 1; UltraVideo's Table 1 provides a unified cross-check):
1) **InternVid**: 7.1M source videos → **234M clips**, 760.3K hours, 4.1 billion words of caption total, average clip 11.7 seconds, average caption 17.6 words; average source-video length 351.9 seconds. Subsets: InternVid-10M-DIV (diversity-sampled 10 million), InternVid-10M-FLT (drawn from DIV, taking the **top 30%** by UMT-SIM score), InternVid-18M-AES (aesthetic score ≥4, 18 million).
2) **Panda-70M**: 3.79M source long videos → **70,817,169 clips** (per the paper) / **70,723,513** (actual released figure — the ~94K difference is attributed, though unstated by the authors, to harmful-content filtering before release; this is an inference), 166.8K hours, ~36TB, average clip **8.477 seconds**, caption **13.2 words**. Released subsets: 10M (matching_score>0.43 and at most 3 per source video, 10,473,922 clips / 37.0K hours), 2M (exactly 3 per source video drawn from the 10M subset, 800K×3=2,400,000 clips / 7.56K hours), validation/test each 2,000 source videos × 3 clips. **Note that "Panda-2M/Panda-5M" in the paper are random subsets, distinct from the quality-filtered 2M/10M versions released in the repository — a distinction very easy to confuse when citing.**
3) **Koala-36M**: **36M clips**, 172K hours (Table 1), average clip 13.75 seconds, caption **202.1 words**, 720p. **This table has an internal-consistency issue: 36M × 13.75s = 137.5K hours, about 25% off from the stated 172K hours** (UltraVideo's Table 1, when citing it, gives 137K hours / 13.6 seconds — i.e., it adopts the product-based figure). Separately, the paper's abstract repeatedly says "over 10M," but that is a threshold statement made when comparing against MiraData/VidGen/OpenVid (all ≤1M), not a second count.
4) **LVD-2M**: **2M clips** (released as ~2.1M rows), each ≥10 seconds, averaging 20.2 seconds, caption 88.7 words, average optical-flow score 47.8. **Total duration is not given in the paper**; extrapolating from 2M × 20.2s gives approximately **11,200 hours** (UltraVideo's table cites 14.6K hours / 20.2 seconds / 2.1M). Duration distribution: 10-15s ~43.5%, 15-20s ~23%, 20-30s ~20.5%, 30-50s ~11%, >50s ~2.5%.
5) **OpenVid-1M**: **1M clips**, of which **OpenVidHD-0.4M = 433K clips at 1080p**; per UltraVideo's Table 1: OpenVid-1M averages 7.2 seconds, 2.1K hours, caption 126.5 words; OpenVidHD averages 9.6 seconds, 1.2K hours, caption 104.5 words. The HF dataset card shows "1.45M rows" (includes duplicate counting from the HD subset). **Total duration/average duration/FPS were not disclosed in the original paper.**
6) **MiraData**: unfiltered pool **788K**, released as four nested tiers **330K / 93K / 42K / 9K**. Average clip **72.1 seconds** (longest among the seven), caption **318 words**, 720p. **The "16K hours" in Table 1 corresponds to the 788K unfiltered pool (788K × 72.1s ≈ 15,785h), not the 330K release — the 330K, on the same basis, is approximately 6,600 hours**, a point extremely easy to misquote. (The "77k long videos" claimed in an external survey misciting the v0 beta's 57,803 clips / 1,754 hours — do not use.)
7) **UltraVideo**: short split **42,184 clips / 62 hours / average 5.3 seconds / caption 824.2 words**; long split **16,597 clips / 143 hours / average 30.9 seconds / caption 850.3 words**. Only **5,000** source videos. Roughly 2,700× smaller than Koala-36M by hours, but captions are about 4× longer and pixel volume 4-16× higher. **Note that its split naming is short/long, not "UltraVideo-1K/42K"; 1K/4K refers to the output resolution of the UltraWan model.**

### Composition of Data Sources (proprietary / public datasets / web crawling / licensed procurement / synthetic data) ⚠️

**All seven are secondary processing of public sources — none uses a proprietary copyrighted library or licensed/procured data** — and there is severe shared lineage (HD-VILA-100M is the common ancestor of at least three of them):
- **Panda-70M**: 100% YouTube, all sourced from **HD-VILA-100M** (Microsoft, 3.3 million YouTube videos, 720p, balanced sampling across 15 popular categories), taking 3.8 million of its long videos and re-splitting them.
- **Koala-36M**: the paper states verbatim "we start from the same raw data with Panda-70M," i.e. **also HD-VILA-100M**, corroborated by the LICENSE pointing to the HD-VILA-100M license. **Not Kuaishou's own short-video corpus**, a point commonly misunderstood.
- **InternVid**: independently crawled from YouTube. Two tracks: a core set of ~2 million from 16 categories of high-quality channels; another ~5.1 million retrieved via **~6,100 action/activity search terms**, a vocabulary formed from the **1,103** action labels of Kinetics/Something-Something/UCF101 plus **5,001** actions extracted by an LLM from visually-grounded corpora and manually verified, referencing the American Time Use Survey (ATUS 2017-2022). Videos already present in public datasets as of April 2023 were excluded from crawling to avoid overlap.
- **MiraData**: four platforms plus one recycled dataset. YouTube — **156 manually curated channels**, ~68K 720p videos (after splitting, ~34K videos → ~173K clips); **HD-VILA-100M recycling** (~100 million clips fed in, only **195K** survived — the authors use this extremely low survival rate to argue for the rigor of their own filtering); Videvo ~63K, Pixabay ~43K, Pexels ~318K. (Footnote 2 of the paper swaps the URLs of Videvo and Pixabay.)
- **OpenVid-1M**: **secondary filtering** from four existing datasets — **Panda-50M/70M (the main source), ChronoMagic, CelebvHQ, Open-Sora-Plan (i.e. the Mixkit/Pexels/Pixabay family)**. CelebvHQ originally had no captions and was captioned by this project. Exact counts per source are not given in tabular form in the paper. [Uncertain]
- **UltraVideo**: **YouTube 4K/8K video pool only** (the original text: "the sole source"). Two recall paths: (a) resolution>4K, frame rate>25FPS, duration>30s re-screening from **Koala-36M**, followed by removal of videos "of no user interest" using **behavioral signals such as view count/likes/comments**, then uniform sampling across categories by similarity between title/description and preset topics, with deduplication; (b) **manual search** for the latest 4K/8K videos using search terms generated by an LLM from 108 topics. A total of 5,000 source videos (ranging from 1 minute to 2 hours), followed by a **second round of manual review** removing low-quality/blurry/watermarked/shaky footage.
- **LVD-2M**: filtered from four existing corpora totaling **220M clips** — **HD-VG-130M (130 million), Panda-70M (70 million), InternVid-38M (38 million), WebVid-10M (10 million)**. The source-selection logic is spelled out clearly: YouTube-sourced material is dynamic enough but requires shot-cut filtering ("only 15% of clips in InternVid exceed 10 seconds, and among these long clips, about 52.5% contain shot transitions"), while stock-footage sources (WebVid) have almost no shot cuts but "nearly half are insufficiently dynamic." Inferred composition by release filename: YouTube-family ~600K (Panda-70M + InternVid mixed in a single file), HDVG ~300K, **WebVid ~1.2 million (nearly 60% of the total)** — this breakdown is not given in the main text of the paper.

### Data Compliance and Provenance (proportion of licensed data, rights-cleared datasets, C2PA, etc.) ⚠️

**The compliance foundation of all seven is fragile, and of varying quality**:
- **A common underlying problem**: the vast majority of the material is YouTube video, and none of the dataset publishers own the copyright — they protect themselves only with the two soft clauses of "non-commercial research" and "takedown available upon infringement claim." Both Panda-70M and MiraData carry explicit takedown commitments ("We will remove the video samples from our dataset as long as you need it"). UltraVideo's license goes further, requiring users to independently comply with YouTube ToS and GDPR/CCPA, and explicitly disclaims any guarantee of ownership of the material — effectively admitting it does not own the YouTube footage.
- **Proportion of licensed data**: the only component that can truly be called rights-cleared is CC0/free-license content from stock-footage sites — MiraData's Videvo/Pixabay/Pexels portion, OpenVid-1M's portion from Open-Sora-Plan (Mixkit/Pexels/Pixabay), and LVD-2M's stock-footage portion via WebVid (though WebVid itself carries Shutterstock watermarks and has already been taken down for copyright issues, making it a high-risk component). **None discloses a numeric proportion of rights-cleared data.**
- **Not distributing the video content itself as an evasion tactic**: Panda-70M / InternVid / Koala-36M / MiraData / LVD-2M — five of the seven — release only URL+timestamp, shifting the legal risk of crawling onto the user. InternVid explicitly states it "follows the practice of existing datasets, sharing only video IDs to comply with YouTube policy." The cost is **irreversible link rot** — the Panda-70M repository directly instructs users to skip samples with status=failed_to_download; LVD-2M's download script even has built-in multi-account rotation (ACCOUNT_NUM) with a warning that Google accounts may get banned. **None has published measured ID-survival-rate data.**
- **The reverse approach**: OpenVid-1M and UltraVideo host the video content directly (12.4TB / full-scale 4K-8K clips), giving the best reproducibility but the greatest legal exposure; UltraVideo closes the loop with "no redistribution of raw video; derivatives must inherit the same terms."
- **Privacy handling**: only **Panda-70M** does one concrete piece of work — using NLTK to replace all person names in captions with "person," and filtering samples containing harmful/violent language, drugs, or hate speech. The other six have no name de-identification and no face-privacy processing.
- **C2PA / watermarking / generated-content provenance**: **none of the seven** has this — there is no provenance mechanism of any kind on the output side.
- **Self-contradictory licenses**: MiraData is the worst offender — the repository is labeled GPL-3.0, and the README's "License Agreement" section prohibits all commercial use, yet the same README's closing sentence says it "is supported for commercial usage." A defensible reading is: code is GPL-3.0, data is for research only, video copyright remains with third parties. Koala-36M's HF page, meanwhile, **has no license tag and no dataset card at all**, with the non-commercial terms existing only in the GitHub repository.

### Clip Duration Distribution and Splitting Strategy ⚠️

**Clip duration is the most fundamental dividing line among the seven, directly corresponding to the trade-off between "short clips are easier to learn from" vs. "long clips are scarce"**, spanning nearly an order of magnitude from 8.5 seconds to 72 seconds:
- **Panda-70M (8.5 seconds, shortest)**: clips under 2 seconds are discarded after splitting; source videos exceeding 60 seconds are truncated to the first 60 seconds; another key step — **10% is trimmed from each end of every clip** to remove unstable camera motion and transition residue. Final average is 8.477 seconds.
- **InternVid (11.7 seconds)**: source videos are limited during crawling to 10 seconds-30 minutes; clip length ranges from 2 seconds to 30+ seconds, with **85% of clips falling in the 0-10 second range**.
- **Koala-36M (13.75 seconds, or 17.2 seconds by the 172K-hour basis)**: clips emerge naturally from transition detection with no fixed-length truncation; the assembly rule requires clips to be longer than **8 frames** to be kept, with **4 frames eroded from each end** (cuts.append((start+4, i-4))) to ensure transition-free content within the clip — the same idea as Panda-70M's 10% edge-trimming, implemented differently.
- **OpenVid-1M (7.2 seconds, OpenVidHD 9.6 seconds)**: the minimum duration threshold is not disclosed. [Uncertain]
- **UltraVideo (short 5.3 seconds / long 30.9 seconds)**: an **explicit dual-split design** — after shot-cutting, clips are routed by frame count, with 3-10 seconds going to the short set and >10 seconds to the long set. To expand the short set, targeted extraction is also performed: for source videos <60 seconds, the **middle 10 seconds** is taken; for those >60 seconds, an additional **10 seconds from each side** is taken. Native frame rate is preserved with no frame subsampling.
- **LVD-2M (20.2 seconds, minimum threshold 10 seconds)**: treats "**≥10 seconds**" as the top design goal (first of four criteria), with no upper truncation, hence a rich long tail (clips >30 seconds account for ~13.5%). Only at captioning time are videos over 30 seconds cut into 30-second segments for separate description.
- **MiraData (72.1 seconds, longest)**: the strategy is the opposite of all other datasets — **not cutting more precisely, but cutting and then stitching back together**. YouTube-sourced clips must be **>40 seconds** to be kept; Videvo/Pixabay/Pexels-sourced clips must be **>10 seconds**. (The v0 version once hard-cut anything over 2 minutes into 2-minute segments; v1 switched to the stitching strategy.) The official 100-clip sample set averages 105.1 seconds in practice, longer than the dataset mean.
**Summary pattern**: three clearly distinguishable routes trade clip length for quality — Panda/InternVid/OpenVid go "short and plentiful," Koala goes "medium-length with clean transitions," and LVD-2M/MiraData/UltraVideo-long go "scarce long-shot resource."

### Resolution/Aspect-Ratio Distribution and Bucketing Strategy ⚠️

**On the resolution dimension, the seven converge heavily on 720p, with UltraVideo and OpenVidHD as the only exceptions**:
- **The 720p tier (five datasets)**: Panda-70M (inherits HD-VILA-100M's all-720p footage; note, however, that **the default download config download_size:360 actually only yields 360p** — 720p requires changing the config, a common pitfall when reproducing); Koala-36M (720p); MiraData (720p, sampled clips measured at 1280×720@30fps); LVD-2M (**the paper never states resolution as a dataset property**, since it does not host video; the download script defaults to --resolution=720p, WebVid shard filenames are labeled 336, and the only resolution figure that appears is a processing parameter — RAFT optical flow computed at 2fps temporally and 520×960 spatially); InternVid (**85% is 720P**, the remaining 15% is 360P-512P; crawling was restricted to 360P-720P).
- **1080p**: OpenVid-1M sets a **minimum resolution threshold of 512×512**, with the OpenVidHD-0.4M subset at 1080p (433K clips). Aspect ratio and FPS distribution are not disclosed in the paper. [Uncertain]
- **4K/8K (UltraVideo, the only one)**: short set has 4K **32,727** clips + 8K **9,457** clips (8K is 22.4%); long set has 4K 12,277 + 8K 4,320. Frame rate is bucketed only into "≤30 FPS" and "≥50 FPS" (short: 31,027 / 11,157; long: 8,146 / 8,451), **31-49 is an empty range**. The HF dataset metadata shows frame sizes of 3,840-7,680 × 1,600-4,320, 12-60 FPS, 36-600 frames per clip. **Native resolution and frame rate are preserved with no transcoding**, which the authors use to argue the dataset can serve frame-interpolation/codec research. Bitrate and encoding format are not mentioned anywhere in the paper.
**Bucketing strategy**: **none of the seven implements resolution/aspect-ratio bucketing for training** — they are datasets, not training frameworks; bucketing is left to downstream users (e.g. Open-Sora) to implement. The UltraWan training accompanying UltraVideo uses fixed sizes (1088×1920×81 frames / 2160×3840×29 frames).
**A notable gap**: **none of the seven** provides aspect-ratio-distribution statistics, and none does targeted supplementation of portrait content (contrast with Open-Sora Plan, which supplements with VIDAL for portrait video) — all implicitly assume 16:9 landscape as the dominant format.

### Category/Domain Distribution and Mixture Strategy (proportion control and concept balancing for people, actions, scenes, styles, etc.) ⚠️

**The seven datasets vary enormously in their level of category governance, from "none at all" to an "explicit taxonomy of 7 themes × 108 subcategories"**:
- **Panda-70M (no category system)**: the paper **has no category pie chart or percentages** — Appendix E only lists 8 categories used for illustrative examples (animals, landscapes, food, sports activities, vehicles, tutorials and narratives, news and TV programs, gaming and 3D rendering), with no numbers attached; Figure 13 is a word cloud over 100K captions. The authors proactively admit in the limitations section that, since HD-VILA-100M skews toward "talking-head-dense" content, the **dominant categories are actually news, TV programs, documentaries, first-person videos, and instructional/narrative videos**. For a category distribution, one can only cite the balanced 15-category sampling of the upstream HD-VILA-100M.
- **Koala-36M (no category system)**: sharing the same source as HD-VILA-100M, the paper likewise gives no category distribution.
- **InternVid (16 themes, but only a pie chart)**: category distribution is presented solely as a pie chart in Figure 3, with **neither the main text nor the appendix giving a numeric table**, so precise percentages cannot be cited. The citable distribution is source-video duration (≤5 minutes 49%, 5-10 minutes 26%, >20 minutes only 8%) and country of origin (US, UK, Australia, Japan, Korea, China, Russia, France, etc.). The authors proactively state that, for copyright reasons, they **deliberately sparsified or excluded surveillance footage, sports events, movies, and documentaries**.
- **MiraData (7-class YouTube taxonomy, bar chart only)**: (1) 3D engine-rendered scenes, (2) city/landscape walkthroughs, (3) movies, (4) first-person perspective, (5) object creation/physics demonstrations, (6) time-lapse photography, (7) human-motion showcases. **Categories (1) and (3) are deliberately oversampled**, on the grounds of "greater diversity and higher image quality," with the added argument that 3D-engine material aids learning of physically-consistent regularities. **Per-category counts exist only as unlabeled bars in Figure 2** (y-axis 0-12K videos / 0-78K clips), with no numeric table; however, the source column of the metadata CSV actually encodes platform + category (e.g. `youtube, 3D engine-rendered scenes`, `videvo, nature`, etc.), **so the exact composition can be recovered via value_counts** — this is the only viable path in this survey for recovering category composition. Note: **there is no dedicated GTA-V source** — game content is folded into "3D engine-rendered scenes," and the paper deliberately avoids naming specific games (presumably for copyright reasons).
- **LVD-2M (8 classes, precise percentages given, most practically usable)**: uses **BART** to classify captions — landscape 24%, people 23%, transportation 13%, sports 11%, animals 11%, food 9%, gaming 5%, other 4%. This is the **only one of the seven that gives a fully numeric category distribution**.
- **UltraVideo (7 major themes × 108 subcategories, most complete system but numbers missing)**: the taxonomy was derived by "noun statistics over Koala-36M's captions → LLM induction → manual revision and confirmation." The seven major themes are: **video scene, subject, action, temporal event, camera movement, video type, emotion**. This is the only system of the seven to incorporate "camera movement" and "emotion" into the data-mixture dimension, and on that basis performs **uniform sampling per category** (explicitly stated as uniform per-category sampling by topic similarity). **However, the 108 subcategories are never listed in the main text, appearing only as a proportion chart in Figure 4(a)**; GitHub issue #5 specifically asks about the topic-to-video mapping and remains unanswered to date.
- **OpenVid-1M**: no category system disclosed. [Uncertain]
**Overall assessment**: only UltraVideo did the proactive design of "define a taxonomy first, then sample uniformly by category"; MiraData did coarse-grained targeted oversampling by category; LVD-2M only did post-hoc statistics; the other four's category distributions are entirely a passive inheritance of their upstream corpora's distribution. **None of the seven performs long-tail concept balancing or semantic-cluster-based resampling.**

### Audio Category Distribution and Mixture (proportions and control strategy for speech/foley/music/ambient/silence) — a dimension unique to AV models

Not applicable. All seven datasets are purely visual + text datasets that neither generate nor process audio, with no classification, mixture, or statistics for speech/effects/music/ambient sound. Only the video files of Panda-70M and UltraVideo passively retain the original audio track (the former via download_audio:True, the latter by stating it preserves native audio), but neither carries any audio-category annotation or mixture control; Koala-36M's re-splitting code, by contrast, directly discards the audio track.

### Narrative Structure Distribution (single-shot vs. multi-shot, average clip duration, shot-count distribution, whether native audio track is present)

**The seven diverge into two diametrically opposed routes for handling "shot count," which is one of the most valuable comparative dimensions in this survey**:
【Route A: strict single-shot, treating multi-shot content as noise to be removed — six datasets】
- Panda-70M: PySceneDetect shot-cutting + ImageBind feature constraints, aiming for single-shot, semantically consistent clips; but its October 2024 addition of **TransNetV2 shot-boundary annotations** (a list of intervals per clip, length 1 meaning single-shot) **is itself an admission that the original pipeline still leaks multi-shot clips**.
- InternVid: PySceneDetect (threshold 27) for shot-cutting.
- Koala-36M: in-house Color-Struct SVM + 3σ probability gate, with a **recall of 0.9395** (deliberately biased toward recall, "rarely misses transitions"), the most thorough transition removal among the seven.
- OpenVid-1M: a cascaded shot-cut detector splits multi-scene source videos into single-scene clips.
- UltraVideo: PySceneDetect AdaptiveDetector, two passes + DINOv2 first/last-5-frame similarity to catch dissolve transitions.
- LVD-2M: "**long shots with no shot transitions**" is one of its four criteria — it uses a low-sample-rate PySceneDetect that also filters out gradual transitions as if they were hard cuts. Human evaluation shows its long-shot purity is **77.5%** (excluding WebVid) / **86.8%** (including the full WebVid mix), significantly higher than HD-VG's 55.0%, Panda-70M's 50.0%, and InternVid's 47.5% — **these numbers are the most direct third-party evidence that "close to half of clips in mainstream datasets contain a shot transition."** The authors admit the remaining failure mode is "subtle jump cuts," which neither PySceneDetect nor the MLLM can catch.
【Route B: over-split first, then stitch back together, actively preserving long continuous shots — one dataset】
- MiraData: deliberately uses a **low threshold of 26** with PySceneDetect to over-split ("ensuring that all distinct clips are extracted"), then uses **a four-model vote** to stitch adjacent clips back together (see the shot_segmentation field for details). This is the only one of the seven to treat "long-shot scarcity" as a core asset to manage. The authors proactively acknowledge a limitation in the v0 README: "clips depicting the same scene from multiple camera angles are not abundant in this release."
【Average shot count and native audio track】the average shot count of training clips across all seven is 1 (a design goal), so **none carries cross-shot-consistency supervisory signal**, nor can any directly support multi-shot narrative generation. Native audio track: retained only by Panda-70M (optional) and UltraVideo, but neither makes any use of it.
【Average clip duration】see duration_distribution: 8.5s (Panda) / 11.7s (InternVid) / 13.75s (Koala) / 7.2s (OpenVid) / 20.2s (LVD-2M) / 5.3s and 30.9s (UltraVideo dual-split) / 72.1s (MiraData).

### Language/Accent Distribution (data foundation for multilingual lip-sync capability)

At the speech level this is entirely not applicable (no audio modeling). At the text level:
- The captions of all seven are **entirely English-only monolingual**; none provides Chinese or multilingual captions, and none has built any multilingual-prompt data.
- **InternVid is the only one touching multilingual data**: its internal collection includes **ASR subtitles in 11 languages** (the paper's Figure 16 shows examples in English, Chinese, Korean, and German), and source-video country coverage spans US, UK, Australia, Japan, Korea, China, Russia, France, etc. However, **these ASR transcripts were neither used to generate captions nor released with the dataset** — the released jsonlines contain no audio, no ASR, and no subtitle field. This is a multilingual resource that was collected but then discarded.
- **Panda-70M used subtitles, but only as input**: English subtitles (including YouTube's auto-generated captions) were fed into the teacher models as textual context (video2dataset config subtitleslangs:['en'], writeautomaticsub:True); the subtitles themselves are not released as a Panda-70M field, only ending up in each clip's JSON metadata.
- Data foundations for accent, speaker identity, and lip-sync: **entirely absent across all seven**.
- A knock-on effect: since captions are all English and generally verbose (Koala 202 words, MiraData 318 words, UltraVideo 824 words), training on this data **requires a long-context text encoder** — MiraData accordingly explicitly abandons CLIP's 77-token encoder in favor of **Flan-T5-XXL (512 tokens)**; LVD-2M, by contrast, suffered for it — its 88.7-word captions were truncated by a frozen CLIP text encoder at 77 tokens, and the authors attribute their unremarkable improvement in I2V text matching directly to this.

## Filtering Pipeline

### Overall Structure of the Filtering Funnel (number of filtering stages, ordering) 

**The differences in the structure of the seven funnels essentially reflect differences in "what is used as the QC gatekeeper,"** which can be grouped into three technological generations:
【Generation 1 · Pure heuristic scorers + thresholds】
- **InternVid (simplest)**: crawl-time constraints (duration 10s-30min, resolution 360P-720P, exclusion of already-existing public datasets) → NSFW binary classifier → PySceneDetect (threshold 27) shot-cutting → removal of static and extremely dynamic clips → multi-scale captioning → post-hoc subset derivation via UMT-SIM and aesthetic scores. **Filtering and captioning disclose essentially no thresholds, giving it the lowest reproducibility.**
- **Panda-70M (splitting as the core, filtering very light)**: the weight of its funnel is not on filtering but on **splitting** — PySceneDetect (threshold 25, min_scene_len=15 frames) → for clips longer than 5 seconds, the first 5 seconds is recursively extracted (the released code changed this to 7 seconds) → keep if ImageBind first/last-frame feature distance ≤1.0 → adjacent clips are stitched if distance ≤0.6 → discard clips <2 seconds or with too little motion (distance ≤0.15) → deduplicate if distance to the preceding clip >0.3 → trim 10% from each end → eight-teacher captioning → UMT retrieval model selects the caption. **No aesthetic filtering, no clarity filtering, no OCR filtering** — the root cause of the collective criticism it later received from OpenVid/Koala/MiraData.
- **OpenVid-1M (proportion-based filtering, four stages)**: source datasets pooled → cascaded shot-cutting → LAION aesthetic score (top **20%** for Panda-50M, top **90%** for the other three sources) → CLIP adjacent-frame cosine similarity, bidirectional removal (too high = near-static, too low = flickering) → UniMatch optical flow, bidirectional removal → DOVER-Technical clarity, top **30%** → LLaVA-v1.6-34B re-captioning. Distinctive in that **it uses percentiles rather than absolute thresholds throughout**, giving good portability but uncontrolled absolute quality.
【Generation 2 · MLLM as the semantic QC gatekeeper】
- **LVD-2M (three-stage, relying entirely on an MLLM as the final backstop for semantics)**: >10-second duration gate → PySceneDetect **ContentDetector (threshold 50, min_scene_len=0, 0.5fps sampling)** removes shot transitions → RAFT optical flow (2fps, 520×960) mean **<20 discarded** → **PLLaVA-7B, taking 8 frames, does a GOOD/BAD binary classification**, with two prompts handling "degree of content change" and "visual diversity + on-screen text" respectively. **No aesthetic scorer, no OCR model** — both are entirely outsourced to the MLLM's semantic judgment, making this the earliest and most thorough "VLM-as-judge" practice among the seven.
- **UltraVideo (four stages, statistical + model, two layers)**: source-level control (5,000 4K/8K videos, manually re-reviewed) → PySceneDetect AdaptiveDetector two passes + DINOv2 first/last-5-frame dissolve detection + routing into short/long by frame count → **statistical filtering** (PaddleOCR text area, black bars, overexposure, grayscaling — four checks, all using the "discard the entire clip if the bad-frame rate >5%" pattern) → **model-based purification** (VTSS<0.01 removed; RAFT optical flow kept within 0.1-100; VideoCLIP-XL-v2 image-text similarity <0.2 removed; **Qwen2.5-VL-72B makes a binary judgment on 16 kinds of low-quality attributes, removed if any hits**) → Qwen2.5-VL-72B ten-dimensional captioning + Qwen3-4B summarization.
【Generation 3 · feeding multiple sub-metrics into a single learned scoring network】
- **Koala-36M (the only one opposing the multi-threshold paradigm)**: same-source data as Panda-70M → Color-Struct SVM transition detection and re-splitting → LLaVA-family structured captioning → **a single VTSS score gate of 2.5**. Its core argument is that **sub-metrics are not orthogonal to one another** (clarity-aesthetic Pearson correlation 0.3774, clarity-motion −0.4028, motion-aesthetic −0.2515), and chaining multiple thresholds accumulates error (Appendix D, Table 8: shifting only the clarity threshold by 10% mis-filters 250K/48M clips, shifting all three thresholds simultaneously by 10% mis-filters 340K), so it instead uses a network that **takes "video pixels + sub-metrics" as joint input and directly regresses a single scalar**.
【MiraData (the most unusual structure: over-split → stitch → filter → caption)】
Collection → PySceneDetect (threshold **26**, deliberately low) over-splitting → **four-model-vote stitching** → four filtering criteria (color, LAION aesthetic score, RAFT motion intensity, Stable Diffusion Safety Checker), all computed at a **uniform 2fps**, producing four increasingly strict tiers of 330K/93K/42K/9K → Panda-70M's short caption as a hint + one round of GPT-4V dialogue producing 5 structured fields.
**Cross-cutting conclusion**: the ranking of filtering strictness is roughly UltraVideo > MiraData (9K tier) > Koala-36M ≈ LVD-2M > OpenVid-1M > InternVid > Panda-70M; while the ranking of "how intelligent the QC gatekeeper is" is UltraVideo (72B VLM) > LVD-2M (7B VLM) > Koala-36M (learned scoring network) > OpenVid/MiraData (shallow scorers) > Panda-70M (almost none).

### Quantitative Funnel Retention Rates (input/output volume at each filtering stage and final retention rate, e.g. Apollo's 27%) ⚠️

**Disclosure levels vary hugely; only three datasets give usable funnel figures**:
- **UltraVideo (most complete disclosure)**: 5,000 source videos → after shot-cutting and frame-count filtering, **62K short + 25K long** → after statistical filtering, **46K short + 19K long** (retention rate ~**74% / 76%**) → after model-based purification, **42K short + 17K long** (retention rate ~**91% / 89%**). **Counting from the post-split pool, the short set's overall retention rate is ~68%.** How much each individual check removes is not reported.
- **Koala-36M (gives two key figures)**: unfiltered pool after re-splitting + captioning is **48M clips** (this figure comes from the denominator of Appendix D, Table 8) → human multi-threshold-filtering baseline **37M** (77.1%) → **VTSS>2.5 final 36M (75.0%)**. That is, the quality-filtering stage's retention rate is **75%**, and VTSS is **slightly stricter** than the human multi-threshold approach (36M vs. 37M) yet retains more high-quality samples. **The yield of the splitting stage, from 103M source videos to 48M clips, is not reported.**
- **LVD-2M (only start and end points)**: **220M clips input → 2M output, overall retention rate ~0.91%** — the lowest among the seven. **Stage-by-stage retention is not reported at all** (the paper's Figure 2 is only a schematic, with no counts). The only intermediate statistic is a diagnostic on InternVid: only 15% of clips exceed 10 seconds, and among these long clips, ~52.5% contain shot transitions.
- **MiraData (has tiers but no thresholds)**: unfiltered pool **788K** → 330K (41.9%) → 93K (11.8%) → 42K (5.3%) → 9K (1.1%). There is also one extremely persuasive standalone figure: **HD-VILA-100M's ~100 million clips fed in, with only 195K ultimately surviving (~0.2%)**, which the authors use to argue that their manually curated YouTube channels are far higher quality than generic crawling. **But the specific thresholds between the four tiers are said by the paper to be in supplementary material, which in fact does not exist** (I checked the arXiv v1 appendix, the NeurIPS camera-ready, and the supplementary ZIP link — none contain the thresholds), a genuine reproducibility gap.
- **OpenVid-1M**: only qualitative description (Panda-50M candidate pool → filtered to Ours-0.4M → expanded by splitting to Ours-0.6M → merged with the remaining three sources to reach ~1M), **absolute counts and overall retention rate at each stage are not disclosed**. [Uncertain]
- **Panda-70M**: 3.8 million source videos → 70.817 million clips, which is an **expansion ratio (~18.7×) rather than a retention rate** — it does almost no quality filtering. The only filtering-related figure is the released-version count of 70,723,513 (about 94K fewer than the paper, attributed to harmful-content filtering, unstated by the authors) and **89.6% of samples have matching_score>0.43**. There is also a desirability distribution added in October 2024 (share of the full dataset): desirable **80.5%**, low expectation score 5.28%, static foreground 6.82%, minimal camera motion 1.20%, picture-in-picture 5.03%, screen recording 1.13% — **meaning, by its own self-assessment, ~19.5% of the samples are still not ideal**.
- **InternVid**: **discloses no funnel figures whatsoever**. [Uncertain]
**A citable rule of thumb**: across datasets, the order of magnitude for "retention rate from a general web-video pool down to a usable training set" is — no quality filtering ~100% (Panda), ordinary quality filtering ~70-75% (Koala, UltraVideo's statistical layer), strict quality + long-shot filtering ~1% (LVD-2M's 0.91%, MiraData's 0.2% on HD-VILA). **The cost of the combined constraint "long shot + high motion + no transitions" is three orders of magnitude of data attrition** — one of the strongest quantitative conclusions of this survey.

### Shot-Splitting Method (PySceneDetect / in-house model / shot-aware splitting) ⚠️

**This is the area of clearest technical divergence among the seven, falling into four routes**:
【Route 1 · PySceneDetect used directly (parameters differ, and the parameter choice itself constitutes the method)】
- **InternVid**: ContentDetector, threshold **27**, no other modification.
- **MiraData**: content-aware threshold **26** — deliberately lowered to **intentionally over-split** ("ensuring that all distinct clips are extracted"), leaving material for later stitching.
- **LVD-2M**: **the most clever parameterization**. Explicitly abandons AdaptiveDetector + rolling average ("hard to detect slow shot transitions with fade-in/fade-out"), switching instead to **ContentDetector, threshold 50, min_scene_len=0 frames, frame sampling rate 0.5fps**. The mechanism: at 0.5fps, adjacent sampled frames are 2 seconds apart, so **any significant change within a 2-second window is judged a transition, which incidentally also filters out fades that typically complete within 2 seconds**. Using the sampling rate rather than detector complexity to handle gradual transitions is a low-cost, high-payoff design.
- **UltraVideo**: AdaptiveDetector, **two passes + rolling average**, to reduce mis-cuts caused by camera motion, followed by **DINOv2 on the first/last 5 frames of each clip** for feature-similarity comparison, catching dissolve transitions that AdaptiveDetector misses.
【Route 2 · PySceneDetect + secondary semantic-feature constraint and stitching (Panda-70M)】
PySceneDetect ContentDetector (threshold 25, min_scene_len=15 frames) → for clips longer than 5 seconds, **recursively extract the first 5 seconds** (to handle unedited continuous-shot footage; the released code changed 5 seconds to 7 seconds "for better splitting results" — **exact reproduction of the paper requires editing lines 199/200 of event_stitching.py**) → take **ImageBind** features at **0.1×n and 0.9×n** of each n-frame clip, **kept only if the first/last-frame distance ≤1.0** → adjacent clips are stitched if **‖f(tail of C¹) − f(head of C²)‖ ≤0.6**. The effect is measured by their self-devised **Max Running LPIPS** metric (the maximum of LPIPS between 1fps keyframes): subtitle-alignment method 0.408/11.8 seconds, plain PySceneDetect **0.247**/4.1 seconds, this method **0.256/7.9 seconds** — **i.e., a consistency cost of only 0.009 buys nearly a 2× increase in clip length**, the paper's most elegant piece of quantitative argument.
【Route 3 · in-house learned transition detection (Koala-36M, unique)】
**Color-Struct SVM (CSS) + temporal 3σ gate**, two stages:
(a) Self-supervised construction of training data — **frame pairs within the same video are negatives (no transition), frame pairs from different videos are positives (has a transition)**; two features: `d_color` = BGR histogram correlation (frame resized to 256×256, 254 bins per channel, range [1,255]), `d_struct` = SSIM computed on a Canny-enhanced luma map (E=max(Gray(I), Canny(Gray(I))), grayscale image resized to 128×128, Canny(100,200), data_range=255); a linear SVM, with **the released code giving the exact fitted coefficients**: `svm_score = 4.61480465×bgr_similarity + 3.75211168×canny_similarity − 5.485968377115124`.
(b) Temporal probability gate — apply a **3-tap sliding average** to the SVM score to get conv_svm; `svm_score<0` is judged a hard cut directly; otherwise, for frame index ≥8 with conv_svm<0.75, estimate μ, σ from the **preceding 8 frames** (sorted, with the top and bottom 20% dropped before computing — a robust statistic), a transition is judged when `conv_svm[i] < μ − 3×max(0.2, σ)` (**σ floor of 0.2** to prevent degeneration). Clips must be longer than 8 frames to be kept, with **4 frames eroded from each end**.
Performance (10K manually annotated clips, roughly half containing transitions): **accuracy 0.7741 / recall 0.9395 / precision 0.7547**, versus PySceneDetect(HSL) 0.4421/0.3096/0.5920, PySceneDetect(HSL+edge) 0.4574/0.4146/0.5854. **Converted to F1: 0.838 vs. 0.407 / 0.485.** In speed, because feature extraction is fixed at a downsampled 256/128, it overtakes on high resolutions: 1080p 12.26ms vs. PySceneDetect-HSL 26.16ms, 4K 41.98ms vs. 102.55ms (6.4× faster than HSL+edge at 4K), but **is actually slower at 256 resolution** (1.42 vs. 0.68ms).
【Route 4 · over-split then stitch via multi-model voting (MiraData, unique)】
Adjacent short clips are re-merged via a "**pairwise, in-group consensus required**" vote among four models: a vision-language-model group, **Qwen-VL-Chat + LLaVA** (judging "is this the same scene"), and an image-feature group, **ImageBind + DINOv2** (judging "are the features similar"). The original rule states: "A connection is made only if **both** vision language models **or both** image feature extraction models agree," i.e. (VLM₁∧VLM₂) ∨ (Feat₁∧Feat₂). Design rationale: VLMs are good at recognizing content-coherent transitions, while feature similarity is good at repairing clips that were incorrectly separated. **This is the only pipeline among the seven that treats "stitching" rather than "splitting" as the core action**, and it is the direct reason MiraData achieves a 72-second average duration.
【Route 5 · details not disclosed】OpenVid-1M only states that it uses a "cascaded shot/cut-point detector," with the specific model and thresholds undisclosed. [Uncertain]

### Quality Filtering (aesthetic scoring, clarity, OCR text filtering, black-bar/watermark/logo detection) ⚠️

**Comparing across four categories side by side — aesthetics / technical clarity quality / OCR text / black bars-watermarks**:
【Aesthetic scoring】
- **Panda-70M: none.** This is its biggest gap.
- **InternVid**: LAION aesthetic predictor, **≥4** yields the InternVid-18M-AES subset (used only for a derived subset, not the main funnel).
- **MiraData**: LAION-Aesthetic predictor, computed at a uniform **2fps**, **threshold undisclosed** (the paper says it is in supplementary material, which does not exist). [Uncertain]
- **OpenVid-1M**: LAION aesthetic score, **top 20% for the Panda-50M source, top 90% for the other three sources** — using percentiles rather than absolute scores.
- **LVD-2M: does not use an aesthetic scorer**, instead relying on PLLaVA-7B's "visual diversity" prompt for semantic judgment (the word "aesthetic" appears in the full text only once, as a VBench metric name).
- **Koala-36M**: aesthetic score is one of VTSS's input sub-metrics (aesthetic_score in the CSV measured in the range **2.28-6.56**), but **no independent threshold is set**.
- **UltraVideo: uses neither a LAION aesthetic score nor DOVER**, directly reusing **Koala-36M's VTSS** (native score linearly rescaled to −0.0575~0.0728), **VTSS<0.01 removed**.
【Clarity / technical quality】
- **OpenVid-1M**: **DOVER technical score, top 30%**.
- **Koala-36M**: clarity_score as a VTSS sub-metric (measured range 0-1 in the CSV); the paper uses DOVER and FastVQA as comparison baselines for the TSA network (TSA PLCC 0.8974 vs. DOVER 0.8554 vs. FastVQA 0.8684).
- **MiraData**: a "**color of the frame**" check — computing average color as well as the color of the brightest/darkest 80% of frames, removing overly bright/dark material. This is the only explicit exposure/color-level filter among the seven (in the same category as UltraVideo's overexposure check).
- **Panda-70M / InternVid / LVD-2M: no dedicated clarity/technical-quality scorer.**
- **UltraVideo: four statistical checks, all using the unified "discard the entire video if the bad-frame rate >5%" pattern** — (a) black bars: mean value of a rectangular region extending inward 3% from each edge **<3** judged a bad frame; (b) overexposure: proportion of pixels with value **>250 or <5** exceeding **12%** judged a bad frame; (c) grayscaling: the whole-image mean of the per-pixel RGB variance **<1.2** judged a bad frame. This is the **only statistical filtering scheme among the seven to give all absolute threshold values**, directly reusable.
【OCR / text filtering】
- **UltraVideo**: **PaddleOCR**, union area of text bounding boxes / total image area **>2%** judged a bad frame, entire video discarded if the bad-frame rate >5%.
- **LVD-2M**: **does not use an OCR model**, instead relying on PLLaVA-7B's second prompt to judge "Text Presence: whether text overlays dominate the frame in a way that detracts from the viewing experience."
- **Panda-70M / InternVid / Koala-36M / MiraData / OpenVid-1M: none has any OCR text filtering** (Koala's paper contains no mention of OCR at all, contrary to common assumption; OpenVid is undisclosed).
【Black bars / watermarks / logos / transition effects】
- **UltraVideo (the only systematic treatment)**: black bars have a dedicated statistical check (see above); watermarks, transition effects, split-screens, screen recordings, picture-in-picture, and other **16 low-quality attributes** are handed to **Qwen2.5-VL-72B for item-by-item binary judgment, with the entire clip removed if any one attribute is hit**. There was also one round of manual review at the source removing watermarked/blurry/shaky videos.
- **Koala-36M**: subtitles, logos, effects, and transitions serve only as **guidance for human annotators' scoring** (the "video naturalness" dimension), passed indirectly and lossily into VTSS via the 200K annotations, **with no independent detector**.
- **The other five: none has black-bar/watermark/logo detection.** Among them, LVD-2M's use of WebVid material (which carries Shutterstock watermarks and has been taken down from that dataset for copyright reasons) is a clear source of watermark contamination risk.

A significant counterintuitive finding: the aesthetic-score + OCR combination widely regarded as "essential" is being replaced in the two most recent datasets (LVD-2M, UltraVideo) — LVD-2M substitutes a 7B VLM across the board, and UltraVideo substitutes a combination of "precise statistical thresholds + 72B VLM attribute judgment," **neither still relying on a LAION aesthetic score**.

### Motion Filtering (optical-flow/motion-score thresholds, static- and shake-removal) ⚠️

**All seven perform motion filtering, and all do so bidirectionally (removing both static and shaky/excessive motion), but the measurement tools fall into three camps**:
【Optical-flow camp】
- **LVD-2M**: **RAFT**, computed on adjacent sampled frames at **2fps** temporally and **520×960** spatially, taking the spatiotemporal average of the flow magnitude, **discarded if <20** (the official 100-sample-set minimum was measured at 20.19, a perfect confirmation of this threshold; sample mean 51.91, max 201.33; dataset-wide mean 47.8). **This is the only dataset among the seven to give a verifiable absolute optical-flow threshold.** The purpose is to remove static scenes and "talking heads in front of a static background." The authors also note optical flow's limitation: **hand-held shaky footage has a high flow score but no meaningful motion** — precisely what motivated adding the additional layer of PLLaVA semantic judgment.
- **MiraData**: **RAFT** frame-to-frame optical flow used as a "motion intensity" filtering criterion, computed at a uniform 2fps, **threshold undisclosed**. [Uncertain] On the evaluation side, it separately proposes **Tracking Strength (average displacement of CoTracker tracking points relative to the first frame)** as a correction to optical-flow-based dynamism — the paper's Figure 5 gives a counterexample: one video pair has optical-flow dynamism of 1.2 vs. 0.7 (wrong ordering), while Tracking Strength gives 4.1 vs. 11.8 (correct ordering), showing that **optical flow can mistake camera shake and back-and-forth local motion for high dynamism**.
- **OpenVid-1M**: **UniMatch** optical flow, removing both the highest and lowest ends of the motion-difference score, keeping "moderate" motion. Threshold undisclosed. [Uncertain]
- **UltraVideo**: **RAFT**, averaged globally after interval sampling, kept within **0.1 ≤ score ≤ 100**.
【Perceptual/feature-distance camp】
- **Panda-70M**: does not use optical flow; uses **ImageBind first/last-frame feature distance** instead — distance **≤0.15** is treated as too little motion and discarded (while distance >1.0 is treated as excessive semantic change and also discarded), i.e., a single feature distance simultaneously serves both the "motion lower bound" and "consistency upper bound" roles. It also uses "distance to the preceding clip >0.3" for diversity deduplication.
【Learned scoring camp】
- **Koala-36M**: motion_score is one of VTSS's three input sub-metrics (measured range **0.01-267** in the CSV), **with no independent threshold** — judged holistically by the VTSS network. Its human annotation guideline for motion is worth citing: "**the moving region should cover more than 30% of the frame, otherwise the score is lowered for insufficient dynamism**," distinguishing and separately penalizing "amateur camera shake" versus rewarding "professional camera movement."
- **InternVid**: only qualitatively describes "filtering out static or extremely dynamic clips (e.g. photo-album browsing)," **with no model and no threshold**. [Uncertain]
- **LVD-2M's human-verification results** can serve as a cross-dataset yardstick for the effectiveness of motion filtering (40 clips per dataset, 3-tier rating of "very dynamic / moderate / not dynamic"): **LVD-2M 30.0%/62.5%/7.5%**, HD-VG 20.0%/37.5%/42.5%, InternVid 15.0%/60.0%/25.0%, Panda-70M 7.5%/67.5%/25.0%, WebVid 7.5%/42.5%/50.0%. **Panda-70M and WebVid each have roughly a quarter to a half of clips judged "not dynamic" by humans**, direct evidence that "large-scale datasets generally lack motion."
【A common gap】none of the seven **writes the motion score into the caption or the training condition as a controllable variable** (in contrast to Open-Sora, which splices the motion score into the caption, and Koala-36M, which injects three scores via AdaLN — note the latter is a design of its **generative model** rather than the dataset's own annotation strategy; still, Koala-36M's CSV does release three structured scores that downstream users can use for conditioning, making it the only one of the seven to provide structured score columns usable for conditional injection).

### Deduplication Method (exact deduplication and embedding-based semantic deduplication recorded separately) ⚠️

**Deduplication across the seven is generally weak, with only two doing substantive work, and neither is embedding-level semantic deduplication**:
- **Panda-70M (the only one with a clip-level dedup mechanism)**: using the mean of the first-stage ImageBind features of each clip as a representation, **keeping only clips whose Euclidean distance to the preceding clip is >0.3** — a dedup mechanism embedded within the splitting pipeline that targets within-clip redundancy. There is also source-level dedup at the release stage via the rule "**at most 3 clips per source video**" (the 10M subset), with the 2M subset taking exactly 3 clips per source video. But **across the full 70M, the average is ~18.7 clips per source video, and the data is not shuffled** (all clips from the same source video land in the same shard, and the repository explicitly instructs users to shuffle themselves), so the redundancy risk is high.
- **UltraVideo**: in its recall path from Koala-36M, a "dedup" step is explicitly mentioned (uniform per-category sampling followed by dedup), but **the specific method is undisclosed**. [Uncertain]
- **InternVid**: **has no dedup pipeline**, only excluding, at crawl time, videos already present in public datasets as of April 2023 (this is cross-dataset overlap avoidance, not internal dedup).
- **Koala-36M: has no deduplication whatsoever.** In the full text, the word "duplicat[e]" appears exactly once, and that is in a reference-list title (a D4 paper). Given its 48M clips are cut from ~100 million HD-VILA YouTube videos, **near-duplicate content is an unaddressed, substantive risk**.
- **MiraData / OpenVid-1M / LVD-2M: none of their papers mentions any deduplication method.**[Uncertain] Of these, LVD-2M's risk deserves the most caution — it simultaneously uses **HD-VG-130M, Panda-70M, and InternVid**, all three of which are sourced from YouTube, so **an unquantified degree of source-level overlap exists among them** (Panda-70M comes from HD-VILA-100M, and HD-VG-130M is likewise crawled from YouTube), and the paper does no cross-source dedup of any kind.
- **Exact dedup (hashing) and semantic dedup (embedding clustering)**: **implemented by none of the seven**. This is the most consistent shared technical gap found across all seven datasets in this survey, and represents a significant generational gap relative to industrial data pipelines.

### VLM/LLM as QC Gatekeeper (multimodal-LLM-based quality scoring and mismatch removal — the 2024-2026 trend of shifting from shallow scorers to large-model semantic judgment)

**This is the axis that most clearly embodies the "2024→2025 technology-generation shift" among the seven, cleanly delineating three generations**:
【Generation 0 · no model judge at all (Panda-70M, 2024.02)】
Filtering is done entirely by PySceneDetect and ImageBind feature distance, with no semantic-level quality judgment of any kind. The closest thing is the **matching_score output by the UMT retrieval model** (>0.43 taken to mean strong image-text association, with 89.6% of the full dataset meeting this bar), but its role is to **pick the best caption out of 8 candidates**, not to score sample quality for removal — **the VLM here functions purely as a captioner (8 teachers), not as a judge**.
【Generation 1 · shallow dedicated scorers + thresholds (InternVid 2023, OpenVid-1M 2024, MiraData 2024)】
LAION aesthetic predictor, DOVER, RAFT/UniMatch optical flow, CLIP adjacent-frame similarity — all lightweight dedicated models outputting a scalar, cut by threshold/percentile. MiraData's only use of a large model is in the **Qwen-VL-Chat + LLaVA voting during the stitching stage** (judging whether adjacent clips are the same scene), which uses a VLM for **structural judgment** rather than quality judgment — a transitional form.
【Generation 2 · MLLM directly making binary semantic verdicts (LVD-2M, 2024.10, earliest)】
**LVD-2M is the earliest and most thorough case in this survey of using an MLLM to replace a scorer**: **PLLaVA-7B, taking 8 uniformly sampled frames, directly outputs a capitalized GOOD/BAD**, "only videos judged good on all defined metrics are retained." Two prompts are worth quoting verbatim — (1) degree of content change: "If the background, setting, and characters are in static states, the video lacks content variation... You must provide a capitalized either 'BAD' or 'GOOD' answer."; (2) visual diversity + text: "A visually diverse video should have rich content that is visually appealing. If the video is only some person talking to the camera with a static background, it is not diverse. And a video with only texts instead of objects is not diverse... Determine if text overlays dominate the video in a way that detracts from the visual experience." **This entirely replaces both the aesthetic scorer and the OCR model.** The motivation is stated clearly: optical flow cannot catch problems like "hand-held shaky video has high optical flow but no meaningful motion." The authors also honestly list a limitation: "current MLLMs are not guaranteed to have video quality assessment capability, and some models struggle to follow the relevant instructions," calling for a benchmark for MLLM-based video quality assessment to be established.
【Generation 2.5 · large-model checklist-style verdicts on attributes (UltraVideo, 2025.06, largest scale)】
**Qwen2.5-VL-72B makes a binary judgment on 16 kinds of low-quality attributes, with the clip removed if any one attribute is hit** (transition effects, watermarks, split-screen, screen recording, picture-in-picture, etc.). It also uses **VideoCLIP-XL-v2** to compute the similarity between the video and a summary caption, **removed if <0.2** — this is the only explicit image-text-consistency removal step among the seven (Panda-70M's matching_score is used only to select the caption, not to remove samples). A 72B-scale judge plus checklist-style structured attribute verdicts is the typical form of 2025.
【Generation 3 · feeding sub-metrics into a learned network that outputs a unified score (Koala-36M, a separate route)】
Koala-36M takes not "a bigger VLM" but "better fusion." Its **Training Suitability Assessment (TSA) network** has three branches: a dynamic branch (**3D Swin Transformer**), a static branch (**ConvNeXt**), and a label branch (feeding traditional sub-metrics like clarity/aesthetics/motion **as additional inputs** rather than as thresholds), with a **Weight Cross-Gating Block (WCGB)** injecting the label branch's features into the dynamic and static branches via learnable fusion weights. **VTSS is the scalar this network directly outputs from "pixels + sub-metrics," not a regression or weighted sum over the sub-scores** — rejecting the weighted-threshold paradigm is exactly the paper's central thesis. Training labels come from **200,000 videos × 8 experts × 1-5 scores**, with two forms of bias correction: personal-preference bias corrected via **per-expert z-score normalization** then restored using the global mean and variance, and annotation-fluctuation bias handled by taking the **mean across 8 people**. Ablation (PLCC/SRCC/KRCC/RMSE): dynamic branch only 0.8684/0.8580/0.7027/0.4644 → +static branch 0.8730 → +label branch 0.8953 → +WCGB **0.8974/0.8868/0.7406/0.4099**; compared to FastVQA 0.8684, DOVER 0.8554. **The choice of a 2.5 VTSS threshold is also simple**: the full-population distribution is bimodal (approximately two Gaussians), and the threshold is simply the valley between the two peaks at 2.5 (independently confirmed by the released CSV's minimum VTSS value being exactly 2.50).
**⚠️ Reproduction warning**: the VTSS checkpoint Koala-36M released is **not the one used to build the data** — test.yml shows its configuration is `DiViDeAddEvaluator + swin_tiny_grpb + fragments-only + divide_head:false`, i.e. the pure fragments branch of FAST-VQA/DOVER, whose performance exactly matches the "dynamic branch only" row of the ablation table (PLCC 0.8684); **the ConvNeXt static branch, the label branch, and WCGB are all entirely missing from the repository**. Anyone reproducing filtering with the open-sourced code will not obtain the paper's VTSS.
**Conclusion**: the datasets from the first half of 2024 (Panda-70M, InternVid, OpenVid-1M, MiraData) remain at "shallow scorer + threshold"; October 2024's LVD-2M and Koala-36M each break through from a different direction ("swap the judge" and "swap the fusion method," respectively); and 2025's UltraVideo pushes the judge scale to 72B and structures it as an attribute checklist — **the evolution of "VLM-as-judge" across these seven datasets forms a very clear timeline**.

### Safety and Compliance Filtering (NSFW, copyright, face/privacy) ⚠️

**Safety and compliance filtering is generally weak across the seven, with only two having a dedicated safety classifier**:
- **InternVid (has a dedicated classifier)**: uses a **binary classifier to identify and exclude unethical/NSFW videos**, and also restricts search terms and channels to SFW scope at the crawling source. This is the earliest and most explicit safety step among the seven.
- **MiraData (has a dedicated classifier)**: uses the **Stable Diffusion Safety Checker**, checking **8 uniformly sampled frames** per video, removing NSFW clips from **all versions, including the 788K unfiltered pool** — meaning the safety filter is applied upfront and cannot be bypassed, a more rigorous approach than the others.
- **Panda-70M (has content and privacy handling but no NSFW classifier)**: prior to release, an internal automated process filters out samples containing harmful/violent language and mentions of drugs or hate speech, and uses **NLTK to replace all person names in captions with "person."** This is the only privacy de-identification measure among the seven. But **there is no visual NSFW detection**.
- **Koala-36M (relayed only indirectly through human annotation, a clear weakness)**: the only place safety is touched is the "video naturalness" dimension of the human annotation guideline — requiring annotators to remove "content that is political, terrorist, violent, bloody, or otherwise uncomfortable." The consequence: **safety is folded into a single scalar VTSS threshold of 2.5, entangled entirely with quality** — an unsafe video with excellent aesthetics and dynamism could well pass. **There is no independent NSFW classifier and no explicit pornographic-content category**, making it impossible to audit or adjust independently. This is the most noteworthy compliance shortcoming among the seven.
- **UltraVideo**: **no NSFW filtering, no face/privacy filtering**. Its 16 low-quality-attribute judgments target image-quality defects, not content safety. Safety implicitly relies on the relatively clean source of "YouTube 4K/8K premium pool + manual re-review."
- **OpenVid-1M**: neither the paper, the repository, nor the HF card **mentions any NSFW or safety filtering**. [Uncertain]
- **LVD-2M**: full-text grep confirms **"NSFW" and "watermark" appear zero times**, **no safety filtering of any kind**, implicitly relying on the respective cleaning of its four upstream datasets.
- **Copyright content detection**: **none of the seven** has this.
- **Output side**: none of the seven has generated-content watermarking or detection tools (they are datasets, but the companion models released alongside them — UltraWan, MVDiT, ViCLIP, etc. — also all lack a safety classifier).
**Overall assessment**: the completeness of safety filtering ranks roughly as MiraData ≈ InternVid > Panda-70M (privacy- and text-focused) > UltraVideo (relying on the source) > Koala-36M (entangled in VTSS) > OpenVid-1M ≈ LVD-2M (none). This stands in sharp contrast to "quality filtering getting ever more refined" — **across these two years, the seven datasets pushed image-quality filtering from nothing all the way to a 72B VLM, while safety filtering essentially stood still or even regressed.**

## Annotation Methods

### Captioning Model Used (in-house VLM / open-source model, model scale) ⚠️

**The choice of captioning model directly determines caption length, spanning a 60× range from 13.2 words to 824.2 words.**
【Panda-70M · multi-teacher + retrieval-based selection, the most architecturally complex yet producing the shortest output】the candidate pool is **31 captioners = 6 base models × weight variants × input-modality combinations** (V=visual/S=subtitle/M=metadata i.e. title+description): Video-LLaMA (pretrain and finetune, 8 variants in total, **using only the visual branch — the audio branch explicitly disabled**, Vicuna-7B), VideoChat (7B, 4 variants), VideoChat Text (4 variants, textualizing the video into tags + dense description + overall description before dialogue; ChatGPT-4 was swapped for LLaMA to control cost), Video-ChatGPT (4 variants), BLIP-2 (opt2.7b/opt6.7b/flant5xl, 3 variants; the image model randomly takes one frame from between 0.3N-0.7N frames), MiniGPT-4 (7B/13B, 8 variants in total). A user study on 1,000 clips performed **greedy set cover**: the single best model alone covers only 30.8%, all 31 together cover 84.7%, and the **8 models chosen greedily** cover 76.8% (Video-ChatGPT was in the pool but not selected). The visual-only prompt is "Please faithfully summarize the video (or image) in one sentence." The **selection step** uses fine-grained video-text retrieval: **UMT large = ViT-L/16 + BERT-large**, VTC+VTM dual loss + **hard negative mining (the 7 unselected captions weighted 1.0, other in-batch negatives weighted 0.01)**, 12 frames at 224×224, AdamW lr 2e-5, batch 32, 10 epochs, 8×A100-80G. Effect: fine-tuned UMT achieves **R@1 of 35.90%** vs. 21.82% for the pretrained UMT, **while human-to-human agreement is only 44.9%** (illustrating how subjective "the best caption" is). **The distilled student model**: the visual branch follows the Video-LLaMA design (8 frames → frozen ViT-G/14 (EVA-CLIP) + Q-Former → temporal fusion → 32×4096), the text branch uses a **text Q-Former** to produce a fixed 32×4096 with the video representation as query, concatenated into 64×4096 fed into **Vicuna-7B-v0**; the gradient from text to visual is blocked, and metadata and subtitles are each independently dropped with probability 0.5; trained on the full Panda-70M for **300,000 steps on 48×A100-80G**. The motivation is practical: the full pipeline runs **8+1=9** model inferences per clip.
【InternVid · multi-scale, using different models for the two scales — the point most often misrepresented】**the coarse scale uses BLIP-2 to describe the middle frame; the fine scale uses the lightweight Tag2Text to describe frame-by-frame at low fps, then a pretrained language model synthesizes these into an overall description.** **The fine scale is not BLIP-2.** The identity of that LLM is not clarified in the paper (the citation lists both T5 and Vicuna without specifying which is actually used); the HF file `InternVid-18M-aes-vc2vicuna.jsonl` suggests a VideoChat2+Vicuna re-captioning pipeline exists, but that is evidence of subsequent re-labeling, not of the original summarizer. **The exact summarization prompt text, Tag2Text's fps, and the specific UMT variant are all undisclosed.**[Uncertain]
【Koala-36M · GPT-4V teacher → LLaVA student distillation】first **GPT-4V** is used on a seed set to produce captions following a structured schema, then a **LLaVA-based** captioner (**not Tarsier**) is fine-tuned to run over the full dataset. The fine-tuning experience is worth reusing: (a) training the vision encoder (not freezing it) improves caption accuracy; (b) a higher-resolution vision encoder helps capture detail; (c) to control token overhead, **2×2 average pooling is applied to the visual tokens' spatial dimension**; (d) **mixed image + video training**, which teaches both static and dynamic understanding while mitigating video-data scarcity.
【MiraData · one round of GPT-4V dialogue producing 5 fields + Panda-70M supplying the short caption】of the 6 fields, **5 are generated by GPT-4V in a single round of dialogue**, and 1 (short_caption) comes directly from **Panda-70M's captioning model**. The input format is distinctive: 8 uniformly sampled frames are stitched into a **single image in a 2×4 grid** (arranged temporally left-to-right, top-to-bottom, separated by white borders), with the Panda-70M short caption injected into the prompt as a **hint**. The repository also contains a `default_prompt_wo_hit` variant that removes the hint entirely, with the authors stating "on a small sample the results are nearly identical" — **this actually undermines the necessity of the Panda-70M step**.
【OpenVid-1M】**LLaVA-v1.6-34B** re-captions all videos, including supplying captions for CelebvHQ, which originally had none. **The exact prompt text is undisclosed.** [Uncertain]
【LVD-2M · layered three-stage process】(1) videos over 30 seconds are first cut into **30-second segments**; (2) each segment has **6 uniformly sampled frames stitched into a single 2×3 grid image** (a method sourced from IG-VLM), fed to **LLaVA-v1.6-34B**, producing background, main character(s), main action, and camera perspective, **with the sampling interval being adaptive (segment length / 6, capped at ≤5 seconds)**; (3) **Claude3-Haiku** is used to refine and merge (two prompts respectively handle "refining a single coarse caption" and "temporally synthesizing the overall segment description"), motivated by the observation that "LLaVA-v1.6-34B tends to produce extraneous speculation and assumptions, leading to redundancy"; (4) the repository also contains a third track, `rewritten_caption`, not mentioned in the paper, generated by **LLaMA-v3.1-70B** to rewrite captions into "a concise, user-prompt-like style." On a 100-sample test, average word counts were: raw 215.4 → refined 81.8 → rewritten 41.3 — **Claude3-Haiku compresses LLaVA's output by about 2.6×**.
【UltraVideo · fully open-source models, largest scale】**Qwen2.5-VL-72B** produces 9 dimensions, then **Qwen3-4B** integrates them into a tenth, the Summarized Description. The authors explicitly contrast this against MiraData (GPT-4V + 2×4 grid) and Koala-36M (GPT-4V distilled into LLaVA), emphasizing their own use of open-source models. **The prompt and the frame-assembly method are both undisclosed** — GitHub issue #3 specifically asks about this and was closed with no maintainer reply. [Uncertain]
【Cross-cutting pattern】in the first half of 2024 (Panda-70M, InternVid), the route was engineering-heavy — "many small models + fusion/selection"; from the second half of 2024 onward (MiraData, Koala-36M, OpenVid, LVD-2M), the field unified around "a single strong VLM (GPT-4V/LLaVA-34B) directly captioning + LLM refinement"; in 2025 (UltraVideo), it shifted further to "a large-parameter open-source VLM (72B) + a small LLM to summarize." **The fusion complexity of teacher models is decreasing while the capability of a single model is increasing.**

### Caption Density and Structuring Level (short/long/dense description, structured fields such as camera movement, style tags) ⚠️

**Caption length and level of structuring is the most immediately visible difference among the seven** (average word counts per UltraVideo Table 1's unified basis): Panda-70M **13.2 words/0 fields**; InternVid **17.6 words/0 fields**; LVD-2M **88.8 words/0 fields (but temporally dense)**; OpenVid-1M **126.5 words/0 fields** (HD subset 104.5 words); Koala-36M **202.1 words/6 fields (merged into one passage)**; MiraData **318.0 words/6 fields (independently addressable)**; UltraVideo **824.2 words/10 dimensions** (long split 850.3 words).
【The four with no structuring】Panda-70M: a single sentence of 13.2 words; the distribution of caption teacher sources is shown in the paper's Figure 11. InternVid: 17.6 words, ~50% in the 10-20 word range, ~1/3 under 10 words, ~11% over 20 words. OpenVid-1M: free-text single captions from LLaVA; the paper only qualitatively illustrates them as "significantly longer than Panda-50M" with figures, **giving no word count** (126.5 words comes from the UltraVideo table's citation). **LVD-2M is a distinctive intermediate form** — its structuring is not expressed as fields but as **temporal density**: each 30-second segment is described separately and then temporally synthesized, so the caption naturally describes "how events unfold over time" rather than enumerating a static frame. Word-count distribution: 30-50 words ~2%, 50-70 words ~20%, **70-90 words ~38%**, 90-110 words ~24.5%, >110 words ~13.5%.
【The two with 6-field structuring — remarkably consistent field design, directly reusable】
- **Koala-36M's six fields**: (1) subject, (2) actions, (3) environment, (4) **visual language** (style/composition/lighting), (5) **camera language** (movement/angle/focal length/shot type), (6) **world knowledge**. The six fields are **generated separately, then fused into a single continuous passage**, ultimately released as a single caption field (caption character count in the CSV measured at 123-3,320).
- **MiraData's six fields (the only one of the seven independently addressable)**: CSV column names directly correspond to `short_caption`/`dense_caption`/`background_caption`/`main_object_caption`/`style_caption`/`camera_caption`. **This is precisely why it can perform "camera alignment / subject alignment / background alignment / style alignment," four separate dimensions of text-video-consistency evaluation, in MiraBench — something none of the others can do.** Word-count composition: **dense caption ~90 words + four structured fields ~214 words = 318 words total** (**often misquoted as "dense caption is 318 words," when in fact it is the sum across all fields**). Field-level means measured on the official 100-clip sample: short 19.0, dense 112.3, main object 84.5, camera 83.8, background 77.2, style 37.3. GPT-4V's prompt has five numbered items that correspond exactly to five fields, **and the few-shot examples directly use the original text of Sora's official demo prompts** (the woman on a neon Tokyo street, the astronaut with a red knit motorcycle helmet), explicitly stating "referencing DALL-E 3, let GPT-4V produce descriptions favorable for training text-to-video models," and specifically constraining "do not describe frame-by-frame, do not use words like 'first frame.'"
【UltraVideo's ten dimensions (highest degree of structuring)】the first 9 are generated by Qwen2.5-VL-72B and the 10th is summarized by Qwen3-4B: (1) Brief Description (2) Detailed Description (3) Background (4) Theme Description (5) Style (6) **Shot Type** (7) **Camera Movement** (8) **Lighting** (9) **Video Atmosphere** (10) Summarized Description. **"Shot type," "lighting," and "atmosphere" are the three dimensions unique to this dataset among the seven**, clearly aimed at film-production-oriented generation needs. Per-dimension average word counts are **only presented as distribution charts in Figure 4(d) and Figure A1, never tabulated**; the HF browser gives character-count ranges as a proxy: Brief 34-526, Detailed 479-2,610, Summarized 586-6,060 characters.
【Two proven lessons from long captions】
1. **The text encoder's capacity must match**: MiraData explicitly switches to **Flan-T5-XXL (512 tokens)** because its 318-word caption cannot fit into CLIP's 77-token limit; LVD-2M, by contrast, suffered for it — its 88.7-word captions were truncated by a frozen CLIP text encoder, and the authors attribute their unremarkable improvement in I2V text matching (16%/70%/14%) **directly to the 77-token cap**.
2. **Mismatch between training captions and user-prompt distribution**: LVD-2M notes that **VBench's prompts average only 7.6 words**, much closer to WebVid's 14.1 words than to its own 88.7 words, so the evaluation itself is **unfavorable** to long-caption datasets. UltraVideo's response is a **random caption sampling strategy** — with 1/3 probability, one of {Brief, Detailed, Summarized} is chosen; if Brief or Detailed is chosen, a further field is **randomly appended** from the remaining 7 structured categories as the final prompt, letting the model adapt to both long and short prompts. **This is the only design among the seven that specifically addresses the distribution mismatch via training-time data augmentation, and is well worth reusing.**

### Joint Audio-Video Caption Structure (whether both visual and auditory tracks are covered, whether split into separate fields, e.g. LTX-2's full soundscape description, Script-a-Video's factorized streams, Foley-Omni's three-field structure)

Not applicable. The captions of all seven datasets **cover only the visual track**, with no description of the auditory track at all, and no audio-visual-split field design exists. Even UltraVideo (10 dimensions) and MiraData (6 independent fields), which have the most fields, have dimensions that are entirely visual (subject/background/style/shot type/camera movement/lighting/atmosphere, etc.), with none touching soundscape, sound effects, speech, or music. Consequently there is no equivalent of LTX-2-style full soundscape description, Script-a-Video-style factorized streams, or a Foley-Omni-style three-field structure. **This is the most fundamental capability gap between this survey's seven datasets and the data systems needed for audio-video joint generation models: existing mainstream video pretraining datasets cannot provide any auditory-side supervisory signal for AV models — AV models' joint audio-video data must be built entirely from scratch.**

### Dialogue Transcription and Speaker Attribute Annotation (ASR transcription + speaker identity/language/accent/emotion)

Not applicable. None of the seven releases ASR transcription, and none has speaker identity/language/accent/emotion annotation. Two related situations that do not constitute dialogue annotation need clarifying: (1) **Panda-70M uses English subtitles (including YouTube's auto-generated captions) but only as textual input to the teacher models** (video2dataset config writesubtitles:True, subtitleslangs:['en'], writeautomaticsub:True); the subtitles end up in each clip's JSON metadata, but are not released as a dataset field and carry no speaker-attribute information; (2) **InternVid's internal collection includes ASR subtitles in 11 languages** (the paper's Figure 16 shows examples in English/Chinese/Korean/German), but these were neither used to generate captions nor released with the dataset — a multilingual resource that was collected but then discarded. Additionally, for privacy reasons Panda-70M **uses NLTK to replace all person names in captions with "person,"** which amounts to proactively erasing speaker-identity information.

### Geometric and Structured Annotation (camera parameters, depth, 3D point tracks, action labels, explicit state)

**Structured annotation across the seven stays entirely at the 2D semantic level; none provides any 3D geometric information**:
【What exists】
- **Camera movement**: six datasets treat camera movement as a descriptive field within the caption (Koala-36M's "camera language" covers movement/angle/focal length/shot type; MiraData has an independent `camera_caption` column; UltraVideo has two independent dimensions, Camera Movement and Shot Type; LVD-2M's caption includes camera perspective; OpenVid/InternVid touch on it sporadically in free text). **But all of these are natural-language descriptions — none provides a structured enumeration of camera-movement labels or camera-trajectory parameters.**
- **Scalar quality scores (the only truly structured part)**: **Koala-36M is the most complete** — its CSV directly releases three columns of scores usable for conditional control: `clarity_score` (0-1), `aesthetic_score` (2.28-6.56), `motion_score` (0.01-267), plus the fused score `video_training_suitability_score` (2.50-4.95). **Panda-70M** releases `matching_score` (the UMT image-text match score), `desirable_filtering` (6-class enumeration), `shot_boundary_detection` (a list of shot intervals output by TransNetV2, length 1 meaning single-shot). **LVD-2M** releases `total score` (average optical-flow score), `scene_cut`, `span`, `video_time`, `dataset_src`. **InternVid** releases a UMT-SIM score and an aesthetic score. For MiraData/OpenVid/UltraVideo, the CSVs are mainly caption fields — quality scores are not released with the data.
- **Video-category tags**: only **LVD-2M** classifies into 8 labels via BART (with precise percentages), and **UltraVideo** has a taxonomy of 7 themes × 108 subcategories (but the mapping is unreleased, and issue #5 asking about it went unanswered).
【What is entirely absent】camera intrinsics/extrinsics, depth maps, 3D point tracks, optical-flow fields as a condition in their own right, explicit object states/action labels, segmentation masks, human pose/keypoints — **all are zero across all seven**. The only thing that comes close is MiraData's evaluation side (MiraBench), which uses **GVGC 3D reconstruction** to compute MAE/RMSE as a "3D consistency" metric, and **CoTracker** to compute Tracking Strength — but that is an **evaluation metric, not a data annotation**, and is not released with the dataset.
**Conclusion**: these seven datasets are purely "video-text pair" resources and cannot directly support camera-controllable generation, depth-controllable generation, or 3D-consistency training — downstream users needing these capabilities must annotate them themselves.

### Synthetic Data Construction (controlled perturbation/editing to construct training pairs, e.g. InstructAV2AV)

**None of the seven uses model-synthesized video data** — training video is 100% authentically collected web video or stock footage. The only "constructed data" that exists is limited to the text and annotation side:
- **Koala-36M (the only self-supervised data construction, and the most worth reusing)**: to train the Color-Struct SVM transition detector, **rule-based construction of positive/negative frame pairs — frame pairs within the same video are negatives (no transition), frame pairs from different video sources are positives (has a transition)**. This treats "splicing together different videos" as a proxy for a transition, at extremely low construction cost and requiring no human annotation at all.
- **LVD-2M (multi-track caption rewriting)**: the same video retains three caption tracks — LLaVA-v1.6-34B's `raw_caption` (215.4 words), Claude3-Haiku's refined `refined_caption` (81.8 words), and LLaMA-v3.1-70B's rewritten `rewritten_caption` (41.3 words). **This is effectively synthesizing long/medium/short prompt distributions for the same video**, directly usable to mitigate training-inference prompt mismatch, making it the only dataset among the seven to provide multi-granularity prompt pairs (and the third track is not even mentioned in the paper — it exists only in the released data).
- **MiraData**: when captioning with GPT-4V, Panda-70M's short caption is injected as a hint — a text-side cascaded generation, not synthetic data.
- **UltraVideo**: Qwen3-4B integrates 9 sub-captions into a 10th, summarized description — a text-side synthesis; separately, an LLM generates search keywords from 108 topics, which are then manually searched — this is a data-recall strategy, not data synthesis.
- **Panda-70M**: teacher captions, after UMT selection, are used to distill the student model — knowledge distillation, not data synthesis.
- **InternVid / OpenVid-1M**: no data-construction step of any kind.
**Common gap**: none of the seven **has any controlled-perturbation/editing-constructed training pairs** (e.g. InstructAV2AV-style before/after editing pairs), nor any synthetic-engine (Unity/UE/Blender) rendered data — MiraData's "3D-engine-rendered scenes" category is **finished renders by others, collected from YouTube**, not from a self-built rendering pipeline.

### Degree of Human Involvement (human annotation, human QC, model pre-screening + human review) ⚠️

**Human involvement ranges from "zero" to "200,000 clips × 8 expert annotators," diverging into three highly distinct use cases**:
【Type A · human annotation as model training data (two datasets)】
- **Koala-36M (200,000 clips × 8 experts, the largest human investment in this survey)**: 200,000 randomly sampled videos, each rated by **8 trained experts on a 1-5 scale** for "suitability as video-generation training data." Three dimensions: **dynamic quality** (moving region should cover **>30%** of the frame; distinguishing amateur shake from professional camera movement), **static quality** (rich subject detail, well-composed, aesthetically pleasing, subject in focus, saturated color), **video naturalness** (preferring natural, unprocessed content, penalizing effects/transitions/subtitles/logos; removing content that is political, terrorist, violent, or bloody). Two forms of bias correction: **personal-preference bias** via per-expert z-score normalization then restored to the global mean and variance; **annotation-fluctuation bias** taken as the mean across the 8 raters. This annotation directly trains the TSA network.
- **Panda-70M (100,000 clips, used to train the caption selector)**: 100,000 randomly sampled clips, with annotators choosing the best of 8 candidate captions. The instruction text is memorable: "Imagine you are talking on the phone with your friend and you need to describe the video to him." An "All Bad" option is provided, and **12,064 clips were discarded because all candidate captions were unacceptable**, with the remainder split into 86,131 training / 1,805 validation. Two additional annotators re-labeled the validation set to measure human agreement (**only 44.9% R@1**).
【Type B · human involvement for calibrating rules/thresholds and source-level gatekeeping】Panda-70M: a 1,000-clip × 31-caption teacher-selection study (asking annotators to "select all good captions," with the standard being "contains no incorrect information and covers the main action or all main objects"), used for the greedy set-cover selection. Koala-36M: **10,000 manually annotated transitions** (roughly half containing transitions) used as the evaluation benchmark for the transition detector. **UltraVideo (the heaviest source-level human gatekeeping)**: (a) **manual searching** for the latest 4K/8K videos starting from LLM-generated keywords; (b) a **second round of manual review** of the 5,000 source videos to remove low-quality/blurry/watermarked/shaky footage; (c) **manual revision and confirmation** of the LLM-induced 7 themes × 108 subcategories. MiraData: the limitations section mentions "manual verification cannot guarantee complete accuracy," implying some manual spot-checking exists, **but the scale is unstated**. [Uncertain]
【Type C · human involvement solely for final evaluation, not fed back into the data】
- **UltraVideo**: **5 annotators each rated 1,000** randomly sampled videos (one batch each for UltraVideo and Koala-36M); a "bad video" is defined as hitting one of 15 defect types (subtitles, abnormal color blocks, green screen, blue screen, transition effects, watermark, sticker, border, split-screen, screen recording, picture-in-picture, still image, blurry video, screen tearing, solid-color background), with annotators explicitly instructed to **ignore resolution** when judging. Result: **UltraVideo's bad-video rate is 2.3% vs. Koala-36M's 41.5%** (inter-annotator agreement not reported). Ten more people participated in a preference comparison between UltraWan and the official Wan.
- **LVD-2M**: three human-evaluation studies — long-shot consistency (40 clips per dataset, blind test), degree of motion (40 clips, 3-tier rating), caption quality (50 clips, A/B); the generation-experiment user study collected **200 valid responses**.
- **Panda-70M**: a 200-video, 5-participant teacher-vs-student preference study.
【Type D · zero human involvement】**InternVid** only involves humans when building the action vocabulary ("5,001 actions extracted by an LLM, then manually verified"); all other stages have no human involvement. **OpenVid-1M** has no record of human annotation, QC, or evaluation of any kind. [Uncertain]
**Two key conclusions**: (1) **none of the seven does human verification or correction of caption content** — even Koala-36M, with its 200,000-clip investment, only uses humans to assign a quality score, with the caption itself entirely VLM-generated and used as-is; UltraVideo is even more explicit: "no human verification or correction of caption content whatsoever." (2) The mainstream use of human effort is shifting from "annotating data" to "calibrating automated rules + verifying results" — Koala-36M's 200,000 × 8 raters is the exception, and the price paid for it is an **unreproducible scorer** (the released version is not the paper's version).

## Audio-Video Alignment

### Audio-Video Synchronization Detection Method (lip sync, event alignment)

Not applicable. All seven datasets lack an audio modality; there is no audio-video synchronization detection step, and no lip-sync or audio-visual event-alignment method of any kind is used.

### Specific Synchronization Metrics and Thresholds (SyncNet/Synchformer/LSE/in-house, threshold values, e.g. MOVA's LSE-D≤9.5 and LSE-C≥4.5, SkyReels-V4's SyncNet |offset|≤3∧conf>1.5)

Not applicable. None of the seven uses SyncNet, Synchformer, LSE-C/LSE-D, AV-align, or any audio-video synchronization metric or threshold.

### Separate Handling of Temporal vs. Semantic Synchronization (time alignment and content-semantic matching treated as two independent filtering conditions)

Not applicable (no audio modality). If drawing a visual-side analogy, the corresponding split would be "temporal consistency" being handled separately from "image-text semantic matching": the **temporal side** is handled by shot-cutting/transition detection (Panda-70M's ImageBind first/last-frame distance ≤1.0, Koala-36M's CSS+3σ, LVD-2M's 0.5fps ContentDetector at threshold 50) and adjacent-frame consistency (OpenVid-1M's bidirectional removal via CLIP adjacent-frame cosine similarity); the **semantic-matching side** is handled by image-text similarity (Panda-70M's UMT matching_score >0.43, though used only to select the caption, not for removal; **UltraVideo's VideoCLIP-XL-v2 similarity <0.2 removal is the only one among the seven to use image-text semantic matching as an independent removal criterion**). But this has nothing to do with audio-video synchronization.

### Audio Quality Filtering (SNR, silence detection and silence-ratio threshold, no-audio-track removal, off-screen-source removal, background-music separation)

Not applicable. None of the seven's pipelines reads the audio track — no SNR computation, no silence detection or silence-ratio threshold, no no-audio-track removal, no off-screen-source removal, no background-music separation. Koala-36M's re-splitting code (cv2.VideoWriter/mp4v) **directly discards the audio track** when re-clipping; Panda-70M (download_audio:True) and UltraVideo (states it preserves native audio) retain the original audio track but perform no quality check of any kind.

### Classification and Separate Handling Strategy for Speech/Sound Effects/Music

Not applicable. None of the seven has a classification or separate-handling strategy for speech/sound effects/music/ambient sound.

## Training Integration

### Multi-Stage Training Curriculum and Data-Curriculum Scheduling (basis for stage division: resolution/duration/quality score/modality; low-res→high-res, image→video, short→long) ⚠️

**The seven are datasets, not training frameworks, so they do not themselves define a training curriculum** — multi-stage scheduling is implemented by downstream users. Still, three curriculum-related data-side design points are worth recording:
1. **MiraData's four nested tiers (330K/93K/42K/9K) essentially amount to a ready-made data curriculum** — layered by increasing strictness of color/aesthetic/motion thresholds, users can directly implement a progressive curriculum of "a large, loose foundation → a small, strict finish," with no need to build layering themselves. This is the most curriculum-friendly design among the seven.
2. **UltraVideo's short/long dual split** (3-10 seconds, 42K clips / >10 seconds, 17K clips) naturally supports a "short→long" duration curriculum.
3. **Panda-70M's 10M/2M subsets and InternVid's FLT/DIV/AES subsets** likewise provide quality tiers, but with coarser granularity (2-3 tiers each).
【The companion models' training curricula, as corroborating evidence】UltraWan is the most complete in terms of curriculum information among the seven's companion models: LoRA fine-tuning of Wan-T2V-1.3B, UltraWan-1K at 1088×1920×81 frames (rank 64), UltraWan-4K at 2160×3840×29 frames (rank 16), LoRA modules = self-attention QKV + output linear layer + FFN's 1st and 3rd linear layers, AdamW betas(0.9,0.999), weight_decay 1e-2, **lr 1e-4, only 1 epoch**, batch 1 per GPU, no tensor parallelism, trained on **H20**, with 1K taking **3.4K GPU-hours** and 4K taking **7.6K GPU-hours**. The authors admit UltraWan-4K is noticeably undertrained due to **training only 1 epoch and being memory-limited to 29 frames**, with more subject artifacts than the 1K version. LVD-2M's experiment is a two-stage approach — "pretrain on short clips first, then fine-tune on LVD-2M for frame-count expansion" (32 frames → 65 frames).
**Common gap**: none of the seven defines a "low-res→high-res" resolution curriculum (since each has a single resolution), and none provides an official data-mixture plan broken down by training stage. [Uncertain]

### Change in Data Mixture Across Training Stages (pretraining / annealing / SFT high-quality subset) ⚠️

**None of the seven gives a "data mixture that changes with training stage" scheme** — this is a fundamental difference between datasets and model technical reports. Three categories of substitute information are available:
1. **The natural internal tiering ratios of each dataset**: MiraData 788K unfiltered → 330K (41.9%) → 93K (11.8%) → 42K (5.3%) → 9K (1.1%); Koala-36M 48M unfiltered → 36M (75%); UltraVideo 62K → 46K → 42K; Panda-70M 70.7M → 10M (high-quality subset) → 2M; InternVid 234M → 18M (AES) → 10M (FLT/DIV). Users can directly treat these tiers as the data sources for the three stages of "pretraining/annealing/SFT."
2. **Mixture ratios among source datasets (LVD-2M gives the clearest picture)**: within its 2M output, **WebVid-family accounts for ~1.2 million (nearly 60%), YouTube-family (Panda-70M + InternVid) ~600K, HD-VG ~300K** — the only cross-source mixture ratio among the seven that can be inferred from the released files. Note the paper's main text does not give this breakdown.
3. **OpenVid-1M's differential filtering intensity by source**: applying the **top 20% aesthetic score for the Panda-50M source, top 90% for the other three sources** — an implicit form of mixture control, applying the strictest filter to the noisiest source, thereby suppressing its share in the final set. This "different filtering intensity per source" approach is practical and worth reusing.
**Annealing and SFT high-quality subsets**: none of the seven defines these explicitly. If one had to pick something to serve as an "SFT curated set," the most reasonable choices would be **MiraData-9K** (1.1% retention rate), **UltraVideo-42K** (measured bad-video rate of only 2.3%), or **InternVid-18M-AES** (aesthetic score ≥4, widely used by the open-source community as a T2V fine-tuning set). [Uncertain]

### Post-Training Data (scale and selection criteria of the SFT curated set, number of preference pairs and annotation method, reward-model training data) ⚠️

**None of the seven has post-training data in the modern sense** — no preference pairs, no reward-model training set, no RLHF/DPO data. This follows from their positioning as "pretraining datasets," but it is also a significant gap relative to industrial data ecosystems. Specifics:
- **Preference data used only for evaluation, not fed back into training**: Panda-70M's 200-video × 5-person teacher-vs-student preference study; UltraVideo's 10-person UltraWan-vs.-official-Wan preference comparison (video aesthetic quality **81.10% vs. 18.90%**, temporal stability 55.70% vs. 44.30%, text consistency 54.50% vs. 45.50%); LVD-2M's 200-valid-response user study. None of this human-preference data **was constructed into training preference pairs**.
- **The only human annotation actually used for training is a "quality score," not a "preference pair"**: Koala-36M's 200,000 clips × 8 experts × 1-5 scores trains a data filter (the TSA network), not a reward model; Panda-70M's 100,000 caption-selection annotations trains a caption selector (UMT), not a reward model. **Both are "using human annotation to train a component of the data pipeline," not "using human annotation to align a generative model"** — a distinction crucial to understanding the positioning of 2024-2025 open-source datasets.
- **Subsets usable as an SFT curated set**: see the stage_data_mixture field (MiraData-9K, UltraVideo, InternVid-18M-AES). Their selection criteria are the threshold combination of each one's strictest funnel tier.
- The companion generative models (ViCLIP, MVDiT, UltraWan, MiraDiT) **all do only pretraining or LoRA fine-tuning — none has undergone preference alignment.**

### Data-Processing Infrastructure and Throughput (NeMo Curator / Data-Juicer / in-house, GPU acceleration ratio, processing scale, cost) ⚠️

**Disclosure of data-processing infrastructure and throughput is extremely sparse across the seven — this is the field with the least information in this survey**:
- **Framework**: **none of the seven uses a mature data-processing framework like NeMo Curator or Data-Juicer** — all use in-house scripts. The only one with engineering-grade packaging is **Panda-70M — it forked and modified `video2dataset`** (the upstream iejMac version could not parse its CSV format), providing a `panda70m.yaml` config, making it the most engineered download-and-splitting pipeline among the seven. Two easy-to-miss default values in its config: `download_size: 360` (**only downloads 360p by default — 720p requires changing the config**), `download_audio: True` (audio track included by default; turning it off can speed things up).
- **Explicitly given compute figures (only three places, and all for model training rather than data processing)**: Panda-70M's UMT selector fine-tuning on **8×A100-80G**, the student captioning model on **48×A100-80G for 300,000 steps**; InternVid's ViCLIP training on **64×A100 for ~3 days** (50 million video-text pairs, using DeepSpeed + FlashAttention, including MAE-style random patch masking for acceleration, followed by 0.5 epochs of unmasked training at lr 4e-6 to bridge the pretraining/deployment gap); UltraVideo's UltraWan at **3.4K GPU-hours for the 1K version / 7.6K GPU-hours for the 4K version (H20)**.
- **The only data-processing-side performance figures (Koala-36M, quite valuable as a reference)**: per-frame-pair CPU time (ms) for transition detection — resolution 256 1.42, 512 2.45, 720p 6.15, 1080p 12.26, 4K 41.98; compared to PySceneDetect(HSL) 0.68/2.63/10.73/26.16/102.55 and PySceneDetect(HSL+edge) 2.50/8.82/30.57/70.11/267.18. **The conclusion is that its method is actually slower at 256 resolution, but 2.4× faster at 1080p and 2.4× faster at 4K (6.4× faster at 4K compared to HSL+edge)**, because feature extraction is fixed at a downsampled 256/128, decoupling it from the source resolution. This is the **only publicly available data-processing throughput material among the seven**.
- **Cost**: **none of the seven** discloses the compute cost of the data-processing stage. Given the need to run optical flow, aesthetics, OCR, and VLM captioning across tens of millions of videos (Panda-70M runs 9 model inferences per clip, UltraVideo uses a 72B VLM to judge 16 attributes per clip), this is very likely a hidden major cost.
- **Storage scale (usable as a capacity-planning reference)**: Panda-70M's full set is ~**36TB** (10M subset ~8.0TB, 2M subset ~1.6TB); OpenVid-1M is ~**12.4TB** (HD subset ~4.5TB); InternVid metadata 40.9GB; Koala-36M metadata 48.9GB; LVD-2M metadata ~5.08GB. [Uncertain]

## Effectiveness Comparison

### Quantitative Impact of Data Strategy Ablations (distinguishing: filtering-strictness ablation / caption-density-and-style ablation / data-mixture ablation, and corresponding evaluation metrics)

**Four of the seven conduct controlled data experiments under the same architecture and compute budget — the most valuable quantitative evidence in this survey**:
【Koala-36M — the most complete ablation design】the same Sora-style 3D full-attention DiT (T5 text encoder + 3D causal VAE) trained from scratch, 2 seconds at 256×256, A100-80G, batch 32, lr 1e-4, all runs trained over 140M samples, VBench evaluation (with VBench captions prompt-expanded to bridge the domain gap). VBench total scores across six comparisons: Panda-70M **0.6493** → Koala-w/o-TSA (48M unfiltered) **0.7140** → Koala-37M-manual (human multi-threshold) **0.7073** → Koala-36M (VTSS) **0.7156** → Koala-w/o-TSA + conditional injection **0.7433** → **Koala-36M + conditional injection 0.7460**. Three separable conclusions: (a) **splitting + captioning improvements alone account for the bulk of the gain** (Panda→Koala-w/o-TSA total score +6.5 points, concentrated mostly in temporal metrics: subject consistency 0.8584→0.9335, background consistency 0.9435→0.9668, temporal flickering 0.9576→0.9857); (b) **VTSS beats human multi-threshold filtering** (0.7156 > 0.7073, and the VTSS-filtered set is even smaller, 36M<37M); (c) **injecting the quality score as a condition is the single largest lever** (+0.0304), especially rescuing dynamism (0.5750→0.9194). Final Koala-36M+condition vs. Panda-70M: **total score +9.67 points, semantic score +28.2 points (0.5915 vs. 0.3093)**, using **only half the data volume of Panda**. Additional resolution/duration experiments' FVD: 256-2s **549.79 vs. 570.87**, 256-4s **354.79 vs. 451.09**, 512-2s **392.26 vs. 579.57**.
【MiraData — caption-density/style ablation, the cleanest among the seven】same model, only the prompt granularity is changed — short caption → dense → structured, dynamism 9.45→17.39→**19.53**, Tracking Strength 27.03→52.53→**68.85**, temporal consistency DTC 24.39→46.13→**60.83**, overall alignment 7.73→14.88→**15.36**. **The conclusion is extremely clear and counterintuitive: longer, more structured captions do not improve image quality** (aesthetic score 4.84→5.14→4.99, imaging quality 63.64→63.43→64.07 essentially flat), **but substantially improve dynamism, temporal consistency, and text alignment (overall alignment nearly doubles from short to structured)**. This is the cleanest piece of "caption-structuring benefit" evidence in the field. There is also a data-source ablation: MiraDiT trained on MiraData vs. WebVid-10M, dynamism 7.12→**15.46**, Tracking 22.36→**49.47** (both roughly doubling), though the authors honestly note the weaknesses — OpenSora is still better on 3D consistency (MAE 75.45 vs. 85.27) and subject alignment (18.12 vs. 14.67).
【LVD-2M — filtering-strictness ablation】the same architecture, a 1.75B MagicVideo-style T2V, pretrained at 32 frames then extended to 65 frames, with the two data groups under identical configurations otherwise (batch 64, 4-step gradient accumulation, 30k iterations, roughly one pass over the 2M). VBench: **LVD-2M wins on 10 of 16 metrics**, key items: dynamism 20.00%→**28.06%**, object class 76.95%→**86.93%**, human action 61.40%→**76.20%**; it loses on background consistency, temporal flickering, aesthetics, imaging quality, multiple objects — i.e., the classic trade-off between **"more dynamic" and "steadier, more aesthetic."** The authors proactively point out that this comparison is unfavorable to them: **VBench prompts average only 7.6 words, close to WebVid's 14.1 words and far from their own 88.7 words**. Generation-side user study (200 responses): diffusion I2V dynamism **60%/34%/6%** (fine-tuned/no preference/pre-fine-tune), LM-style T2V dynamism 52%/24%/24%, text matching 58%/10%/32%.
【OpenVid-1M — combined data + architecture ablation】STDiT at 256×256: WebVid-10M (VQAA 13.40/VQAT 13.34/Blip_bleu 23.45/Clip_temp 99.62), Panda-50M (17.08/9.60/24.06/99.60), **OpenVid-1M (17.78/12.98/24.93/99.75)**; at 1024×1024 under WebVid+4× super-resolution 69.26/65.74, Panda+4× super-resolution 63.25/53.21, **OpenVidHD-0.4M 73.46/68.58** — **showing native high-definition data significantly outperforms low-definition + super-resolution**. The architecture+data ablation: STDiT+Ours-0.4M (11.11/12.46) → MVDiT+Ours-0.4M (22.39/14.15) → **MVDiT+the full OpenVid-1M (24.87/14.57)**.
【UltraVideo — resolution ablation】UltraWan-1K (LoRA) exceeds the official Wan-T2V-1.3B-480p on multiple VBench items (subject consistency 97.27 vs. 96.11, object class 82.29 vs. 66.66); the key contrast is that **Wan-T2V-1.3B collapses severely when pushed directly to 1K inference** (aesthetics 54.82, object class 33.33, scene 0.00), while fine-tuning on UltraVideo restores normal behavior — **proving that high-resolution capability must come from native high-resolution data, and cannot be obtained via inference-time upscaling.** Note that its VBench evaluation uses only **~96 prompts (1/10 of VBench)**, a fairly small sample; the authors also point out that VBench is not well-suited for evaluating high-resolution generation (dynamism and background consistency can contradict human perception, and motion smoothness and dynamism directly OOM under true 4K).
【The two with no data ablation】**Panda-70M** only has end-to-end comparisons of "trained on it vs. trained on something else" (AnimateDiff on Panda-2M achieves UCF101 FVD of **421.9 vs. 499.3 for the official 2.5M-trained version**, with **generated results carrying no watermark**, compared against WebVid-derived models; UMT on Panda-5M achieves zero-shot MSR-VTT retrieval R@1 of **37.2 vs. 30.2**), with no internal-strategy ablation. **InternVid** likewise only has an end-to-end comparison: WebVid-10M alone vs. +InternVid-18M-AES, UCF101 FVD **705.25→616.51**, IS 13.97→**21.04**, FID 98.25→**60.25**, MSR-VTT CLIPSIM 0.2657→**0.2951**; and being **watermark-free** is its primary reason for wide adoption by the open-source community. But it **does not do a coarse-scale vs. fine-scale caption ablation** — a notable omission for a paper whose central selling point is "multi-scale."

### Evidence for Quality vs. Quantity (cases where small, curated data outperforms large, messy data)

**This survey has accumulated a cross-dataset evidence chain for "quality over quantity" across the seven datasets, with strength far exceeding any single model's report**:
【The strongest single data point · Koala-36M】**with the same source and same architecture and same compute as Panda-70M, using only half the data volume (36M vs. 70M), it scores 9.67 points higher on VBench total score and 28.2 points higher on the semantic score.** This is the most tightly controlled comparison — the same batch of HD-VILA-100M raw material, differing **only in splitting method, caption quality, and filtering strategy**, ruling out confounding from data-source differences. **This may be the cleanest "same source, different processing" controlled experiment in the entire field.**
【The most extreme ratio evidence · UltraVideo】**42K clips / 62 hours**, about 2,700× smaller than Koala-36M by hours, yet its human blind evaluation gives a bad-video rate of **2.3% vs. Koala-36M's 41.5%**, and its UltraWan-1K beats the official Wan on video aesthetics **81.10% vs. 18.90%**. This shows that, on certain capability dimensions (high resolution, high image quality), **data volume can yield to data purity by three orders of magnitude.**
【The most extreme funnel evidence · LVD-2M and MiraData】LVD-2M **220M → 2M (retention rate 0.91%)**, MiraData on HD-VILA-100M **100 million clips → 195K (~0.2%)**. Both independently show that: **the combined constraint of "long shot + high motion + no transitions" costs 2-3 orders of magnitude of data attrition**, in exchange for a human-evaluated long-shot purity of 77.5% (vs. Panda-70M's 50.0%, InternVid's 47.5%) and a "very dynamic" share of 30.0% (vs. Panda-70M's 7.5%).
【The most direct counter-evidence · Panda-70M's self-assessment】its desirability annotation added in October 2024 shows that, even by its own standard, **~19.5% of the full dataset remains undesirable** (low expectation score 5.28%, static foreground 6.82%, minimal camera motion 1.20%, picture-in-picture 5.03%, screen recording 1.13%). Combined with LVD-2M's human evaluation showing **50% of clips contain shot transitions and 25% were judged "not dynamic,"** the actually usable proportion of Panda-70M may be less than half — **this explains why VidGen-1M, OpenVid-1M, and Koala-36M — three subsequent works — all cite "refining/redoing Panda-70M" as their founding motivation.**
【Quality-over-quantity on the caption dimension · MiraData】at the same data volume, simply switching the caption from short to structured **nearly doubles text alignment (7.73→15.36), increases dynamism by +107%, increases temporal consistency by +149%, while image quality stays flat** — showing that "caption quality" is an axis of improvement orthogonal to "video quality," and at far lower cost than re-filtering video.
【An important counter-note】**resolution is the one dimension that cannot be obtained through filtering.** UltraVideo's comparison shows that Wan-T2V-1.3B collapses severely when pushed directly to 1K inference (scene score 0.00), and OpenVid's comparison shows native 1080p (VQAA 73.46) significantly outperforms low-definition + 4× super-resolution (69.26/63.25). **That is, "quality" in the sense of image quality, dynamism, and semantics can be obtained through filtering and re-captioning, but pixel-level resolution must come from source-level acquisition** — the one shortcoming among the seven that cleaning cannot compensate for, and the entire reason UltraVideo exists.
【Methodological caveat】except for Koala-36M, most of the above are **cross-dataset comparisons rather than strict "same data, different processing" controlled experiments**, with confounding factors from differing data sources; and each evaluation uses different criteria (VBench subsets, prompt lengths, and model sizes all differ), so **numbers across papers cannot be directly compared side by side.**

### Alignment Between Training-Data Domain Distribution and Evaluation-Benchmark Category System (e.g. VABench's seven major categories) ⚠️

**Alignment between the seven's training-data category systems and evaluation-benchmark categories is generally poor, with only two having a substantive correspondence**:
【Evaluation benchmarks used】the seven overwhelmingly rely on **VBench** (Koala-36M, LVD-2M, and UltraVideo all use VBench as primary), followed by FVD/FID/IS/CLIPSIM (Panda-70M, InternVid, LVD-2M); OpenVid-1M uses DOVER-derived VQAA/VQAT plus Blip_bleu and Clip_temp_score; **MiraData built its own MiraBench**.
【MiraBench — the only benchmark custom-built for its own caption structure, and the only true case of category alignment】17 metrics / 6 major aspects: **temporal motion intensity** (Dynamic Degree via optical flow, **Tracking Strength (CoTracker, newly proposed)**), **temporal consistency** (DINOv2 structural consistency, CLIP semantic consistency, AMT motion smoothness), **3D consistency (newly proposed)** (MAE and RMSE from GVGC reconstruction), **image quality** (LAION aesthetics, MUSIQ imaging quality), **text-video alignment** (five items computed via ViCLIP: **camera alignment/subject alignment/background alignment/style alignment/overall alignment**), **distribution similarity** (FVD/FID/KID). **The key point: the five sub-items of text alignment correspond one-to-one with MiraData's six caption fields (camera_caption→camera alignment, main_object_caption→subject alignment, background_caption→background alignment, style_caption→style alignment) — this is the only design among the seven to achieve a one-to-one mapping between "training-data field system ⇄ evaluation-metric system"**; the other six, because their captions are not independently addressable, simply cannot do per-dimension alignment evaluation. Two design details: Tracking Strength was proposed to correct optical flow's misjudgment of dynamism (the paper's Figure 5 gives a counterexample: optical flow gives 1.2 vs. 0.7, the wrong order; Tracking gives 4.1 vs. 11.8, the correct order); the three temporal-consistency items are **multiplied by Tracking Strength** before being reported (because "the greater the motion, the naturally lower the consistency"), **so MiraBench's consistency numbers are not comparable to VBench's**. One further note: the actual code outputs 19+ values (3D consistency alone outputs 5 sub-metrics, plus KVD, which is computed but not reported in the paper), not entirely matching the paper's stated 17.
【UltraVideo — has the most complete data taxonomy, yet it is unaligned with evaluation】its 7 major themes (video scene/subject/action/temporal event/camera movement/video type/emotion) × 108 subcategories form the most complete data-category system among the seven, and uniform sampling per category was performed accordingly, **but evaluation still uses the generic VBench, and with only ~96 prompts (1/10 of VBench)** — the two systems are completely disconnected. The authors instead level sharp criticism at VBench: background consistency and dynamism can **contradict human perception**, human action and color show **no discriminative power** (multiple models tying at 66.66 and 100.0), and motion smoothness and dynamism **directly OOM** under true 4K — that is, **existing benchmarks cannot evaluate high-resolution generation**, a genuine benchmark gap.
【The remaining five, with no alignment at all】Panda-70M, InternVid, Koala-36M, OpenVid-1M, LVD-2M **none of them has established a data-side domain-category system** (LVD-2M has BART-derived 8 classes, but used only for post-hoc statistics, not for mixture control or evaluation correspondence), so alignment does not even come into question. What can be observed is only an **indirect, low-level correspondence**: VBench's aesthetic quality ↔ LAION aesthetic filtering, imaging quality ↔ DOVER/clarity filtering, dynamic degree and motion smoothness ↔ bidirectional optical-flow/LPIPS motion filtering, temporal flickering ↔ transition and consistency filtering. **That is, the filters were in fact designed around VBench's low-level image-quality and motion dimensions, while VBench's semantic-category dimensions (object class, multiple objects, human action, spatial relationship, scene) have no data-side mixture or coverage guarantee at all** — the same issue seen from another angle in the domain_distribution field's "absence of category governance."
【Relationship to VABench-style audio-video benchmarks】**all seven are irrelevant** (none has audio capability), and none can be used for training-data-side alignment with audio-video joint-generation benchmarks.

## Other Information

### summary_note

**Core positioning**: these seven datasets form the complete spectrum of open-source video-generation data from 2023-2025, and happen to arrange themselves along a clean "scale ↔ quality" evolutionary axis: InternVid(234M) → Panda-70M(70.8M) → Koala-36M(36M) → LVD-2M(2M) → OpenVid-1M(1M) → MiraData(330K) → UltraVideo(42K), where **each drop of one order of magnitude in data volume corresponds to a step up in caption length and filtering strictness** (13.2 words → 202 words → 318 words → 824 words).
**The six specific techniques most worth reusing**: (1) **Koala-36M's Color-Struct SVM transition detection** — the code is open-source and gives the exact fitted coefficients (`4.61480465×bgr_sim + 3.75211168×canny_sim − 5.485968377`) and the 3σ temporal gate, with accuracy 0.7741/recall 0.9395, far exceeding PySceneDetect's 0.4574/0.4146, and faster than PySceneDetect above 1080p; (2) **LVD-2M's low-sample-rate shot-cutting trick** — ContentDetector threshold 50 + **0.5fps sampling**, using the clever idea that "any significant change within 2 seconds is judged a transition" to solve missed fade-in/fade-out detection at zero extra cost; (3) **MiraData's over-split-then-stitch paradigm** — deliberately over-splitting at threshold 26, then stitching back together using **pairwise, in-group-consensus voting** between Qwen-VL-Chat+LLaVA and ImageBind+DINOv2, the only viable path to obtaining 72-second long shots; (4) **UltraVideo's four-part statistical filtering suite** — PaddleOCR text area >2%, black-bar region mean <3, overexposed pixels >12%, RGB variance <1.2, all uniformly applying "discard the entire clip if the bad-frame rate >5%," **the only scheme among the seven to give fully absolute thresholds, directly reusable as-is**; (5) **the highly consistent six-field caption schema shared by MiraData and Koala-36M** (subject/action/environment/style-composition-lighting/camera language/world knowledge); (6) **UltraVideo's random caption sampling** — 1/3 probability of choosing a long or short caption, then randomly appending one further structured category, to counter training-inference prompt distribution mismatch.
**The three strongest quantitative conclusions**: (a) **Koala-36M vs. Panda-70M is the cleanest "same source, different processing" controlled comparison in the entire field — the same HD-VILA-100M material, with half the data volume yet +9.67 points on VBench total score and +28.2 points on the semantic score**; (b) **MiraData demonstrates that caption structuring only improves dynamism/temporal consistency/text alignment (alignment score nearly doubles) while not improving image quality at all** — these two quality-improvement paths are orthogonal; (c) **the cost of "long shot + high motion + no transitions" is 2-3 orders of magnitude of data attrition** (LVD-2M 220M→2M, i.e. 0.91%; MiraData on HD-VILA only 0.2%).
**The shared gaps most deserving caution across all seven**: ① **none supports audio** — all 8 audio-video-alignment fields are entirely not applicable, and none can provide any auditory-side supervision for AV models; ② **none has substantive deduplication** (both exact hashing and embedding-based semantic dedup are zero across the board), and LVD-2M simultaneously uses three co-sourced YouTube corpora without any cross-source dedup; ③ **none has any 3D geometric annotation** (camera parameters, depth, point tracks are all absent); ④ **safety filtering has made almost no progress over two years** — image-quality filtering went from nothing to a 72B VLM, while NSFW detection exists only for InternVid and MiraData, and Koala-36M entangles safety into an unauditable single scalar threshold of 2.5; ⑤ **none performs human verification of caption content**; ⑥ **five datasets do not host the video content itself**, so link rot is irreversible and none has ever published a measured ID-survival rate.
**Six data traps to be aware of when citing**: ① "Panda-2M/Panda-5M" in the Panda-70M paper are **random subsets**, distinct from the quality-filtered 2M/10M versions released in the repository; ② Koala-36M's "36M×13.75s=137.5K hours" **contradicts its Table 1's stated "172K hours" by about 25%**; ③ the "16K hours" in MiraData's Table 1 corresponds to the **788K unfiltered pool**, while the released 330K version is actually approximately 6,600 hours; the "77k long videos" cited in an external survey is a misciting of v0 beta — do not use; ④ UltraVideo's split is **short/long**, there is no "UltraVideo-1K/42K" — 1K/4K refers to UltraWan's output resolution; ⑤ **the VTSS checkpoint Koala-36M released is not the one used to build the data** (the released version is the fragments-only FAST-VQA baseline at PLCC 0.8684, while the paper's version, including the ConvNeXt static branch + WCGB, is 0.8974) — the paper's filtering cannot be reproduced from its open-sourced code; ⑥ **MiraData's filtering thresholds are said by the paper to be in supplementary material that in fact does not exist** (checked: the arXiv appendix, the NeurIPS camera-ready, and the supplementary ZIP link), so the tiering criteria for its four subsets are not reproducible.
